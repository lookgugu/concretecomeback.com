Hard plastic riser pads (`1/8"` to `1/2"` thick) add vertical clearance between your skateboard deck and wheels to prevent sudden wheel bite when riding `55mm` to `60mm+` wheels or carving hard. Soft rubber or polyurethane shock pads (`1/8"` / `3.2mm` thick) sit in the same spot between the truck baseplate and wood, where they dampen high-frequency road vibration and reduce stress cracks around the mounting bolts. For returning older skaters, the choice comes down to whether your setup problem is wheel clearance under a heavier body weight or foot numbness from rough pavement.

Most returning skaters in their 40s and 50s swap out tiny street wheels for [softer, slightly larger wheels](/blog/wheel-hardness-guide-for-adult-skaters/) within their first month back. That upgrade solves rough neighborhood asphalt, and five minutes later it introduces a new question at the shop counter: whether to put plastic risers, rubber shock pads, or nothing at all under the trucks.

## What Is the Difference Between Riser Pads and Shock Pads?

Skateboard riser pads are rigid spacers made of hard injection-molded plastic (`1/8"`, `1/4"`, or `1/2"` thick) designed to lift the deck away from larger wheels so the urethane never grabs the wood during a deep turn. Shock pads are thin cushions (`1/8"` / `3.2mm`) made of soft rubber or medium-durometer polyurethane that compress under the truck baseplate to filter out high-frequency surface chatter and protect the deck plies around the bolt holes.

The two parts look almost identical in a parts bin, which is why riders treat them as interchangeable. Mechanically, they do opposite jobs where the metal baseplate meets the seven-ply maple deck.

| Feature | Hard Riser Pads | Soft Shock Pads |
|---|---|---|
| **Material** | Rigid polypropylene, nylon, or hard acrylic | Soft rubber, high-density foam, or 80A–90A polyurethane |
| **Standard Thicknesses** | `1/8"` (`3.2mm`), `1/4"` (`6.4mm`), `1/2"` (`12.7mm`) | `1/8"` (`3.2mm`), occasionally `1/16"` (`1.6mm`) |
| **Primary Job** | Prevent wheel bite on `56mm`–`60mm+` wheels | Dampen road buzz and prevent bolt-hole pressure cracks |
| **Compression Under Bolts** | Zero; holds exact deck-to-axle height | Compresses ~`0.5mm`–`1.0mm` when locknuts are tightened |
| **Effect on Tail Pop** | Steeper tail angle; slower, higher-leverage snap | Minimal geometry change at `1/8"` thickness |
| **Best Match for Older Skaters** | Bowl and cruiser setups running `56mm`–`60mm` wheels | Rough asphalt, crust, and park setups running `53mm`–`56mm` wheels |

## Do Shock Pads Reduce Joint Impact on Rough Pavement?

A `1/8"` (`3.2mm`) rubber shock pad noticeably reduces high-frequency road vibration (the buzzing chatter from coarse asphalt, brick, and pebble-worn concrete that leaves your feet tingling after twenty minutes), and it buffers the sharp wood-on-metal spike that causes pressure cracks at the mounting bolts. However, three millimeters of rubber cannot compress enough to absorb the vertical ground-reaction force of a heavy drop or stiff-legged landing.

Skate gear marketing frequently blurs the line between **vibration attenuation** and **impact absorption**. When you roll across weathered neighborhood tarmac, your wheels drum against thousands of aggregate peaks per minute. That high-frequency vibration travels straight up the aluminum hanger, through the steel kingpin and baseplate, and into the soles of your feet. Over a thirty-minute session, that constant buzz fatigues the small intrinsic muscles of the foot arch and leaves your ankles feeling wooden. A soft rubber pad (such as Independent `1/8"` shock pads, Dooks Silencers, or Mini Logo rubber risers) breaks the metal-to-wood acoustic bridge and damps that chatter before it reaches your grip tape.

What a `3.2mm` rubber pad cannot do is replace knee flexion, [impact-absorbing cupsole shoes](/blog/cupsole-vs-vulcanized-skate-shoes-older-skaters/), or softer wheel urethane on vertical landings. Once the four mounting bolts are tightened, the rubber pad is already partially pre-compressed between the baseplate and the maple ply. It takes the metallic sting out of landing bolts on flat ground, while your wheels, insoles, and lower-body eccentric control still carry the real orthopedic load.

## When Do You Need Riser Pads for 54mm, 56mm, and 60mm Wheels?

Whether you need riser pads depends on three variables working together: wheel diameter (`54mm` to `60mm+`), truck axle height (low, mid, or standard/high), and rider body weight. Because a 190- to 220-pound older skater compresses truck bushings further into a carve than a 140-pound teenager, heavier riders hit the wheel-bite threshold about `2mm` earlier in wheel diameter unless they also upgrade to firmer bushings.

Most wheel-bite charts online assume a lightweight rider on factory `90A` cushions. If you weigh over 180 pounds and lean hard into a frontside carve at a [local concrete park](/directory/parks), the hanger pivots deeper toward the deck than standard shop charts account for. Replacing soft factory cushions with [weight-matched 94A to 96A truck bushings](/blog/skateboard-truck-bushings-durometer-weight-guide/) controls the lean speed, and the pad thickness below sets the physical clearance floor.

| Wheel Diameter | Rider Under 175 lbs (Mid/High Trucks) | Rider 175–220+ lbs (Mid/High Trucks) | Low Trucks (Any Weight) | Recommended Hardware Bolts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **52mm – 53mm** | No pad needed (or `1/8"` shock pad for vibration) | No pad needed (or `1/8"` shock pad for vibration) | No pad needed | `7/8"` or `1"` (no pad) · `1"` (`1/8"` pad) |
| **54mm – 55mm** | No pad needed (or `1/8"` shock pad for rough ground) | `1/8"` shock pad or riser (unless running `94A+` bushings) | `1/8"` hard riser | `1"` or `1 1/8"` |
| **56mm – 57mm** | `1/8"` shock pad or hard riser | `1/8"` hard riser (with `94A+` bushings) or `1/4"` riser | `1/4"` hard riser | `1"`–`1 1/8"` (`1/8"` pad) · `1 1/4"` (`1/4"` pad) |
| **58mm – 60mm+** | `1/4"` hard riser (or deck with milled wheel wells) | `1/4"` hard riser + wheel wells (or `1/2"` for `62mm+`) | Not recommended | `1 1/4"` (`1/4"` pad) · `1 1/2"` (`1/2"` pad) |

Every millimeter increase in wheel diameter only moves the top edge of the wheel `0.5mm` (the radius increase) closer to the deck. Moving from a `52mm` street wheel to a `56mm` park or hybrid wheel shrinks your clearance by just `2mm` (`5/64"`). A single `1/8"` (`3.2mm`) pad more than restores that lost gap while adding extra room for deeper transition carves.

## How Extra Deck Height Changes Pushing Fatigue and Tail Timing

Adding a `1/8"` (`3.2mm`) pad is barely noticeable underfoot, whereas stepping up to a `1/4"` (`6.4mm`) or `1/2"` (`12.7mm`) riser raises your standing platform enough to change two mechanics older skaters feel immediately: front-leg quad fatigue while pushing and the timing of your tail snap.

Every time you push down the street or across a park flat bottom, your front leg performs a partial single-leg squat to lower your pushing foot to the pavement. Lifting the board a quarter-inch to a half-inch with tall risers and `60mm` wheels forces your front knee and ankle through a deeper range of motion on every push. Over a forty-minute cruiser session, that extra quarter-inch of drop adds up in your front quadriceps and patellar tendon.

Height also changes tail geometry. When your axles sit further from the ground, the tail has to travel through a steeper arc before the wood strikes the concrete. For flatground ollies or lifting the nose over a curb, a `1/4"` riser delays the pop by a fraction of a second and asks for a sharper downward snap from your back ankle. If you skate `56mm` wheels and want to keep your deck low and predictable for flatground and mellow mini ramps, pairing a `1/8"` pad with properly rated bushings and high-geometry trucks (such as Independent Stage 11 or Ace AF1) beats stacking a thick `1/4"` block under low trucks.

## What Length Hardware Bolts Fit 1/8-Inch and 1/4-Inch Pads?

Standard skateboard mounting hardware is sold by total bolt length in inches, measured from the underside of a countersunk Phillips or Allen head to the tip of the threads. A bare deck mounted directly to a truck baseplate takes `7/8"` or `1"` bolts; every `1/8"` of pad thickness requires stepping up accordingly so the bolt threads fully engage the nylon lock ring inside the nut without sticking out half an inch underneath.

| Pad Setup | Minimum Bolt Length | Flush / Easy-Start Bolt Length | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No pads (bare deck)** | `7/8"` | `1"` | `7/8"` sits completely flush; `1"` leaves 1–2 threads showing |
| **1/8" shock pad or riser** | `1"` | `1 1/8"` | `1"` works on most decks; `1 1/8"` is easier to start on new rubber pads |
| **1/4" hard riser** | `1 1/4"` | `1 1/4"` | `1"` bolts will not reach the nylon lock insert |
| **1/2" hard riser** | `1 1/2"` | `1 1/2"` | Common only on dedicated cruiser or old-school pool setups |

If you walk into a [welcoming local skate shop](/directory/shops) to buy wheels and pads together, ask for `1"` or `1 1/8"` hardware whenever you add `1/8"` shock pads. Starting a `1"` nut onto a brand-new rubber shock pad takes firm thumb pressure on the bolt head until the rubber compresses enough for the nylon ring to catch the threads. If less than one full thread exits the bottom of the nylon insert, swap to the next bolt length up so a hard carve never backs a nut off mid-session.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you stack a rubber shock pad on top of a hard plastic riser?
Avoid stacking two separate pads under the same truck baseplate. Two sliding interfaces allow the mounting bolts to flex and shear under side-load during turns or landings. Instead, buy a single `1/4"` medium-durometer polyurethane riser or pair a single `1/8"` rubber shock pad with higher-profile trucks and weight-matched bushings.

### Does rubbing wax on the deck stop wheel bite without risers?
Rubbing skate wax or bar soap on the wood directly above each wheel reduces friction during a light rub at the end of a turn, buying a fraction of a second to shift your weight back to center. Wax will not prevent a lockup if a `56mm` or `60mm` wheel presses firmly into the ply under a 190-pound carve.

### How tight should you bolt down rubber shock pads?
Tighten the nylon locknuts until the bolt heads sit flush with the grip tape on top of the deck and the rubber pad barely bulges around the edges of the metal baseplate. Cranking the nuts until the `1/8"` rubber is crushed flat squeezes out its elastic give and turns the shock pad into a thin, rigid spacer.
