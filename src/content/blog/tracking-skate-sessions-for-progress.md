A patient in her fifties told me last year that she'd been skating "for months and going nowhere." When I asked what a typical week looked like, she genuinely couldn't tell me — not because nothing was happening, but because she had no record of it. Once we started writing down what she was actually doing, the picture that emerged was a lot more encouraging than "going nowhere," and it also showed exactly why one of her knees kept flaring up every other week.

That's the case for tracking your sessions, and it has nothing to do with turning skating into a spreadsheet exercise. It's about having actual data instead of a feeling, because feelings about your own progress and your own overtraining are both notoriously unreliable — especially when you're relearning a physical skill later in life, where progress is often too slow to notice day to day and too easy to blame on age rather than a fixable pattern.

## Why memory alone gets this wrong

Two things happen without a record. First, you underestimate progress, because you're comparing today to some idealized memory of skating well, not to where you actually were a month ago — and a month of small gains rarely feels like anything from the inside. Second, you miss the buildup to overuse injuries, because soreness that's mild and forgettable on any single day can be part of a pattern that's genuinely too much, and a pattern is invisible unless you're writing individual data points down somewhere.

A log fixes both problems with almost no effort, because it turns "I feel like I'm not improving" and "I feel a little off today" into something you can actually look back at and compare.

## What to actually log

Keep it short enough that you'll do it every time. Four things, thirty seconds, right after you finish:

**Duration and rough intensity.** How long you skated and whether it was an easy cruising session or a harder one pushing a specific skill. This alone lets you spot whether a bad week followed an unusually hard one — which is useful information, not a coincidence to ignore.

**What you worked on.** One line: "kick turns," "pump track laps," "just cruising." Over a few weeks this becomes a rough map of where your actual practice time has gone, which is often surprisingly different from where you thought it was going.

**Soreness or pain, rated simply.** A 0–2 scale covers ordinary post-session soreness: 0 is nothing, 1 is normal muscle fatigue, 2 is a specific joint or spot that's noticeably sore. The pattern matters more than any single entry here — a joint that keeps showing up at 2 across several sessions is telling you something a single sore day never could.

New pain that makes you avoid loading a joint or limb isn't a number to log and watch trend — it's a different category entirely, and it gets a different response. Don't wait for a pattern to confirm it. Stop skating on it that day and get it assessed, the same [red-zone rule that applies to any new or unstable joint pain](/blog/skating-with-arthritis-chronic-joint-pain/) — same-day care if you can't bear weight after a fall. Write it down anyway, so you have a record for whoever assesses it, but the log is documentation at that point, not a wait-and-see tool.

**One observation.** Something you noticed — "carving felt smoother today," "still catching my back foot on kick turns," "windy, harder to balance." This is the note that turns into evidence of progress later, because you'll forget the small wins otherwise.

## Reading the pattern, not the day

The value isn't in any single entry — it's in what shows up after three or four weeks, within the ordinary 0–2 range. A soreness score that's crept from mostly 0s and 1s to mostly 2s without you skating any harder is a signal to back off before it becomes an actual injury, not after. A skill note that's shifted from "still can't" to "getting closer" to "did it twice" is real, trackable evidence of progress on exactly the days it doesn't feel like anything is changing. None of that pattern-reading applies to load-avoiding pain — that gets addressed the day it shows up, not tracked for a trend.

This is also how you catch overtraining before it costs you time off the board entirely. Cumulative joint strain in older skaters rarely announces itself with one dramatic session — it builds quietly across several, the same way the [hip and joint issues I see most often in clinic](/blog/hip-health-for-skaters-over-45/) tend to. A log that shows rising soreness scores across a week gives you a chance to add a rest day or shorten a session before your body forces the issue for you.

## Low-tech is genuinely fine

You don't need a wearable or an app for any of this, though plenty of older skaters enjoy using one and there's nothing wrong with that if it keeps you consistent. A notes app entry, a line in a physical notebook by the door, or a recurring note in your calendar all work exactly as well as a dedicated fitness tracker, because the value comes from the act of recording something consistently, not from the sophistication of the tool. Pick whichever one you'll actually keep up for a month, since a system you abandon after a week tells you nothing.

## Give it a real trial before judging it

A single week of logging won't show you much — the patterns that matter, both the progress and the early warning signs, only become visible across three or four weeks of consistent entries. Pair it with [a structured plan like our 8-week return program](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/) if you want a shape to measure against, or just start logging whatever you're already doing. Either way, thirty seconds after each session is a small enough cost that there's no real reason not to find out what your actual pattern looks like, instead of guessing at it from memory.
