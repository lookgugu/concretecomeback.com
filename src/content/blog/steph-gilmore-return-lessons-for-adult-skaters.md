Steph Gilmore is returning to the World Surf League Championship Tour in 2026 after two years away from full-time competition. She's 38. She last won a world title — her seventh — in 2022, and she's one of two surfers (alongside Carissa Moore) accepting a season wildcard to come back for a full year on tour rather than a one-off event appearance.

I don't surf and this isn't a surfing site, but a 38-year-old with seven world titles choosing to step back into full-time competition against surfers half her age is a comeback story in exactly the shape this site exists to talk about. It's worth looking at closely, because the parts of it that matter have nothing to do with surfing technique.

## She stepped away on her own terms, not because she had to

Gilmore didn't retire. She took a deliberate break from the full grind of the tour, citing nagging injuries as part of the reason, and used the time to let her body recover properly instead of managing through pain on a competition schedule that doesn't pause for anyone.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should. A lot of adults who've taken years off skating tell a story where the break was something that happened to them — work, kids, an injury, life getting in the way. Fewer people frame a long break as something they chose, even partially, because choosing it on purpose means owning the return on purpose too. If your years away included a stretch where you deliberately let something heal instead of forcing it, that wasn't lost time. It was the only way the comeback you're making now was going to be possible at all.

## The field got younger. She came back anyway.

Two years is a long time in a sport that recruits teenagers. The tour Gilmore is returning to has a new generation of surfers who've spent that entire window building the specific fitness, repetition, and competitive experience she's stepped away from. She is, by a wide margin, not going to be the most physically explosive surfer in most of her heats.

She's returning anyway, specifically to find out how she stacks up against surfers who are younger than her now than they were when she last competed. That's a useful thing to notice if you've been putting off a return to the skatepark because the crowd there now is twenty years younger than you remember it being. The field moving on without you isn't a reason the comeback doesn't count. It's just what a comeback after a real gap actually looks like, for anyone, at any level.

## Chasing one more title isn't the same as chasing your 2008

Coverage of Gilmore's return keeps circling back to the idea that she could chase a ninth world title — a number that would put her alone at the top of the sport's history. It's a legitimate goal, but notice what it isn't: it isn't a plan to ski back to exactly who she was in her twenties. It's a new goal, built for where she is now, that happens to use the same sport and the same body of experience she already has.

Most adults coming back to skating get this backwards. They set the goal as "get back to where I was" — the trick list from twenty years ago, the speed, the fearlessness — and then feel like they're failing when the comparison doesn't hold up. A better-shaped goal looks like Gilmore's: something new, built for the skater you are right now, that uses everything you already know instead of measuring you against a version of yourself that doesn't exist anymore.

## What actually transfers to the skatepark

You're not training for a world tour, and the stakes of a Tuesday-evening session aren't the stakes of a championship heat. But three things from Gilmore's comeback transfer directly:

- **A deliberate break isn't a failure to explain.** If your time away included real rest or real recovery, that's not a gap in your story. It's the reason you're able to come back at all.
- **A younger crowd doesn't disqualify your comeback.** It's simply what returning after a real amount of time away looks like, in any sport, at any level.
- **Set the goal for who you are now, not who you were.** A new personal best built on your current body and your current life beats chasing a ghost of your younger self every time — it's achievable, and it doesn't require pretending the years in between didn't happen.

Gilmore's season hasn't happened yet as I write this, and it might not go the way anyone hopes. That's not really the point. The decision to show up and find out, after two years and at 38, against a field that's gotten younger without her — that part has already happened, and it's the part worth borrowing before you've even seen a single heat.
