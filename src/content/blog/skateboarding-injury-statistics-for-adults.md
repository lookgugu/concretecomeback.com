Patients ask me some version of the same question when they find out I skate: isn't that dangerous at your age? It's a fair question, and it deserves a real answer instead of either "it's totally safe" or "yes, obviously." Here's what the injury data actually shows, and what it doesn't.

## The headline number, and what it can't actually tell you

The best long-run picture of US emergency-room visits comes from a 2019 analysis in *Clinical Pediatrics* by Peter Tuckel and colleagues, which tracked falls from skateboards in national ER data since 2000. Its headline finding runs the other way from what most people expect: visits and admissions for children and teenagers have declined noticeably since 2000, and the profile of who gets hurt has shifted — more urban, more often on a street or road. Adults, meanwhile, are now a large enough group to get their own studies: a 2024 analysis in the *American Journal of Emergency Medicine* of adult skateboard injuries in the same national ER dataset (2018–2022) found an average patient age of about 28, with a spread of roughly ten years either side of that.

Here's the honest caveat: these compilations count raw ER visits, not injuries per skater. They don't track how many adults are actually skating, so they can't tell you whether the injury *rate* per adult skater has gone up, stayed flat, or gone down. What we do know independently — participation surveys and the sheer volume of adult-focused gear, coaching, and content that didn't exist a decade ago — is that a lot more adults are skating now than were a decade ago. A rising count of injured adults is consistent with more adults skating at a similar rate of injury each; it doesn't by itself prove that, and it doesn't rule out the less comfortable possibility that something about how adults are skating has gotten riskier. Nobody has the denominator to say which explanation carries more of the number, and it's worth being suspicious of anyone — this post included — who states it as settled.

What that means practically: don't read a rising adult injury count as an all-clear that skating hasn't gotten more dangerous, and don't read it as proof that it has. Treat it as a real, unresolved question, and let the specific, well-supported points below — which body parts, which gear helps — do the actual work of informing how you skate.

## What actually gets hurt, and how often

The adult-specific numbers are the ones worth knowing. In that 2024 *American Journal of Emergency Medicine* analysis of adult skateboarders seen in US emergency rooms, roughly 62% of injuries were to the upper extremity, and the single most common fracture was the wrist (about 20% of all fractures), followed by the ankle (about 12%). Head and face injuries are a smaller share of ER visits, but they dominate the serious end: a *Journal of Trauma* analysis of 2,270 skateboard-related admissions to US trauma centres found a traumatic brain injury in 45% of patients older than 16, and that age group carried the highest mortality in the study, at 2.6% of admissions.

That split is the whole logic behind the standard skate gear recommendation. Wrists get hurt more often because the instinctive hand-catch on a fall is close to universal and very hard to train out, especially in adults relearning to fall. Heads get hurt less often but worse, because a head impact doesn't scale down the way a wrist impact does.

## Where the protective gear actually earns its place

The pattern above is why [wrist guards and a certified skate helmet](/blog/gear-for-aging-joints/) aren't equally optional. The helmet evidence is skateboard-specific, which is rarer than you'd think: that same *Journal of Trauma* trauma-centre analysis compared helmeted and unhelmeted skateboarders directly and found helmet use associated with well under half the odds of a traumatic brain injury (an odds ratio of 0.39). Skating in a designated skatepark rather than on the street was independently protective against head injury in the same data. Be precise about what that does and doesn't show: it's an observational comparison among people already hurt badly enough to be admitted, not a controlled trial, and it says nothing about how many unhelmeted riders skate for years without incident. It lines up with the basic physics — a helmet is built to reduce head trauma, and head trauma is what kills — and with the broader wheeled-sport helmet literature, which points the same way. Treat any specific "X% reduction" figure you see quoted for skateboarding with real skepticism unless it points to a study like that one, that actually compared helmeted and unhelmeted riders.

One more thing the fatality data tells you that most gear articles skip: a *Journal of Transport & Health* analysis by Kevin Fang and Susan Handy counted roughly 150 skateboarding deaths in the US over 2011–2015, and found that essentially all of them happened on roads and other transportation facilities, with about three quarters involving a collision with a motor vehicle. For a returning skater whose sessions happen in a park, the fatal-risk picture is overwhelmingly a traffic story, not a bowl story — which is one more argument for the park, and for treating any street-skating commute as a road-safety problem first.

Wrist guards with a rigid splint have real evidence behind them, but be clear about where it comes from: the strongest study is of in-line skaters, not skateboarders — a 1996 *New England Journal of Medicine* case-control study that found skaters without guards had roughly ten times the odds of a wrist injury. [The wrist guard study every returning skater should know about](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/) goes through that paper and its limits properly. Given how routinely the instinctive hand-catch happens in a fall, guards are addressing the injury mechanism that's actually most common, not a hypothetical one — and [learning to fall without catching yourself](/blog/how-to-fall-without-breaking-your-wrist/) is still the first line, guards or not.

## What the numbers can't tell you

Aggregated injury statistics are useful for spotting patterns — which body parts, which rough proportions, which direction the counts are trending — but they're a blunt instrument for anything more specific than that. Most of them don't separate out skill level, terrain choice, or whether gear was worn, and most of the widely-cited compilations are secondary analyses of broader ER and trauma datasets rather than a study designed around skateboarding specifically. Treat the percentages here as a reasonable general picture, not a precise clinical finding.

What they do support, consistently and across sources, is the same practical conclusion: most of the injury risk in skateboarding concentrates in a couple of predictable categories, both of which respond well to specific, unglamorous protective gear. That's a genuinely useful thing to know, even if the exact percentage varies a few points depending on which dataset you're reading.

## The actual takeaway

Skating is not risk-free, and nobody serious about the sport claims it is. But the injury data doesn't support treating it as uniquely dangerous for an adult body, either. It supports a specific, narrow conclusion: wear a real helmet, wear wrist guards, and you've addressed the two categories that show up most often and matter most when they go badly. That's not the same as saying everything else is minor — ankle, knee, shoulder, and spinal injuries are real categories in the same data, and no piece of gear covered here prevents most of them. Skating within your current level, on terrain that matches it, and building the strength and balance work the rest of this site covers does more for that remaining risk than any single purchase can.
