Emergency room and trauma registry studies show a clear, repeatable pattern in skateboarding injuries: roughly 62% of emergency department visits among skaters aged 18 and older involve the upper extremity, with the wrist accounting for about 20% of all fractures and the ankle accounting for about 12%. Severe and fatal injuries follow a different distribution, concentrating in unhelmeted head impacts and collisions with motor vehicles on public roads rather than sessions inside designated skateparks.

Understanding what those numbers measure, and what they leave out, helps older skaters make practical decisions about pads, helmets, and where to skate instead of relying on guesswork.

## The headline number, and what it cannot tell you

The broadest long-run picture of US emergency-room visits comes from a 2019 analysis in *Clinical Pediatrics* by Peter Tuckel and colleagues, which tracked falls from skateboards in national ER data since 2000. Its headline finding runs the other way from what most people expect: visits and admissions for children and teenagers have declined noticeably since 2000, while the profile of who gets hurt has shifted toward urban streets and roadways. Skaters aged 18 and over now form a large enough group to get dedicated studies: a 2024 analysis in the *American Journal of Emergency Medicine* examining skateboard injuries in the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) from 2018 to 2022 found an average patient age of about 28, with a standard deviation of roughly ten years either side of that.

An important methodological caveat applies to all of these compilations: they count raw ER visits rather than injuries per active skater. Because hospital databases do not track how many older skaters are riding in a given year, they cannot tell you whether the injury *rate* per skater has gone up, stayed flat, or gone down. Independent participation surveys and the growth of older-skater groups, gear, and coaching show that far more people in their 30s, 40s, 50s, and 60s are skating today than a decade ago. A rising count of injured older riders is consistent with higher participation at a similar per-skater risk, though it does not by itself prove that, nor does it rule out the possibility that some riding environments carry higher risk. Nobody has the denominator to say which explanation carries more of the number, so anyone who states it as settled is overreading the data.

Practically, do not read a rising raw injury count as proof that skateboarding has become uniquely hazardous, and do not read it as a blanket all-clear either. Let the specific injury distributions below guide how and where you skate.

## What gets hurt most often

In the 2024 *American Journal of Emergency Medicine* analysis of skateboarders aged 18 and older seen in US emergency rooms, roughly 62% of injuries involved the upper extremity. The single most common fracture site was the wrist (about 20% of all fractures), followed by the ankle (about 12%). Head and facial injuries represented a smaller share of routine ER visits, but they dominated high-severity trauma admissions: a *Journal of Trauma* analysis of 2,270 skateboard-related admissions to US trauma centers found a traumatic brain injury in 45% of patients older than 16, and that age group carried the highest inpatient mortality in the study at 2.6% of trauma admissions.

That split explains the standard protective gear hierarchy. Wrists get hurt most frequently because the instinctive outstretched-hand catch during a sudden fall is nearly universal and takes deliberate practice to override, especially for older returners. Head impacts happen less often than wrist sprains or fractures, but a head strike against concrete does not scale down the way a limb injury does.

## Where protective gear and terrain choice change the odds

The trauma data explains why [wrist guards and a dual-certified skate helmet](/blog/gear-for-aging-joints/) sit at the top of the gear list. Skateboard-specific helmet data comes from that same *Journal of Trauma* trauma-center analysis, which compared helmeted and unhelmeted skateboarders directly and found helmet use associated with well under half the odds of a traumatic brain injury (an odds ratio of 0.39). Skating inside a designated skatepark instead of on public streets was also independently protective against head injury in the same dataset.

Be precise about what that study shows and its limits: it is an observational comparison among riders already injured badly enough to be admitted to a trauma center, not a randomized trial, and it does not measure how many unhelmeted skaters ride without incident. Even with those observational limits, the finding matches basic impact biomechanics and the wider wheeled-sport helmet literature. Treat any unattributed "X% reduction" marketing claim with skepticism unless it cites a direct comparative study.

Fatality records add a second piece of context that gear guides often overlook. A *Journal of Transport & Health* study by Kevin Fang and Susan Handy examined roughly 150 skateboarding fatalities in the US between 2011 and 2015 and found that nearly all of them occurred on roads and transportation corridors, with roughly three-quarters involving a collision with a motor vehicle. For an older skater riding in an enclosed skatepark, fatal risk is overwhelmingly a road-traffic story rather than a mini-ramp or bowl story. That makes a strong case for skating in dedicated parks and treating any [street commute on a skateboard](/blog/skateboard-as-daily-transport-after-40/) as a traffic-safety environment first.

Wrist guards with rigid palmar and dorsal splints also have controlled evidence behind them, though the landmark trial studied inline skaters rather than skateboarders: a 1996 *New England Journal of Medicine* case-control study found that inline skaters who did not wear wrist guards had roughly ten times the odds of a wrist injury compared with those who wore them. [Our breakdown of the wrist guard evidence for older skaters](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/) covers that paper and its design limits in detail. Because the forward or backward FOOSH (fall onto an outstretched hand) mechanism is identical when a board slips out, rigid splints address the exact fracture pattern that leads the ER tables, alongside [practicing safe falling mechanics](/blog/how-to-fall-without-breaking-your-wrist/).

## What the numbers cannot tell you

Aggregated hospital statistics are useful for identifying which joints take the brunt of falls and where severe trauma happens, but they remain a blunt instrument for individual risk. Most national datasets do not separate flatground cruising from stair sets, do not record whether the skater warmed up, and do not track whether protective gear was worn properly at the moment of impact. Treat the percentages here as a broad epidemiological map rather than a personal forecast.

Across every dataset, the practical takeaway stays consistent: a large share of severe and fracture-level skateboarding injuries clusters into head impacts, wrist fractures on outstretched hands, and motor-vehicle collisions on open roads. Wearing a certified helmet, strapping on rigid wrist guards, skating in traffic-free parks, and [building ankle and hip stability off the board](/blog/skateboarding-balance-training-over-40/) will not eliminate ankle rolls, knee strains, or bruising, but those four habits directly target the injuries that send older skaters to the emergency room most often.
