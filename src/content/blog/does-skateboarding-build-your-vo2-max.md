VO2 max has become the number every longevity-focused doctor, podcast, and fitness watch wants you thinking about. If you've spent any time in that world lately, you've probably absorbed the headline: your body's capacity to take in and use oxygen during intense effort is one of the strongest predictors researchers have found for how long — and how well — you're likely to live. A patient asked me last month, half-joking, whether her skate sessions were "wasting" her cardio time compared to running. They're not, but the honest answer to whether skating builds VO2 max is: it depends on how you skate, same as most cardio questions.

## What VO2 max actually measures

VO2 max is the maximum rate at which your body can take in, transport, and use oxygen during maximal exertion. It's usually measured in a lab, on a treadmill or bike, with you breathing into a mask while the intensity ramps up until you can't sustain it anymore. The number itself reflects several systems working together — lung capacity, heart output, blood's oxygen-carrying ability, and how efficiently your muscles actually use the oxygen once it arrives.

The reason it's gotten so much attention outside exercise-science circles is that some of the largest studies ever done linking fitness to mortality have found cardiorespiratory fitness to be one of the strongest predictors of long-term survival researchers have identified — in several major analyses, a stronger predictor than traditional risk factors like blood pressure, smoking status, or even diabetes. You don't need to obsess over the exact number a smartwatch estimates for you, which is itself only a rough approximation of a real lab test, but the underlying idea — that building your aerobic ceiling protects your health span, not just your performance — holds up well.

## Where skating fits on the intensity scale

This site has already covered where skateboarding sits on the standard MET scale exercise physiologists use: general skateboarding lands around 5 METs, solidly moderate intensity, and sustained, hard-pumping sessions push toward the 6 MET mark that's the entry point for vigorous activity. [The full breakdown is here](/blog/skateboarding-counts-as-real-exercise/) if you want the numbers behind it.

That matters for VO2 max specifically because the training effect that improves it comes from sustained time at a genuinely elevated effort — not from occasional short bursts of hard work followed by long rests. This is the same distinction covered in [the Zone 2 piece](/blog/skateboarding-zone-2-heart-rate-training/): skating covers a wide range of the intensity spectrum, and which part of it you spend your time in is mostly a function of how continuously you move.

## The two skating styles that build it, and the one that mostly doesn't

**Standing around between short attempts** — the classic technical-street session, working one trick for twenty minutes with long rests between tries — does very little for VO2 max, whatever else it does for your skill. Your heart rate spikes and drops constantly, and the sustained load a VO2-max adaptation actually needs never accumulates.

**Continuous pumping through transition** — bowls, mini-ramps, flow sections where you're generating speed with your legs rather than pushing with your foot — is genuinely effective. Sustained, moderately hard effort held for extended stretches is close to the textbook stimulus for building aerobic capacity, and it's the mode most adult skaters underuse because it's less immediately social than standing around a feature with friends.

**Interval-style sessions** — repeated hard runs at a feature with short recoveries between them — also train it, through a different, well-established mechanism: high-intensity interval training is one of the more time-efficient ways to raise VO2 max, and a session built around hard sprints or hard lines with brief rest between them is functionally an interval workout, even if nobody planned it that way.

## Making a session count toward it, if that's the goal

You don't need to turn every session into structured training. But if VO2 max specifically is what you're after on a given day, a few adjustments make a real difference:

**Favor terrain with continuous movement.** A bowl or flow section you can pump through repeatedly gives you far more sustained elevated heart rate than a technical spot where you reset constantly.

**Build in real duration.** Thirty to forty-five minutes of mostly continuous effort, at a pace where conversation is difficult but not impossible, is a reasonable target — this is the same range covered in the Zone 2 guide, and time in the effort zone is what drives the adaptation, not brief spikes.

**Or go the other direction on purpose — with real screening first.** A session structured as genuinely hard runs — pushing yourself close to your limit for 20–30 seconds, recovering, then going again — trains the same capacity through intervals instead, and is a legitimate substitute on days continuous flow terrain isn't available. But this is the highest-intensity option here, and it deserves the same caveat [the Zone 2 guide](/blog/skateboarding-zone-2-heart-rate-training/) gives its own targets: if you're new to structured training, inactive for a long stretch before your comeback, managing a cardiovascular condition, or on a medication that alters heart rate, get your actual safe intensity range from a doctor or cardiologist before pushing close to your limit, and build up to it gradually rather than starting there. Near-maximal effort on a moving board carries a second risk ordinary interval training doesn't: exertional dizziness or a sudden drop in control turns into a fall, not just a stopped treadmill, so this is one to earn through gradual progression rather than attempt on day one.

**Don't assume a casual cruising session is doing this work.** Easy pushing on flat ground is pleasant, low-impact movement with real value of its own, but it mostly won't be the stimulus that moves your VO2 max, because the effort level rarely gets high enough for long enough.

## The honest bottom line

Skateboarding can be a genuine part of building and maintaining your VO2 max, and for a lot of adults it's a far more sustainable way to get that stimulus than forcing yourself onto a treadmill, because you'll actually keep showing up for it. But it isn't automatic. A session built around standing, chatting, and short attempts is wonderful for a lot of reasons — community, skill, mental health — and isn't, on its own, training this particular system. If VO2 max is part of why you're skating, structure at least some sessions around continuous movement or genuine intervals, and let the rest stay exactly as unstructured and social as you want it to be.
