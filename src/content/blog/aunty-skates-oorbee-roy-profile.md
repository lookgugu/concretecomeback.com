At almost every weekend skatepark, there is a row of parents sitting on the concrete ledge by the fence while their kids take turns on the banks. Some of those adults want to try rolling down the mellow slope themselves, and many talk themselves out of it for the same reason: they do not want to be the only forty-something learning in public while everyone else already knows how to ride. Coming back to a board at forty, I think fear of falling is only half the barrier. The harder part for many adults is feeling that the park was never meant for someone who looks like them.

Toronto skater Oorbee Roy built her entire platform by stepping straight into that discomfort. As she recounted in a [June 2022 first-person piece for *Best Health*](https://www.besthealthmag.ca/article/aunty-skates-skateboarder/), Roy had watched her husband skate for years and then watched her two children pick it up too; in 2018, at age forty-three, she took a beginner lesson right after her daughter's lesson because she refused to stay the parent standing on the sidelines. In February 2021, during a Toronto COVID-19 lockdown, she launched the TikTok account `@auntyskates` to document her progress as an adult skateboarder—dropping into bowls, working on ollies, and skating alongside her kids.

As [Chase DiBenedetto wrote in a January 2022 *Mashable* profile](https://mashable.com/article/skateboarding-mom-oorbee-roy-tik-tok), Roy's account challenged narrow assumptions about who belongs on a skateboard and what a late-in-life hobby looks like for older women of color. Instead of waiting until she was an expert to be seen at the park, she made learning in her forties—pads, falls, plateaus, and recovery days included—the entire point of the feed.

## Turning online visibility into people on concrete

A viral clip can give someone permission to buy a complete, but a screen does not keep you skating through a cold month. What makes Roy's trajectory useful to local organisers is what she did beyond social video. Through [Aunty Skates](https://auntyskates.com/) and her Toronto clinics, she moved from posting progress clips to teaching beginner adults how to get on a board safely, ride mini-ramps and bowls, and join week-long women's skate, surf, and yoga retreats in Costa Rica—giving older learners a specific date, a group of peers at the same stage, and a place to show up in person.

That shift from watching someone online to rolling alongside people near you is where a comeback actually sticks. Whether it takes the shape of a neighbourhood meetup when you are [finding other older skaters](/blog/finding-other-adult-skaters/) or a regional network like [Board Women](/blog/board-women-midlife-community/) in the UK, the mechanics are the same: adults stay on boards when they do not have to be impressive to belong at the park.

Roy did not wait until she had a deep bag of tricks before taking up space on the coping, and nobody at a healthy adult session is waiting for you to be polished either. The best thing a local crew can borrow from her example is how people treat the newest rider on the flatground—saving room on the bank, welcoming a first clean kickturn, and making sure the person who finally stepped off the parents' bench feels part of the session before they ever land a trick.
