If you've spent any time in adult-skater corners of the internet, you've probably already seen her: a woman in her 40s, skating in a sari, going by "Aunty Skates." If you haven't, it's worth an introduction, because her story is one of the clearest proof points I know of that this comeback thing you're doing isn't a niche curiosity. It's a real, growing, public movement — and she's one of the people who made it visible.

## Who she actually is

Oorbee Roy is a mother of two from Toronto who started taking skateboarding lessons at 43, after watching her family skate. She's said she was probably the oldest person in the class and that it took her about six months to feel comfortable on the board. In February 2021 she started posting the process on TikTok under the name Aunty Skates — a deliberate riff on the "aunty" figure from South Asian culture, the older woman who's supposed to have already settled into a fixed, sensible role by this point in life. Filming herself learning to skate, often in traditional Indian dress, was the joke and the point at the same time: here's an aunty doing the opposite of what an aunty is supposed to do.

It worked. The clip that broke the account open was one her kids filmed at a Toronto park of her clearing a bowl in a purple sari; it went viral, and she passed 100,000 followers within four months of starting. The growth has been slower since but it hasn't stopped: by the time the Globe and Mail profiled her in April 2025, when she was 50, she was past 185,000 TikTok followers. Numbers aside, the actual substance of her content has stayed consistent the whole time: a grown woman, visibly still learning, having fun doing it in public.

## Why the sari matters as much as the skating

It would be easy to read this as a novelty act — skater in traditional dress, good for a scroll-stopping clip — but that undersells what's actually going on. The outfit is the whole argument compressed into one image: skateboarding and "aunty" are not supposed to belong in the same frame, and every video where they do chips away at that assumption a little more.

That's directly useful to anyone reading a site called Concrete Comeback. A lot of the hesitation adults feel about starting or returning to skating isn't really about balance or fear of falling — it's about whether this activity is "for" someone like them. Roy's entire platform is built on answering that question in public, one clip at a time, for an audience that includes a lot of people who look like her and are exactly her age.

## From videos to an actual community

The TikTok following is the visible part, but it's not the whole story. Roy has been explicit that the goal is to get more adults onto boards, not just watching them: she started a Facebook group for exactly that, and she teaches lessons for adults in Toronto — the same basic move a lot of the local organisers covered on this site have made: turn an online following into people showing up at a real park together.

In 2023 she took that further and launched an annual skate retreat in Costa Rica, bringing adult skaters together for a trip built around the sport rather than around skating being a side activity on someone else's vacation. The 2026 edition was a week-long women's retreat with beginner lessons built into the schedule. The underlying idea is the same one behind every community effort worth copying: give people a specific date and place to show up, and a lot of them will.

## What her story actually proves

None of this required Roy to be exceptional at skateboarding. Watch her content and you'll see someone who is visibly still a learner, still building basic tricks, still figuring things out — and that's exactly why it resonates with the audience it does. She didn't wait to get good before she started sharing the process. The sharing and the learning happened at the same time, in public, which is a much harder and much more honest thing to do than waiting until you've "arrived" to say anything about it.

That's a useful model whether or not you have any interest in posting your own skating online. The lesson isn't "build a following." It's that visible, ongoing beginnerhood — the thing a lot of adults are most afraid of — is exactly what built an audience of hundreds of thousands of people who wanted to see themselves in it. Nobody was waiting for her to be impressive. They were waiting to see someone like them try.

## Finding your own version of this

You don't need 185,000 followers to get the benefit of what Roy built. You need a handful of accounts and groups that remind you people your age are actually doing this, and ideally a local version of the same thing — a session, a group chat, a standing Sunday meetup.

If you haven't already, start by [finding other adult skaters](/blog/finding-other-adult-skaters/) near you, and look at what a community can grow into once it's organised, the way [Board Women](/blog/board-women-midlife-community/) did at scale in the UK. Following Aunty Skates and accounts like hers is a fine place to start feeling less alone in this. Showing up somewhere local, even just once, is what actually turns that feeling into a skating life.

## The part worth remembering

Oorbee Roy didn't get a following by hiding the beginner parts. She got one by showing them, consistently, at an age and in an outfit nobody expected to see on a skateboard. If you're sitting on the sidelines of your own comeback because you don't feel ready to be seen doing this yet, her whole platform is built on the opposite bet — and it paid off.
