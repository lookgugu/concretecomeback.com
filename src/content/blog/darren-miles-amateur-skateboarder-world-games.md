Darren Miles, from Plymouth in the UK, first got on a skateboard in 1977, in Norfolk, as a kid. He's 60 now, has never been a professional skater, and this October he's representing Great Britain in slalom at the World Skate Games in Asunción, Paraguay — on a national team built from a mixture of professionals and amateurs, him included.

That last detail is the one worth sitting with. This site has covered plenty of remarkable skaters lately: [Andy Macdonald becoming the oldest Olympic skateboarder at 51](/blog/andy-macdonald-oldest-olympic-skateboarder/), [Judi Oyama still racing slalom at 66](/blog/judi-oyama-oldest-competitive-skateboarder/). Both are extraordinary athletes with decades of professional competition behind them. Miles isn't that, and his story is useful precisely because of it. He's an ordinary guy who kept skating, picked up a new discipline late, and ended up on a national team without ever having a sponsor, a pro career, or a plan to get there.

## He didn't come back. He never fully left — and still had to relearn

Miles isn't a returning-after-decades story in the usual sense: he'd kept some connection to skating since the 1970s. But by his own account, his fitness had slipped noticeably after COVID-19 lockdowns, and when he picked up slalom specifically — racing a cone course on a longboard, built around carving technique rather than vert or street tricks — it meant essentially relearning the sport from a standing start.

That's a more relatable starting point than it might sound. A lot of adults reading this site aren't true beginners either; they're carrying some old muscle memory and a body that doesn't match it anymore. Miles's version of dealing with that wasn't to wait until he felt ready. It was to pick a discipline suited to where his body actually was, and build from there.

## Why slalom, specifically

Slalom rewards exactly the things an older body still has plenty of: balance, technique, reading a line, controlling speed through smooth weight transfer. It doesn't ask for the high-impact landings that vert and technical street skating do. [Judi Oyama's slalom career](/blog/judi-oyama-oldest-competitive-skateboarder/) makes the same point from the professional end of the sport — Miles is the amateur proof that the same logic works just as well for someone who picked it up as a hobby in their late fifties.

If your own comeback has you gravitating toward carving and flat-ground work rather than stairs and rails, Miles and Oyama are both evidence that this isn't a lesser version of skating. It's a legitimate discipline with its own ceiling, and that ceiling is a long way up.

## The point of competing without a sponsor

Most skating stories that make the news involve a record, a medal, or a career. Miles's story is simpler: he kept doing something he liked, improved enough at a new version of it to make a national team roster, and is going to a World Games as himself — not as a marketing story, not chasing a result that changes his life. He's going because he kept showing up.

That's a genuinely different kind of motivation than the one usually on offer in sports media, and it's the one that actually applies to almost everyone reading this. You are extremely unlikely to ever need a sponsor, a pro model, or a Hall of Fame nomination to justify the hours you put into skating. Miles's trip to Paraguay is proof that the sport will still make room for you, and take you seriously, without any of that.

## What actually transfers to your comeback

**You don't need to go pro for this to matter.** Miles has skated, on and off, for close to 50 years without once needing a professional career to justify it. The hours were worth it on their own terms the whole way through.

**Picking the right discipline for your body is a skill, not a compromise.** Switching to slalom after lockdown wasn't Miles settling for something easier. It was matching the sport to what his body could actually do well, the same move this site has recommended again and again for adults weighing street skating against carving, cruising, or bowl riding.

**Representing something — even just "the amateur who kept at it" — doesn't require elite status.** You don't need a medal to have a story worth telling. You need to keep going long enough that the story accumulates on its own.

If you're a few months into your own comeback and still measuring yourself against the pros this site writes about, Miles is the other half of the picture: the guy from Plymouth who never turned pro, kept skating anyway, and is representing his country at 60 because he never stopped showing up long enough for it to become possible.
