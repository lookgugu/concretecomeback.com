Patients used to ask me, half-apologetically, whether skateboarding "really" counted as exercise, or whether they should also be doing something more legitimate on top of it. I understood the question. Skating doesn't look like a workout. There's no machine telling you your heart rate zone, no class structure, no sense of grinding through reps. It looks like play.

It also is one, by every standard measure exercise physiologists use to classify physical activity. It's worth knowing the actual numbers, because the "is this real exercise" doubt is one of the more common reasons adults quietly downgrade skating in their own minds — and that doubt can chip away at the motivation to keep showing up.

## Where skateboarding sits on the intensity scale

Exercise scientists classify activities using METs — metabolic equivalents, a measure of how much energy an activity burns relative to sitting still. One MET is your resting metabolic rate. Walking at a moderate pace sits around 3 METs. Anything at 6 METs or above is generally classed as vigorous activity.

Skateboarding is typically classified in the 5–6 MET range, depending on how it's being ridden. Casual cruising sits toward the lower end; active skating at a park — pumping, carving, working transitions — sits at the upper end, putting it in the same intensity bracket as recreational basketball or a moderate-paced jog. This isn't a fringe classification. It's the same framework used to write exercise prescriptions in clinical settings.

What that means in practice: a session that feels like messing around on a board is, physiologically, comparable to the workouts people specifically schedule and sweat through at the gym.

## Calorie burn, roughly

Calorie estimates for any activity depend heavily on body weight, intensity, and how continuously you're moving versus standing around chatting between runs — so treat any specific number as a rough guide, not a precise figure. As a general range, an hour of active skateboarding burns somewhere in the neighborhood of 300–500 calories for most adults, comparable to an hour of moderate cycling or a brisk hike.

The honest caveat: a typical skate session isn't continuous exertion the way a structured cardio class is. You're stopping to rest, session with friends, wait your turn at a feature. That's fine — it doesn't erase the intensity of the active portions, and the interval-like pattern of hard bursts followed by recovery is itself a legitimate training structure, not a lesser version of "real" cardio.

## The bone density piece nobody mentions

Weight-bearing, impact-tolerant activity is one of the standard recommendations for maintaining bone density as you age, because bone responds to mechanical loading by staying denser — a mechanism sometimes summarized as "use it or lose it" for skeletal tissue. Walking provides some of this. Running provides more. Skateboarding, with its pushing, landing, and repeated small impacts, sits in that same weight-bearing category.

This matters more the older you get. Bone density naturally declines with age, and the activities that best counter that decline are the ones involving actual mechanical load on the skeleton — not purely low-impact options like swimming or stationary cycling, which are excellent for cardiovascular health but do less for bone. Skateboarding gives you both in one activity.

## Why the framing actually matters

Believing skating "doesn't really count" has a practical cost: people who see an activity as pure recreation are less likely to prioritize it when life gets busy, and more likely to feel guilty skipping the gym in favor of a skate session. Believing it counts — because it does — makes it easier to treat as legitimate training and schedule it accordingly.

This cuts the other way too. Skating is not a full substitute for every kind of fitness. It builds balance, leg power, and cardiovascular capacity extremely well; it does less for upper-body strength or deep flexibility. Treat it as a strong core of your fitness routine, not the entirety of it.

## What this means for how you skate

If you're skating specifically for the fitness benefit, a few adjustments push the intensity higher: favor pumping transitions over standing and rolling, keep sessions moving rather than long standing breaks between runs, and treat parks with real elevation change — bowls, ramps — as a better cardio stimulus than flat, mellow cruising.

But the bigger point stands regardless of how you skate. The next time the thought "I should probably also do a real workout" shows up after a good session, the science says you already did one. Skating earns its place on the same list as running and basketball — not because it feels like effort, but because, measured the way exercise physiologists measure everything else, it is.
