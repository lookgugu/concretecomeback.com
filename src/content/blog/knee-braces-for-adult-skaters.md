A patient asked me last month whether strapping on a knee brace before a session meant she was "basically admitting she's old." She'd been skating twice a week for three months, loved it, and her knee ached by the end of most sessions — but the brace sitting in her gear bag felt like a concession she wasn't ready to make.

I hear a version of this a lot. Somewhere along the way, a brace got coded as a last resort instead of what it actually is: a tool, like wrist guards or soft wheels, that some knees benefit from and some don't. Here's how to figure out which category you're in.

## The wrist guard comparison, and where it breaks down

This site's [gear guide for aging joints](/blog/gear-for-aging-joints/) already covers wrist guards as a near-universal recommendation, because almost every adult skater falls onto their hands eventually. Knees are different. A brace isn't a blanket recommendation the way wrist guards are — it's a targeted fix for a specific problem, and wearing one for the wrong reason doesn't help much. The first question isn't "which brace," it's "do I actually need one, and why."

Good reasons to consider a brace: a diagnosed ligament issue, osteoarthritis that's worse on one side of the joint than the other, or a knee you're rebuilding confidence in after an old, already-assessed injury. Less good reasons: general soreness that responds fine to softer wheels and a longer warm-up, or wearing one because it feels safer with no specific problem behind it. If that's you, [the orthopedic checklist](/blog/orthopedic-checklist-for-skaters-over-40/) and a proper warm-up routine will likely do more than a brace ever will. And if your knee has ever actually given way, or feels unstable in a way that's new rather than familiar, that's not a shopping question at all yet — stop skating on it and get it assessed before you buy anything, the same way [the site's guide to in-session joint support](/blog/in-session-joint-management-guide/) recommends for any new instability.

## The three types, and what each one is actually for

**Compression sleeves** are the simplest option — an elastic tube that slides over the knee, available at any pharmacy for not much money. They don't add structural support, but they improve proprioception (your brain's sense of where the joint is in space) and provide mild compression that some skaters find genuinely reduces ache and swelling after a session. If your knee has a familiar, already-assessed ache — the kind you recognize, not a new symptom — a sleeve is the sensible place to start. A new ache is a different situation: [stop and get it looked at first](/blog/in-session-joint-management-guide/) rather than masking it with compression while you keep loading whatever's causing it.

**Hinged braces** add rigid or semi-rigid supports on either side of the knee, connected by a hinge that moves with the joint. They give real side-to-side stability, which makes them the right fit once you're skating on a knee with a diagnosed, known instability — an old ligament repair, or a joint your physio has told you needs that extra support. The hinge lets you bend and extend normally while resisting the sideways forces a wobbly landing or an awkward push-off can put through the joint. A hinged brace belongs on a joint your clinician has already looked at and cleared — not as a first response to a knee that has given way or feels newly unstable, which, as above, needs [assessing before you buy anything](/blog/in-session-joint-management-guide/).

**Unloader braces** are the most specialized and most expensive of the three, usually fitted by an orthotist rather than bought off a shelf. They're built for osteoarthritis that's worse on one side of the knee — usually the inner side — and they work by shifting load away from that damaged compartment toward the healthier one. If you've been told your osteoarthritis is concentrated in one compartment, this is the brace type with the most clinical research behind it — knee bracing for osteoarthritis carries a moderate-strength recommendation in the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons' 2021 guideline — and it's worth asking your physio or orthopedist whether you're a candidate rather than guessing from a product page.

## Roughly what to expect to spend

Compression sleeves run cheap — think pocket-change to maybe thirty dollars for a well-made one. Hinged braces cost meaningfully more — from a few tens of dollars for a basic off-the-shelf model to a couple of hundred for a well-built one, depending on the brand and how much lateral support it's built to give. Unloader braces are the real investment — custom or semi-custom versions fitted by a specialist can run into the hundreds, sometimes covered in part by insurance if you have a documented diagnosis. Don't buy at the top of that range before you've confirmed, with an actual assessment, that you need what it provides.

## Fit matters more than brand

Whichever type you land on, a brace that slips down mid-session or bunches behind the knee when you crouch is worse than no brace at all — it's a distraction at exactly the moment you need to be paying attention to your landing. Try a brace during a low-stakes session before trusting it on terrain that matters, and check it still holds its position through a deep squat, not just standing still in a fitting room.

## What wearing one actually means

It means you've identified a specific issue and you're managing it deliberately, the same as choosing softer wheels for a bad back or a helmet with a better fit. It doesn't mean your comeback has an expiration date. Plenty of skaters in their 40s, 50s, and well beyond wear a brace every session and have no plans to stop skating because of it — the brace is what's letting them keep going, not a sign that they shouldn't be.

If your knee is telling you something specific, listen to what it's actually saying before you reach for a product. A sleeve, a hinge, or an unloader each solve a different problem, and matching the tool to the actual issue is what makes any of them worth wearing.
