I used to plan trips the normal way — pick a destination, then figure out what to do once I got there. These days it's often backwards. I find a city with a park or two worth skating, then build the trip around it. It sounds like a small shift, but it's changed how I travel more than almost anything else I've picked up since getting back on a board.

If you've never thought about a vacation this way, here's how to actually do it.

## Why build a trip around a park at all

A new skatepark gives you something most tourist stops don't: an actual activity, not just a view. You get real time in a place instead of a photo of it — watching how a local skate community moves, who shows up at what hour, what the regional style looks like. It's also just a good way to meet people. Skaters are generally easy to talk to at a park, and "I'm visiting from out of town" is an instant conversation starter almost anywhere in the world.

It's a different kind of travel memory than a landmark, too. Most people can't tell you much about the museum they walked through on a trip five years ago. Most skaters can describe, in detail, the quarter pipe in a park three countries away.

## Finding parks worth the detour

Start broad, then narrow using the same research habits that work for scouting any new park — Google Maps reviews and photos, Instagram hashtags for the park or city, and local or regional skate subreddits. (If you haven't done this kind of research before, [this site's guide to scouting a park before you go](/blog/how-to-scout-a-skatepark-before-you-go/) covers the mechanics in more depth than I'll repeat here — everything there applies just as well to a park you're flying to as one you're driving to.)

For trip planning specifically, add a few more filters:

**Search "best skatepark [country/region]" before you pick a city.** A lot of destinations have a park worth the trip on its own — flagship municipal builds, former competition venues, or parks that turn up repeatedly on "best of" lists compiled by skate media and local shops. Let that search shape your shortlist of cities rather than retrofitting a park search onto a city you'd already picked for other reasons.

**Check for a local skate shop near the park.** A shop is a better source of current, accurate local information than almost anything online — current conditions, which other parks nearby are worth it, when the quiet hours actually are versus what a two-year-old review says. If you can, stop in before you skate. A five-minute conversation there is often worth more than an hour of research at home.

**Look at what else is nearby.** The most satisfying travel days I've had combine a park session with something else close by — a coffee shop, a coastal walk, a neighborhood worth wandering. A park in isolation, miles from anything else, is a harder sell for a full travel day than one that's part of a walkable cluster of things worth doing.

## Traveling with a board

This is the part that stops a lot of people before they start, and it's more manageable than it seems.

**Flying:** Most airlines treat a skateboard as checked luggage, sometimes as an oversized or sporting-equipment item with its own fee — policies vary enough by airline that it's worth checking the specific carrier's sporting-equipment policy before you book, rather than assuming. A padded board bag protects your setup and makes it easier to carry alongside normal luggage. If you're flying somewhere for a shorter trip and don't want to deal with board logistics at all, many destination cities with an active skate scene also have shops that rent or sell secondhand decks — worth a quick search before you assume you need to bring your own.

**Driving:** The easy case. Just don't forget gear in the rush of packing for everything else — pads and a helmet get left behind more often than the board itself, because the board is the thing you remember to grab and the gear is the thing you don't.

**Packing list beyond the obvious:** a multitool for truck and wheel adjustments (terrain and climate differences can mean a different setup feels better than what you ride at home), your own bearings if you're picky about them, and a printed or downloaded copy of whatever you researched about the park — don't assume you'll have signal when you arrive.

## Timing the session

Jet lag and unfamiliar terrain are a bad combination for a first session. Give yourself a low-stakes first visit — go watch, push around the flat sections, get a feel for the surface and the crowd before trying to do much more. Save any pushing of your limits for day two or three, once you've adjusted and the park isn't a complete unknown anymore.

Early morning and weekday midday are reliably quieter almost everywhere, same as at home, and that's doubly useful when you're also adjusting to an unfamiliar layout — fewer people to navigate around while you're still learning where the lines run.

## Make it a habit, not a one-off

Once you've done this once, it's hard to go back to planning trips the old way. A handful of travel skaters I know now keep a running list of parks they want to hit, the same way other people keep a restaurant list, and pick destinations partly by what's still unchecked. You don't need to go that far to get the benefit. Even picking one trip a year and building a single afternoon around a park you've never skated is enough to notice the difference — you come home with more than photos. You come home having actually been somewhere, on your own board, with whoever happened to be at that park the day you showed up.
