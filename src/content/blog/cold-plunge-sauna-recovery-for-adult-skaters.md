I resisted the cold plunge trend for a long time, mostly out of stubbornness — it looked like one more expensive wellness ritual borrowed from people with more disposable income and free time than sense. Then a training partner talked me into trying an ice bath after a rough weekend of hard bowl sessions, and I noticed the next day was genuinely better than usual. That's not nothing, but it's also not proof of anything, and it's worth being honest about the difference before you spend money on a tub or a sauna membership.

## Where this evidence actually comes from

Most of what we know about cold water immersion and sauna use for recovery comes from endurance sport and general strength training research, not from anything skating-specific. That matters, because skating's demand profile is different from both: it's heavy on eccentric loading (absorbing landings, controlling deceleration), heavy on balance and reactive movement, and light on the steady-state endurance work most of this research is built around. Treat everything here as reasonable extrapolation, not a skating-specific verdict.

## Cold water immersion: what it actually does

**It reduces perceived soreness.** That's the most consistent finding across the research on cold water immersion after exercise: a [Cochrane review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008262.pub2) of trials using water below 15°C found some evidence that it beats plain rest at reducing how sore people report feeling in the day or two afterward, while warning that the trials were small and not well designed. That's a genuine, useful effect if you've got another session, work, or family commitments the next day and need to feel less wrecked.

**The controversy: it may blunt training adaptations.** This is the part worth knowing before you make cold plunging a habit after every session. The clearest study is a 12-week trial in which 21 men strength-trained twice a week and finished every session with either 10 minutes in 10°C water or 10 minutes of easy cycling ([*Journal of Physiology*, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1113/JP270570)): the cold-water group gained less strength and less muscle, and the researchers traced it to a dampened muscle-building signal in the hours after training — likely because the inflammatory response that immersion suppresses is part of how your body triggers adaptation in the first place. The effect shows up most clearly in research on resistance training aimed at building muscle and strength; it's less clear how much it applies to skating's mix of skill practice, balance work, and impact tolerance, which don't map cleanly onto a hypertrophy protocol.

**The practical read:** if you're chasing pure adaptation — trying to build strength, bone density, or connective tissue resilience over months — routine cold immersion after every session may be working slightly against you. If you need to recover fast for a specific reason — a competition, a trip, a physically demanding week ahead — using it selectively makes more sense than making it a daily ritual.

**Cold immersion carries its own cardiovascular caution, separate from sauna's.** Sudden cold exposure triggers a cold-shock response — an abrupt gasp reflex, spike in breathing rate, heart rate, and blood pressure — that can provoke a dangerous arrhythmia in someone with hypertension, an existing heart rhythm condition, or other cardiovascular disease. This isn't a reason to avoid cold immersion outright, but it is a reason to get medical clearance first if any of that applies to you, and to ease in with a cold shower or a moderately cool bath before a full ice-water plunge rather than starting with the coldest, longest version you can find. The soreness-reduction studies behind this use specific, controlled water temperatures held for longer than most people guess — a [meta-analysis in *Sports Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-015-0431-7) found 11–15°C for 11–15 minutes worked best, which is cold, not ice-cold — so treat any home version as an approximation of that research rather than an exact, proven protocol, and let how your body actually responds guide the duration rather than assuming shorter is automatically just as effective.

## Sauna and heat: a different, less contested case

Sauna use has a better-established case for general cardiovascular and recovery benefit, largely from a Finnish study that followed 2,315 men aged 42 to 60 for a median of 21 years ([*JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.8187)) and found that men using a sauna four to seven times a week had about 40% lower all-cause mortality than once-a-week users, with sudden cardiac death cut even more sharply. It's an observational link — frequent sauna-goers may differ in other ways — but a striking one in exactly the age range this site is written for. Add to that more mechanistic work on heat exposure improving blood vessel function and triggering some of the same cellular stress-adaptation pathways that exercise itself does.

For recovery specifically, heat doesn't carry the same "blunts your gains" controversy that cold immersion does — if anything, some research suggests heat exposure may support adaptation rather than working against it, through effects on blood flow and certain repair-related proteins. It's also simply pleasant in a way that supports the unglamorous but real recovery habit of actually relaxing after a hard session, which matters for the reasons [the sleep and recovery guide](/blog/sleep-most-underrated-skate-recovery-tool/) covers on the rest side of the equation.

The caution here isn't about undermining training — it's cardiovascular. Sauna use raises heart rate and drops blood pressure through fluid shifts and vasodilation, and combining that with dehydration from a hard skate session is a real, avoidable risk. If you have any cardiovascular condition, are on blood pressure medication, or aren't sure, check with your doctor before adding regular sauna sessions, and never combine sauna use with alcohol.

## Timing, if you're going to use either

**Skip cold immersion immediately after a session focused on strength or skill-building you actually want to improve.** The research behind the adaptation-blunting concern tests immersion right after exercise, in that narrow post-workout window — it doesn't establish a specific safe cutoff afterward, and the biological signals that drive adaptation keep running for hours, not minutes. Waiting until later that evening or the next day is a reasonable, lower-risk habit if soreness reduction is still your goal, but treat it as an untested guess at caution, not a confirmed workaround that fully protects your gains.

**Cold immersion is the more useful choice between sessions of a demanding stretch where feeling fresh matters more than long-term adaptation** — the evening after day one of a multi-day trip where you're skating every day, or between rounds of a tournament, rather than immediately before you get back on the board. Cold exposure without time to rewarm can leave muscles measurably cooler, less powerful, and less coordinated for a while afterward, which is a fall risk on a board rather than just a performance dip — so build in a few hours, not minutes, between a cold session and your next time riding. The cardiovascular caution above still applies every time, event or not.

**Sauna use has more flexibility around timing**, since it doesn't carry the same adaptation-blunting concern — but skip it when you're already dehydrated, and rehydrate properly before you use one after a hard session.

**Neither replaces the fundamentals.** [Sleep](/blog/sleep-most-underrated-skate-recovery-tool/), [structured recovery days](/blog/recovery-days-for-adult-skaters/), and eating enough protein and calories to actually repair tissue do more for your recovery than a cold plunge or a sauna ever will. Both are a reasonable addition on top of those basics, not a substitute for getting them right.

## A practical home version, if you're not buying equipment

You don't need a purpose-built cold plunge tub or a home sauna to test any of this, but "a couple of bags of ice" isn't a controlled temperature — a small tub with a lot of ice gets far colder than a large one with a little, and neither reliably lands in the 11–15°C range the research behind this actually tested. A cheap pool or aquarium thermometer removes the guesswork; a cold shower is a gentler, lower-risk starting point than an unmeasured ice bath while you're still working out how your body responds. A local gym or spa with sauna access is a reasonable way to try heat exposure a few times before deciding it's worth a bigger investment. Try either for a few weeks, pay attention to how your soreness and your actual training progress respond, and let that — not the wellness-media hype cycle, and not a guess at water temperature — decide whether it earns a permanent spot in your routine.
