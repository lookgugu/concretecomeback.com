I resisted the cold plunge trend for a long time, mostly out of stubbornness — it looked like one more expensive wellness ritual borrowed from people with more disposable income and free time than sense. Then a training partner talked me into trying an ice bath after a rough weekend of hard bowl sessions, and I noticed the next day was genuinely better than usual. That's not nothing, but it's also not proof of anything, and it's worth being honest about the difference before you spend money on a tub or a sauna membership.

## Where this evidence actually comes from

Most of what we know about cold water immersion and sauna use for recovery comes from endurance sport and general strength training research, not from anything skating-specific. That matters, because skating's demand profile is different from both: it's heavy on eccentric loading (absorbing landings, controlling deceleration), heavy on balance and reactive movement, and light on the steady-state endurance work most of this research is built around. Treat everything here as reasonable extrapolation, not a skating-specific verdict.

## Cold water immersion: what it actually does

**It reduces perceived soreness, reliably.** Across a large body of research on cold water immersion after exercise, the most consistent finding is a real reduction in how sore people report feeling in the day or two afterward. That's a genuine, useful effect if you've got another session, work, or family commitments the next day and need to feel less wrecked.

**The controversy: it may blunt training adaptations.** This is the part worth knowing before you make cold plunging a habit after every session. Several studies on strength and hypertrophy training have found that regular post-workout cold water immersion can dampen some of the muscle-building and strength-adaptation signal your body would otherwise produce — likely because the inflammatory response that immersion suppresses is part of how your body triggers adaptation in the first place. The effect shows up most clearly in research on resistance training aimed at building muscle and strength; it's less clear how much it applies to skating's mix of skill practice, balance work, and impact tolerance, which don't map cleanly onto a hypertrophy protocol.

**The practical read:** if you're chasing pure adaptation — trying to build strength, bone density, or connective tissue resilience over months — routine cold immersion after every session may be working slightly against you. If you need to recover fast for a specific reason — a competition, a trip, a physically demanding week ahead — using it selectively makes more sense than making it a daily ritual.

**Cold immersion carries its own cardiovascular caution, separate from sauna's.** Sudden cold exposure triggers a cold-shock response — an abrupt gasp reflex, spike in breathing rate, heart rate, and blood pressure — that can provoke a dangerous arrhythmia in someone with hypertension, an existing heart rhythm condition, or other cardiovascular disease. This isn't a reason to avoid cold immersion outright, but it is a reason to get medical clearance first if any of that applies to you, and to ease in with a cold shower or a moderately cool bath before a full ice-water plunge rather than starting with the coldest, longest version you can find. The soreness-reduction studies behind this generally use specific, controlled water temperatures held for longer than most people guess — often 10–15 minutes in cold, not ice-cold, water — so treat any home version as an approximation of that research rather than an exact, proven protocol, and let how your body actually responds guide the duration rather than assuming shorter is automatically just as effective.

## Sauna and heat: a different, less contested case

Sauna use has a better-established case for general cardiovascular and recovery benefit, largely from a long-running body of Finnish population research linking regular sauna use to lower cardiovascular risk over time, plus more mechanistic work on heat exposure improving blood vessel function and triggering some of the same cellular stress-adaptation pathways that exercise itself does.

For recovery specifically, heat doesn't carry the same "blunts your gains" controversy that cold immersion does — if anything, some research suggests heat exposure may support adaptation rather than working against it, through effects on blood flow and certain repair-related proteins. It's also simply pleasant in a way that supports the unglamorous but real recovery habit of actually relaxing after a hard session, which matters for the reasons [the sleep and recovery guide](/blog/sleep-most-underrated-skate-recovery-tool/) covers on the rest side of the equation.

The caution here isn't about undermining training — it's cardiovascular. Sauna use raises heart rate and drops blood pressure through fluid shifts and vasodilation, and combining that with dehydration from a hard skate session is a real, avoidable risk. If you have any cardiovascular condition, are on blood pressure medication, or aren't sure, check with your doctor before adding regular sauna sessions, and never combine sauna use with alcohol.

## Timing, if you're going to use either

**Skip cold immersion immediately after a session focused on strength or skill-building you actually want to improve.** The research behind the adaptation-blunting concern tests immersion right after exercise, in that narrow post-workout window — it doesn't establish a specific safe cutoff afterward, and the biological signals that drive adaptation keep running for hours, not minutes. Waiting until later that evening or the next day is a reasonable, lower-risk habit if soreness reduction is still your goal, but treat it as an untested guess at caution, not a confirmed workaround that fully protects your gains.

**Cold immersion is the more useful choice before an event or a demanding stretch where feeling fresh matters more than long-term adaptation** — a tournament, a trip where you want to skate every day, a physically demanding week where showing up rested matters more than optimizing for the next training cycle. "More useful" isn't "unrestricted," though: the cardiovascular caution above still applies every time, event or not.

**Sauna use has more flexibility around timing**, since it doesn't carry the same adaptation-blunting concern — but skip it when you're already dehydrated, and rehydrate properly before you use one after a hard session.

**Neither replaces the fundamentals.** [Sleep](/blog/sleep-most-underrated-skate-recovery-tool/), [structured recovery days](/blog/recovery-days-for-adult-skaters/), and eating enough protein and calories to actually repair tissue do more for your recovery than a cold plunge or a sauna ever will. Both are a reasonable addition on top of those basics, not a substitute for getting them right.

## A practical home version, if you're not buying equipment

You don't need a purpose-built cold plunge tub or a home sauna to test any of this. A cold shower for a few minutes, or an ice bath in a regular tub with a couple of bags of ice, gives you most of the practical effect people are chasing. A local gym or spa with sauna access is a reasonable way to try heat exposure a few times before deciding it's worth a bigger investment. Try either for a few weeks, pay attention to how your soreness and your actual training progress respond, and let that — not the wellness-media hype cycle — decide whether it earns a permanent spot in your routine.
