Rory McIlroy won the 2026 Laureus World Comeback of the Year award at a ceremony in Madrid this April, for a 2025 season in which he finally completed golf's career Grand Slam by winning the Masters — his first major title in eleven years. He called it a huge honour, both personally and for the sport.

I organize adult skate sessions for a living, more or less, and I spend a lot of that time talking people through exactly the kind of gap McIlroy just closed: a long stretch where the thing you used to be good at stopped happening, followed by one specific day where it finally did again. His version played out on golf's biggest stages in front of television cameras. Most of ours plays out on a Tuesday evening at a nearly empty park. The shape of the gap, and what it took to close it, is the same.

## Eleven years is a long time to carry an unfinished thing

McIlroy won four majors by his mid-twenties and looked, for a while, like someone who was going to collect the career Grand Slam early and often. Then eleven years passed without a fifth. That's not a short slump — it's over a decade of being one specific tournament away from something he clearly wanted, in full public view, with every year that passed making the question "will he ever finish it?" louder.

Most adults carrying an unfinished thing from skating don't have reporters asking about it every spring, but the private version is familiar: the trick you could never quite land before you stopped skating, the drop-in you chickened out of, the session that ended badly and never got a rematch. An unfinished thing doesn't go away just because you stopped actively working on it. It sits there, and the longer it sits, the more weight it seems to accumulate — exactly the dynamic that made McIlroy's eleven-year wait feel so much heavier than a single bad round ever could.

## The award wasn't for winning. It was for the shape of the return.

Laureus didn't give McIlroy the Comeback of the Year award simply because he's a good golfer having a good season — he'd already been that for most of his career. It recognized the specific arc of a long public drought ending in the exact result it had been missing. The comeback was the story, not just the trophy.

That's worth sitting with if you've ever felt like a return to skating "doesn't count" unless it ends in something impressive — landing the trick you used to do, skating a bowl you used to be scared of, whatever your personal equivalent of a major title is. The arc is the thing people actually respond to, McIlroy's included. Nobody gets a comeback award for never having left.

## He didn't fix it by trying harder in the same way

Golf commentary around McIlroy's drought spent years picking apart what was supposedly wrong — his swing, his mental game, his course management — as if the fix was always going to be more of the same effort, aimed slightly better. What actually changed, by his own account over the years, was less about raw effort and more about how he carried the weight of wanting it. Closing an eleven-year gap wasn't about trying harder in the same way he'd already been trying. It was about changing his relationship to the outcome enough to let the outcome happen.

If you've been grinding at the same stalled trick or the same avoided section of the park using the exact same approach that hasn't worked for months, that's worth noticing. Sometimes the unlock isn't more reps of the thing that isn't working. It's stepping back far enough to change what trying actually looks like — slower, with less riding on any one attempt, with the pressure of "this has to be the time" deliberately turned down rather than up.

## What this actually looks like at the skatepark

A few ways to borrow this without owning a career Grand Slam of your own:

- **Name the unfinished thing instead of just avoiding it.** A specific trick or drop-in you've been quietly carrying for years loses some of its weight the moment you say out loud, even just to yourself, what it actually is.
- **Don't wait for a dramatic result to call it a comeback.** The return itself — showing up again, staying with it through a bad stretch — is the story, the same way it was for McIlroy before anyone handed him an award for it.
- **If your usual approach to the stuck thing isn't working, change the approach, not just the effort.** Lower the stakes on a single attempt. Try it slower, or earlier in a session before fatigue, or with nobody watching, before you try it harder.

McIlroy's major drought and your stalled kickflip aren't the same scale of problem, obviously. But an eleven-year public wait ending at the exact tournament that had eluded him longest says something true at any scale: the thing you've been carrying doesn't have an expiration date on when it's allowed to finally go right.
