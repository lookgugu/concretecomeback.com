In older-skater communities, the word "comeback" usually means one thing: pulling a board out of a garage or buying a fresh setup after fifteen or twenty years away, finding [a park that doesn't feel hostile](/blog/inclusive-skateparks-all-ages/), and relearning how to push without pulling a calf. That part takes nerve, and it gets most of the attention when adults talk about starting again. Six months or a year later, though, a different problem sets in. You are already back, rolling comfortably almost every week, and yet one specific thing—an axle stall, a four-foot quarter-pipe drop-in, a curb you keep rolling up to and bailing from—refuses to budge.

That second kind of stall is why Rory McIlroy winning the [2026 Laureus World Comeback of the Year Award](https://www.laureus.com/world-sports-awards/2026/laureus-world-comeback-of-the-year/rory-mcilroy) in Madrid on April 20 caught my eye. The award recognized his April 2025 victory at Augusta National, where he [defeated Justin Rose in a sudden-death playoff at the Masters](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2025/apr/14/rory-mcilroy-secures-career-grand-slam-after-dramatic-masters-playoff-win-over-justin-rose) to end an eleven-year drought in major championships and complete golf's career Grand Slam.

Between his fourth major in August 2014 and his fifth in April 2025, McIlroy never left golf. He stayed near the top of the rankings and kept winning regular tour events on both sides of the Atlantic. What stalled for more than a decade was one specific objective on four weeks of the calendar—and above all Augusta, the single title he still needed. Accepting the Laureus award by [video message this spring](https://golfbusinessnews.com/news/sponsorship-and-events/mcilroy-bags-second-laureus-world-sports-award/), he pointed straight to those years: "Completing the career Grand Slam was incredibly emotional. It's something I've been chasing for a long time, and it probably means more because of everything that came before it — the near misses, the setbacks, and the questions along the way."

## When the stall happens while you are already rolling

I want a group session to have room for the person who has been working on the same turn for months. They should not have to announce a new achievement every time they arrive. During a twenty-year break from skateboarding, you aren't trying to land anything, so nothing feels stalled; once you are showing up regularly, backing off the same feature week after week can easily make you feel like you owe the park an explanation.

Sports commentary loves to explain away a long drought as a mindset puzzle, as if McIlroy's ten-month turnaround from losing the [2024 U.S. Open at Pinehurst](https://apnews.com/article/rory-mcilroy-us-open-pinehurst-majors-0fd83a6f760248be0c234af75ea57409)—where he bogeyed three of the last four holes to lose by one shot—to winning at Augusta came down to discovering inner calm. The actual Sunday at the 2025 Masters looked nothing like a smooth breakthrough: he lost his lead, missed a short par putt on the 72nd hole that would have won in regulation, and had to walk back to the 18th tee minutes later for the playoff with Rose, where he birdied the same hole he had just bogeyed.

Nobody outside the ropes knows what happened in his head, and pretending there is a secret mental switch doesn't help a forty-four-year-old looking down a bank at a local skatepark either. What McIlroy's decade shows is how separate a single stubborn goal can be from the rest of your ability. He wasn't a failing golfer for eleven years; he was an active player who kept missing one tournament until the week he played an extra hole and made it. A healthy adult skate session works the same way: friends can show up, skate together, and trade runs on familiar concrete without anyone needing a progress report on the one trick that still hasn't clicked.
