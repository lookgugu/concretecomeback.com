Ask an adult skater what gear they wear and you'll hear helmet, wrist guards, maybe knee pads, almost every time. Almost nobody mentions their hips, even though a direct fall onto a hip is one of the more common ways a skateboarding fall actually lands, and a hip fracture is a dramatically more serious injury at 50 than it is at 20. Impact shorts — padded underlayers built to cushion that exact fall — are a real, buyable piece of gear that most returning skaters have simply never been told exists.

## Why the hip specifically deserves attention as you age

Bone density declines gradually through adulthood and accelerates for women around menopause, which [this site's piece on perimenopause](/blog/perimenopause-joints-and-skateboarding/) covers from the hormonal side. The hip is one of the areas that decline shows up most, and a hip fracture is a different category of injury from a sprained ankle or a broken wrist. Recovery is measured in months rather than weeks, surgery is common, and for older adults generally, a hip fracture carries real risk of lost independence afterward — not because skateboarding makes that specific outcome likely, but because it's the injury class where the stakes are highest if something does go wrong.

A fall directly onto the side of the hip, rather than a controlled slide or a hand-catch, is exactly the kind of fall that produces that injury. It's also a completely normal way to go down on a board — missing a trick, catching a wheel, or getting caught off guard by uneven pavement can all put you on the ground sideways with no time to tuck and roll the way you'd want to.

## What the hip-protector research actually says

Be honest about the evidence here, because it's more mixed than a typical gear-buying article lets on. The research base on hip protectors mostly comes from fall-prevention studies in older adults, not from skateboarding specifically — there's no skating-specific trial the way there's a real study behind the standard wrist-guard recommendation. Within that older-adult research, the picture is uneven: trials in nursing-home and residential-care settings have shown a modest, statistically significant reduction in hip fractures among people who actually wear the protectors. Trials of independent, community-dwelling older adults have generally not shown the same benefit.

Part of that gap is poor compliance. The devices studied in a lot of this research were bulky, hard plastic shells sewn into unflattering underwear, and plenty of participants in the community trials simply didn't wear them consistently enough for any protective effect to show up — a hip protector that stays in the drawer protects nobody. But compliance isn't the whole explanation: the largest community-based randomized trial also found no benefit among people who *did* wear theirs regularly (odds ratio 1.12, nowhere near a protective effect), and the Cochrane review's own conclusion is that hip protectors probably have little or no effect in community settings — not just that bad data obscured a real one. Treat poor compliance as a real, documented contributor to the mixed results, not a complete explanation for them.

## Why today's "impact shorts" are a different product

What's sold as impact shorts for action sports now — aimed at skaters, snowboarders, and mountain bikers — is a meaningfully different design from the rigid nursing-home shell studied in most of that research. Modern versions use slim, flexible foam or gel padding over the hip and tailbone, often the same impact-absorbing foam used in other protective sportswear, built into compression shorts that are actually comfortable enough to wear under normal clothes for a full session. That addresses part of why the community trials likely underperformed — but given that even regular wearers of the old-style protectors didn't show a benefit in the largest trial, a more wearable design closing the compliance gap is not the same as proof that it closes the protection gap too.

What that means is that today's impact shorts don't have their own body of skateboarding-specific evidence behind them, and the mixed community-trial results above mean you shouldn't assume comfort alone fixes what those trials found. What you're actually relying on is the basic physics of padding absorbing and spreading impact force — the same logic behind every other piece of protective gear on this site — on a product you'll realistically keep wearing, which matters but isn't a substitute for the impact-testing evidence the next section asks you to look for.

## What to look for when you buy

Coverage over the greater trochanter — the bony point on the outer hip you'd land on in a sideways fall — and documented impact testing are the two non-negotiables. Pad placement, coverage, and how much force the foam actually attenuates vary a lot between products, and a short that merely looks padded isn't the same as one that's been tested to show what it does on impact. If a product page doesn't state or link independent impact-attenuation testing, treat its protection as unproven rather than assuming coverage alone means protection — this matters even more if you're buying specifically because of a bone-density concern. Some versions extend coverage to the tailbone as well, which is worth having if you skate transition or anything with a backward-fall risk. Beyond that:

- **Fit that stays put.** A pad that shifts out of position during a session protects nothing. Try sitting, crouching, and dropping into a stance before you buy, not just standing in front of a mirror.
- **Breathable fabric.** You're layering this under regular clothes for a full session; a shell that doesn't breathe gets skipped on hot days, which is the compliance problem all over again.
- **Washability.** Gear that's unpleasant to clean gets worn less often. This matters more than it sounds like it should.
- **Pads you can remove or replace.** Foam compresses and loses some protective capacity over repeated hard impacts, same as helmet foam. A shell that lets you swap pads extends the useful life of the garment.

## Who should prioritize this most

Women past the menopause transition and anyone with a known bone density concern get the clearest benefit, given how directly hip fracture risk tracks with declining bone density. Beginners of any age are also a good fit, simply because less-controlled falls are more common early in a comeback, before your balance and falling technique catch up to your enthusiasm.

## The honest bottom line

Impact shorts aren't a guarantee against a hip fracture, and nobody should skate more aggressively because they're wearing a pair — the community-trial evidence above means that claim isn't one this site is willing to make. What they are is a low-friction way to add plausible, physics-backed protection to the one major impact zone most adult skaters have never thought to cover, in a design that's actually comfortable enough to wear consistently, which the older style of hip protector largely wasn't. Pair it with the basics — a real helmet, wrist guards, and skating within your current level — rather than treating it as a replacement for any of them.
