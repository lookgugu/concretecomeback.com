For years, most of what I did in clinic was rehab: someone shows up after a joint has already failed them, and we build it back up from there. Prehab flips that order. It's the same kind of loading and strengthening work, done before anything's gone wrong, aimed at the joints most likely to give you trouble later. In sports medicine generally, that shift — treating the joint before it breaks down rather than after — has moved from elite training rooms into mainstream advice for anyone active enough to have something to protect. Adult skaters are exactly the population it's for.

Rehab and prehab aren't different exercises so much as different timing. A lot of the moves below look like physical therapy homework, because in a sense they are — you're just doing them on your own schedule, before an injury forces the issue.

## Why adult skaters specifically need this

Skating loads three joints hard and repeatedly: ankles, knees, and hips. A twenty-year-old coming back from a break usually has enough residual joint capacity that a season of skating without any dedicated prep doesn't cost them much. An adult returner is working with less margin — years of sitting, less baseline mobility, and often some old injury history the joint hasn't fully forgotten. Prehab is how you rebuild that margin deliberately instead of finding out where it ran out the hard way, usually mid-session, on a landing that wouldn't have bothered you five years ago.

The other reason this matters more for returners than for people who never stopped: a joint that's been undertrained for years doesn't announce its weak points in advance. It just tolerates ordinary loads until one session asks slightly more of it than usual, and that's when something gives. Prehab work is aimed at finding and closing those gaps on your terms, in a controlled setting, rather than on a board.

## Ankle stability

Ankles absorb a huge share of the unpredictable, off-axis forces in skating — landing slightly rolled, catching an edge, correcting for a board that didn't land flat. Strength alone doesn't cover that; you need the joint to react correctly under load without you consciously directing it.

**Single-leg balance work** is the foundation. Standing on one foot, first with eyes open and then eyes closed, trains the small stabilizing muscles around the ankle to fire automatically. Do it near a wall or counter you can catch yourself on. Once that's easy, add an unstable surface — a folded towel or a pillow — but restart with eyes open on the new surface before progressing back to eyes closed; stacking a surface change and closed eyes together at the same time is a bigger jump than it looks like.

**Resistance band ankle work** — inversion, eversion, and dorsiflexion against a light band, a few sets a couple of times a week — builds strength through the ranges that a rolled landing actually stresses, not just the up-and-down motion most people's ankles get plenty of already from walking.

**Calf raises, done slowly.** A basic calf raise, controlled through the full range and paused briefly at the top, builds the lower-leg strength that supports the ankle joint itself. Two or three sets, a few times a week, is enough to notice a difference within a month or so.

## Knee loading

Knees in skating mostly take repetitive, compressive load — pumping, absorbing a landing, holding a crouch through a carve — rather than the sharp twisting people tend to picture first. That's not the whole picture, though: a rushed carve or a twist to catch your balance after a near-fall does load the knee sideways, and if you're carrying an old ACL tear or ligament repair, that twisting load is exactly what the joint tolerates worst. The prehab work below is aimed at the general compressive load every adult skater's knees take on; it's not a substitute for the tailored strength and stability work a physio would build around a specific ligament history.

**Step-downs and controlled squats** — slow, to a depth you can control without your knee caving inward — build the quad and glute strength that actually protects the joint under load. Depth matters less than control; a shallow squat done with a straight, tracking knee beats a deep one that wobbles.

**Terminal knee extensions** with a light band anchored at knee height train the last few degrees of extension specifically — a range that's easy to neglect and builds the quad strength to control your knee through it. This is strength work for the muscles around the joint, not a cue to skate with a locked knee: pumping itself wants the opposite, a low, bent, fluid stance that absorbs terrain rather than a straight leg that can't.

## Hip strengthening

The hip is the joint most likely to get skipped in an adult skater's own sense of what needs attention, because it rarely hurts until it already has a problem. Glute strength is the main lever here: bridges, clamshells, and single-leg glute work, done consistently, give the hip the support it needs to control the load skating puts through it rather than just absorbing that load passively.

## Building a simple routine

You don't need a separate gym day for this. Fifteen minutes, two or three times a week, covering one ankle exercise, one knee exercise, and one hip exercise, is enough to build real margin over a few months. Do it after a session, on a non-skating day, or at some other point in your day entirely — not as part of your pre-skate warm-up. Fatiguing sets like these are strength work, not warm-up material, and doing them right before you skate can temporarily blunt the power and reaction speed you need the moment you step on the board. Attach it to something you already do instead — after a shower, while something's in the microwave — so it doesn't depend on finding extra time you don't have.

Progress slowly. The point of prehab is steady, boring, cumulative loading, not a personal record. If an exercise causes sharp pain rather than the ordinary fatigue of working a muscle, stop and get it looked at rather than pushing through.

## When this isn't enough on its own

Prehab reduces risk; it doesn't eliminate it, and it's not a substitute for addressing pain that's already showing up. If a joint is already talking to you — a knee that aches after every session, a hip that pinches at the bottom of a crouch — that's a rehab conversation with an actual professional, not something a home routine will train its way past. Prehab is for the joint that's fine right now and you'd like to keep that way. Use it while things are quiet. That's the whole point of doing it early.
