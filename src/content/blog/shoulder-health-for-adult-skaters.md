Ask most adult skaters which joints worry them and you'll hear wrists, ankles, knees, maybe hips. Shoulders almost never come up — until a bad fall catches one wrong, and suddenly it's the joint that won't let you lift a coffee cup the next morning.

That silence is the problem. Every other major joint skating stresses has its own guide on this site. The shoulder doesn't, despite being one of the joints most likely to take the full force of a fall you didn't manage to roll out of.

## Why the shoulder takes the hit

When you fall on an outstretched arm, the force doesn't stop at your wrist. It travels up the forearm, through the elbow, and lands at the shoulder — a joint built for range of motion, not for absorbing sudden compressive or shearing load. That tradeoff is deliberate; a shoulder that moved less would also hit, carve, and push less well. It just means the joint has comparatively little structural backup when something goes wrong fast.

The shoulder is really a group of structures working together: the rotator cuff (four small muscles that stabilize the ball-and-socket joint), the acromioclavicular (AC) joint where your collarbone meets your shoulder blade, and the ligaments holding the whole thing in place. A hard fall can strain the rotator cuff, sprain or separate the AC joint, or in harder impacts, dislocate the joint itself or fracture the collarbone.

## Why this matters more at 40+ than it did at 20

Rotator cuff tendons lose some of their elasticity and blood supply with age, the same way other connective tissue does. If you played overhead sports — swimming, tennis, throwing sports, even years of manual work — your shoulder may already be carrying wear you're not aware of until it's tested by an unplanned fall. None of that means your shoulder is fragile. It means it benefits from the same proactive attention you'd give an ankle with an old sprain in it.

## Prehab, not rehab

The goal isn't to wait for an injury and then fix it — it's to build enough stability and range of motion that a hard fall is less likely to do real damage in the first place. None of this requires a gym membership.

**Band external rotations.** Anchor a light resistance band at elbow height, elbow bent 90 degrees and tucked at your side, and rotate your forearm outward against the band. Ten to fifteen slow reps, each side. This targets the rotator cuff muscles directly and is the single most useful exercise here.

**Scaption raises.** Holding light weights or nothing at all, raise your arms at a 30-degree angle from your sides (between a front raise and a side raise) up to shoulder height and back down. This works the muscles that stabilize your shoulder blade, which most people never train directly.

**Wall slides.** Stand with your back against a wall, arms bent in a goalpost shape, backs of your hands and elbows touching the wall. Slide your arms up overhead and back down while keeping contact with the wall. This builds overhead mobility without loading the joint.

**Prone Y-T-W.** Lying face down (on a bench or the edge of a bed works), lift your arms into a Y shape, then a T, then a W, pausing briefly in each position. This hits the smaller stabilizing muscles around the shoulder blade that a standard warm-up skips entirely.

Two sets of each, two or three times a week, is enough to notice a difference within a few weeks. This pairs naturally with [your regular pre-session warm-up](/blog/warm-up-routine-for-adult-skaters/) rather than replacing it — think of prehab as the longer-term conditioning work and the warm-up as the day-of activation.

## Falling in a way that protects the joint

Technique matters as much as strength here. [Learning to fall safely](/blog/how-to-fall-safely-after-40/) generally means tucking and rolling rather than bracing with a rigid, locked-out arm — the same principle that protects your wrist protects your shoulder, because a stiff, outstretched arm transmits force straight up the chain instead of dissipating it. If you already wear [wrist guards](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/), good instinct — just know they protect the wrist specifically, not the shoulder above it, so the falling technique itself is still doing most of the work for this joint.

## When it needs more than rest

Most minor shoulder strains settle with rest, ice for the first day or two, and an easy return to the exercises above once the sharp pain has eased. But get it looked at the same day if you can't lift your arm away from your body at all, there's visible deformity or an obvious bump at the collarbone or shoulder, or you have numbness or tingling running down the arm — that combination points to a fracture, dislocation, or nerve involvement rather than a simple strain. Pain that's still sharp after a week of rest, or a shoulder that keeps catching, clicking, or feeling unstable during ordinary movement, is worth a physiotherapy or sports medicine visit even without those red flags.

Treat your shoulders the way you've learned to treat every other joint skating asks something of: a little unglamorous maintenance now is cheaper than the alternative later.
