A patient of mine, 46 and three months into her comeback, told me she had stopped wearing her wrist guards because they kept slipping sideways on impact instead of staying put. That is a fit problem, and it happens often because most protective gear on the market was designed, sized, and tested around men's hand, wrist, and knee proportions.

The protective benefit of a wrist guard or knee pad comes from a rigid or semi-rigid shell staying in the exact position it was designed to protect. A guard that shifts, gaps, or pinches stops doing its job at the moment you need it.

## Why proportion matters as much as size

Scaling a men's design down to a smaller size does not solve the underlying proportion gap. Women's hands and wrists are, on average, proportioned differently relative to overall size, generally narrower across the palm relative to length, with a different wrist circumference-to-palm ratio. A [wrist guard](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/) sized purely off overall hand length can still seat poorly across the palm, letting the splint rotate out of position on impact instead of spreading the load the way it is meant to.

The same logic applies lower down. Knee pads and [hip protection](/blog/impact-shorts-hip-protectors-for-adult-skaters/) are built around hip-to-knee proportions and thigh circumference, both of which differ on average between men's and women's bodies. A knee pad that is the right length can still be the wrong shape, leaving a gap at the side of the knee or bunching behind it right where you do not want slack or pressure during a fall.

Standard unisex gear still fits plenty of women well. Bodies vary widely within any group, and many riders find a standard men's or unisex guard that locks in cleanly. The practical rule is that fit needs to be checked directly against your own measurements instead of assumed from a general size chart.

## What has changed in the gear market

The growth of women's skate communities in recent years, including groups like [Board Women](/blog/board-women-midlife-community/), [Oorbee Roy's Aunty Skates clinics](/blog/aunty-skates-oorbee-roy-profile/), London Skate Mums, and the Skate Mamas of SCV, has created enough collective demand that more brands now offer women's-specific cuts instead of treating "women's" as a repainted men's model in a smaller size. Powerslide, for example, sells a women's wrist guard line with a differently shaped shell and lining, separate from its standard unisex range.

The core safety standard most wrist, knee, and elbow guards are certified against, EN 14120 in Europe with comparable standards elsewhere, grades impact performance by body weight and by activity level (a lower Level 1 tier for ordinary recreational use and a higher Level 2 tier for more acrobatic use), not by sex. There is no separate women's safety threshold in the standard itself. What varies between a women's-labeled model and a standard one is typically the shell shape, sizing curve, and lining. Two guards at the same certification level and weight range, one women's-cut and one unisex, protect you equally once each fits properly; checking fit makes sure you get the protection either one is built to provide.

## How to check fit before you buy

### Measure both wrist and palm circumference
Wrap a soft tape measure around your wrist just below the knuckle of your thumb, and separately around the widest part of your palm (excluding the thumb) with your hand flat. Different brands size off different measurements, some by wrist circumference and some by palm or hand circumference, so check which measurement a specific model's chart uses before ordering or visiting a shop from our [skate shop directory](/directory/shops).

### Test the guard in motion
A guard that feels fine sitting still can still ride up or rotate once your wrist bends and your hand pushes off the ground. If you are buying in person, mimic a falling position with your hand flat and wrist extended back to see whether the splint stays centered in your palm.

### Check where a knee pad sits during a crouch
Stand in a skating stance and bend your knees the way you would absorbing a landing. The pad should stay centered over the kneecap through that motion without sliding down your shin or riding up your thigh.

### Use a retailer with a clear return policy
Protective equipment is worth trying in your living room before you take it to concrete, especially the first time you buy a brand or line you have not worn before. A guard you can exchange if the shell rotates is far more useful than a cheaper one that sits in your trunk.

### Check the size chart regardless of the label
Treat a women's-labeled model the same way you treat any other: check the measurements and the fit on your own joints.
