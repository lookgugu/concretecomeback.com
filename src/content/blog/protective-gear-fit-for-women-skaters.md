A patient of mine, 46 and three months into her comeback, told me she'd stopped wearing her wrist guards because they kept slipping sideways on impact instead of staying put. That's not a tolerance problem or a willpower problem. It's a fit problem, and it's more common than it should be, because most protective gear on the market was designed, sized, and tested around men's hand, wrist, and knee proportions.

This matters more than it sounds like it should. The protective benefit of a wrist guard or knee pad comes from a rigid or semi-rigid shell staying in the exact position it was designed to protect. A guard that shifts, gaps, or pinches doesn't just feel uncomfortable — it stops doing its job at the moment you actually need it.

## Why fit, not just size, is the real issue

Scaling a men's design down to a smaller size doesn't solve the underlying problem. Women's hands and wrists are, on average, proportioned differently relative to overall size — generally narrower across the palm relative to length, with a different wrist circumference-to-palm ratio. A wrist guard sized purely off overall hand length can still seat wrong across the palm, letting the splint rotate out of position on impact instead of spreading the load the way it's meant to.

The same logic applies lower down. Knee pads and impact shorts are built around hip-to-knee proportions and thigh circumference, both of which differ on average between men's and women's bodies. A knee pad that's the right length can still be the wrong shape, leaving a gap at the side of the knee or bunching uncomfortably behind it — exactly where you don't want slack or pressure during a fall.

None of this means standard "unisex" gear never fits a woman well. Bodies vary enormously within any group, and plenty of women find a standard men's or unisex guard that fits them fine. It means fit needs to be checked directly against your own measurements rather than assumed from a general size chart.

## What's actually changed

The growth of women's skate communities in the last few years — groups like London Skate Mums and the Skate Mamas of SCV, among others — has created enough collective demand that more brands now offer women's-specific cuts rather than treating "women's" as a repainted men's model in a smaller size. Powerslide, for example, sells a women's wrist guard line with a differently shaped shell and lining, separate from its standard unisex range. That's a genuine, relatively recent shift, not something that was widely available a decade ago.

It's worth being precise about what this does and doesn't mean. The core safety standard most wrist, knee, and elbow guards are certified against — EN 14120 in Europe, with comparable standards elsewhere — covers impact performance and is typically graded by body weight rather than by sex. There's no separate "women's" safety threshold in the standard itself. What varies between a women's-labeled model and a standard one is the shell shape, sizing curve, and lining — the fit, not the underlying protection requirement. A women's cut that fits you well and a unisex guard that fits you well protect you equally; the point is finding whichever one actually does.

## How to check fit before you buy

**Measure, don't guess.** Wrap a soft tape measure around your wrist just below the knuckle of your thumb, and separately around the widest part of your palm (excluding the thumb) with your hand flat. Different brands size off different measurements — some by wrist circumference, some by palm or hand circumference — so check which one a specific model's chart actually uses before ordering.

**Try the motion, not just the static fit.** A guard that feels fine sitting still can still ride up or rotate once your wrist bends and your hand grips a bar or pushes off the ground. If you're buying in person, mimic a falling motion — hand flat, wrist bent back — and see whether the splint stays put or shifts.

**Check where a knee pad actually sits during a crouch.** Stand in a skating stance and bend your knees the way you would absorbing a landing. The pad should stay centered over the kneecap through that motion, not slide down your shin or ride up your thigh.

**Buy from a retailer with a real return policy on gear.** Protective equipment is one category worth trying before you commit, especially the first time you're buying a brand or line you haven't worn before. A guard you can return if the fit is wrong is worth more than a marginally cheaper one you're stuck with.

**Don't assume "women's" automatically means "right for you."** Treat a women's-labeled model the same way you'd treat any other: check the actual size chart and the fit, not just the label. The goal is gear that stays exactly where it's supposed to be during a fall — whichever line gets you there.

## The part that actually matters

A wrist guard or knee pad that doesn't fit isn't a lesser version of protection — functionally, it's closer to no protection at all, because the mechanism that's supposed to spread load away from the joint only works if it stays in place. If gear has ever felt like it was working against you rather than for you, that's worth troubleshooting as a fit problem before you give up on wearing it.
