Returning to skateboarding after a total hip replacement, total knee replacement, or partial knee replacement requires individual clearance from your orthopedic surgeon and depends on how you plan to ride. Orthopedic consensus surveys generally discourage high-impact jumping, stair sets, and steep transition drops after joint arthroplasty because of implant wear and periprosthetic fracture risk from falls. For experienced former skaters who are well past full surgical rehabilitation and want to push and carve gently on flat, smooth pavement in full protective gear, surgeons evaluate clearance case by case based on implant fixation, bone quality, balance, and surgical approach precautions.

I hear some version of this question every few months from skaters who rode decades ago, had a hip or knee replaced in their 50s or 60s, and want to know whether stepping onto a deck is permanently off the table. [Our orthopedic checklist for skaters over 40](/blog/orthopedic-checklist-for-skaters-over-40/) and [what physical therapists think about skating after 50](/blog/what-physical-therapists-think-about-skating-after-50/) both flag a joint replacement as a reason to get your surgeon's sign-off before you start. That conversation differs from [managing arthritis in a native joint](/blog/skating-with-arthritis-or-an-old-injury/), because an artificial joint has its own mechanical rules.

## What a replaced joint is managing

A hip or knee replacement swaps worn articular surfaces for metal, ceramic, and polyethylene components fixed to your bone with cement or a porous press-fit surface designed for bone ingrowth. The procedure is reliable at relieving pain and restoring function for walking, stairs, and everyday life, while still operating as a mechanical implant with a finite service life.

In two 2019 systematic reviews and meta-analyses of national joint replacement registries in *The Lancet*, Jonathan Evans and colleagues found that roughly [57.9% of total hip replacements](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30782340/) and [82.3% of total knee replacements (and 69.8% of unicondylar partial knee replacements)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6381229/) remained in place without revision at 25 years. Because of that long-term horizon, orthopedic surgeons weigh two distinct risks when asked about sport: cumulative polyethylene wear or loosening over years of repetitive loading, and the immediate risk of a fall or sudden torque causing a dislocation or a fracture of the bone around the implant (a periprosthetic fracture).

## What orthopedic activity surveys show

Orthopedic activity guidance after knee and hip replacement is organized by impact and fall risk rather than by a single universal rulebook. Low-impact activities such as level walking, cycling, swimming, and golf are widely encouraged because they load the joint through a smooth, predictable arc without sharp vertical ground-reaction spikes.

When the Hip Society and the American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons surveyed their members ([Klein and colleagues, *Journal of Arthroplasty*, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17275629/); [Swanson and colleagues, *Journal of Arthroplasty*, 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19698910/)), more than 95% placed no limits on level walking, stair climbing, stationary or level cycling, swimming, or golf. Higher-impact or high-fall-risk sports were more often discouraged, though recommendations varied across surgeons, with hip replacements often given slightly broader latitude than knee replacements and respondents noting that clinical recommendations relied primarily on surgeon consensus rather than randomized trials.

Skateboarding does not sit in a single bucket because riding styles differ sharply in joint load. Cruising a flat bike path at jogging speed places a very different demand on a knee or hip than dropping into a bowl or jumping down steps. That difference is why your conversation with your surgeon needs to describe the exact movement you have in mind.

## Questions to bring to your surgeon

### Describe the exact terrain and speed you mean
Instead of asking a yes-or-no question about "skateboarding" in the abstract, describe the specific activity you want to do: flatground pushing, gentle carving on smooth pavement, and staying at a pace where you can step off at a walk, with no jumps, stairs, or steep ramps.

### Ask about impact loading and rotational torque
Pushing a skateboard loads the stance leg in single-leg knee and hip flexion while the pushing foot contacts the ground repeatedly. Ask how your specific implant design, bearing surface, and bone fixation tolerate that repetitive single-leg loading and mild rotational pivoting.

### Check surgical approach precautions for hip replacements
After a posterior-approach total hip replacement, classic movement precautions (avoiding hip flexion past 90 degrees, crossing the leg past the midline, or excessive internal rotation) are routinely prescribed for the first six to twelve weeks and sometimes longer. Anterior-approach procedures carry different extension and external-rotation considerations. Because a deep skating crouch or an awkward recovery step can approach those angles, ask your surgeon whether any lifelong range-of-motion limits apply to your hip.

### Confirm your rehabilitation milestones first
Surgeons generally expect you to be well past the initial healing window, typically many months post-operation, with symmetrical single-leg strength, full pain-free range of motion, and steady single-leg balance (see our [balance training guide for skaters over 40](/blog/skateboarding-balance-training-over-40/)) before considering any board sport.

## If your surgeon clears you for flatground riding

### Rebuild from basic flatground mechanics
Start from stationary foot placement, gentle pushing, and controlled foot-braking on smooth, level pavement following [the 8-week return plan for older skaters](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/), regardless of what you skated thirty years ago.

### Minimize surface vibration and instability
Use a wide, stable deck with a generous wheelbase and soft cruiser wheels (78A to 87A) paired with supportive cupsole shoes (covered in our [gear guide for aging joints](/blog/gear-for-aging-joints/) and our [retiree equipment guide](/blog/skateboarding-equipment-for-retirees-over-60/)) to cut down high-frequency road chatter through the implant.

### Treat fall prevention as non-negotiable
A hard fall onto a replaced hip or knee carries a serious risk of periprosthetic fracture or implant loosening. Wear a certified helmet, rigid-splint wrist guards, knee pads, and [impact shorts with hip shells](/blog/impact-shorts-hip-protectors-for-adult-skaters/), recognizing that padded shorts help spread surface impact but cannot guarantee protection against a fracture around an implant. Keep your sessions strictly within the flat, predictable terrain you discussed with your surgeon, such as a smooth park path or a quiet morning flatground apron from our [skatepark directory](/directory/parks), and stop for medical re-evaluation if you notice any new pain, swelling, clicking, or instability in the joint.
