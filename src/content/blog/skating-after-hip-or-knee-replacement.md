I get some version of this question every few months, usually from someone who skated decades ago, had a hip or knee replaced in their 50s or 60s, and is standing in my clinic wondering if the sport is permanently off the table now. [The site's guide to skating with arthritis or an old injury](/blog/skating-with-arthritis-or-an-old-injury/) touches on joint replacement briefly, but the question deserves its own honest answer, because it's not the same conversation as managing arthritis in a joint you still have.

## Start with what a replaced joint actually is

A hip or knee replacement swaps the worn joint surfaces for metal and plastic components, fixed to your bone with cement or a press-fit designed to let bone grow into it over time. It's an extraordinarily successful procedure at relieving pain and restoring function for ordinary life — walking, stairs, most daily activity. What it isn't is a return to a joint with zero limitations. The implant is a mechanical device with a service life, and the way orthopedic surgeons think about "what's okay to do" is shaped by two separate concerns: wear on the components over years of use, and the risk of a fall or a sudden force dislodging or damaging something that's otherwise working well.

## What the general guidance actually says

Orthopedic activity guidance after knee and hip replacement is usually organized by impact level rather than by naming specific sports. Low-impact activities — walking, cycling, swimming, golf — are broadly encouraged, because they load the joint through a smooth, predictable range without repetitive impact. Higher-impact activities that involve jumping, running, or sudden pivoting are the ones surgeons are more cautious about, mainly because of long-term wear on the implant and, for some activities, fall risk. Where a surgeon lands on any specific activity varies more than patients expect — this is genuinely an area where two good surgeons can give different answers to the same question, particularly for someone who did the activity regularly before surgery and wants to return to it at a modest, controlled level rather than push it hard.

Skateboarding doesn't fit neatly into that framework, because it isn't one thing. Cruising a smooth path at a comfortable pace is a very different load than dropping into a bowl or riding stairs. That distinction — which the framework is built around even when skating itself isn't named in it — is exactly what makes this a conversation worth having specifically, rather than a yes/no question with a generic answer.

## The conversation to actually have

Don't ask your surgeon "can I skateboard" and accept whatever reflexive answer comes back — most surgeons haven't been asked that question often enough to have a considered position on it. Instead, describe what you actually want to do, the way [the arthritis guide](/blog/skating-with-arthritis-or-an-old-injury/) recommends for any joint conversation: pushing, carving, and cruising on smooth pavement, not stair sets or vert ramps. Ask specifically about impact loading, about any rotational or pivoting precautions that apply to your particular implant and surgical approach, and about how far out from surgery you'd need to be before even the low-impact version is reasonable. For a hip in particular, ask directly about any movement precautions from your specific surgical approach — some hip replacement techniques carry restrictions on certain hip positions, especially in the first few months, and skateboarding's stance and crouch can intersect with those in ways worth checking rather than guessing at.

Timelines vary by procedure and by person, but most surgeons want to see you well past the initial recovery window — generally several months out, often longer — with good strength, full range of motion, and confident balance before adding anything beyond walking and low-impact cardio. Rushing this isn't a shortcut; a setback early in recovery costs you far more time than the extra weeks of patience would have.

## If you get a green light

Assume you're starting over on technique, not picking up where you left off. Rebuild pushing and balance on flat, smooth ground exactly the way [the 8-week return plan](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/) lays out for any returning adult, regardless of how advanced you were decades ago. Favor the softest wheels that still roll well — vibration through a replaced joint is worth minimizing even more than for a joint that's merely aging — and treat falls as a genuinely bigger deal than they were before, since a hard fall onto a replaced hip or knee is a different risk than the same fall onto a natural one. Build your session around flow and carving rather than anything involving drops, stairs, or transition with real height, and check in with your surgeon or physio again if anything about the joint feels different than expected once you're actually loading it regularly.

## What this isn't

This isn't permission to skip the conversation, and it isn't a reason to assume the answer is automatically no. Plenty of people with joint replacements return to genuinely active lives, including some recreational activities their surgeon would have hesitated to volunteer unprompted. The honest answer for most people is: probably yes, at a deliberately adapted version of what you used to do, once you're cleared and you build back in gradually — not a blanket no, and not a free pass to jump back in at the level you left off.
