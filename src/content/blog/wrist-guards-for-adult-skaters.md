In clinic, the wrist is the joint I see most often after a first skating fall — usually a few weeks into someone's comeback, usually from a low-speed tumble that should have been nothing. It's rarely the trick that gets people. It's the reflex: you feel yourself going, and your hands shoot out to catch you, wrist bent back, full body weight landing straight through it.

That reflex is exactly what wrist guards exist to interrupt, and there's real research behind why that matters, not just gear-shop advice.

## The instinct that gets adults hurt

Kids fall forward onto their hands too, but they're lighter, closer to the ground, and their bones bend more before they break. Adults are heavier, falling from a taller stance, with bones that are less forgiving. The exact same reflex — hands out, wrist cocked back to absorb the impact — produces a very different outcome once you're past 40.

The wrist is built to bend backward a certain amount before something gives. A hard fall onto an open hand routinely pushes it well past that point, which is why a broken wrist is one of the most common injuries in adult beginners and returners specifically, more than in teenagers doing the same falls.

## What the research actually found

The number worth knowing comes from a study published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1996, looking at in-line skaters rather than skateboarders specifically. Researchers found that skaters who weren't wearing wrist guards had roughly ten times the odds of a wrist injury compared to skaters who were — an adjusted odds ratio of 10.4.

It's worth being precise about what that study covers: it's in-line skating, not skateboarding, and it's now three decades old. Nobody has run the equivalent trial on skateboarders specifically. But the injury mechanism the guard is designed to interrupt — a forward or sideways fall onto an outstretched hand — is the same mechanism in both sports, and a separate biomechanical study on the same population found that wrist guards meaningfully increased the force needed to cause a fracture, on the order of 50%, with less severe breaks when a fracture did occur. Between the epidemiological finding and the biomechanical one, the case for wearing them is stronger than for almost any other piece of skate gear besides a helmet.

## Why a rigid splint is the part that matters

Not everything sold as a "wrist guard" does this job. A padded glove without a splint protects your skin from road rash and does very little for the bone underneath.

The part doing the actual work is a rigid splint — plastic or composite — running along the palm and sometimes the back of the hand, that physically stops your wrist from bending past a safe angle when you land on it. That's the entire mechanism: not cushioning the impact, but preventing the joint from reaching the position where it breaks.

Look for a guard with a splint on the palm side at minimum, ideally with some structure on the back of the wrist too, and a snug-but-not-restrictive strap system that keeps the splint in the right place through the fall rather than sliding around your forearm.

## The catch: they change how you fall

Wrist guards work, but only if you fall onto them correctly, and that takes some relearning even for experienced skaters.

The instinct they're fighting isn't just "hands out" — it's "grip and catch," fingers spread and gripping at whatever's underneath them. With a rigid splint on, that grip reflex works against you: you want to land flat on the guard and let the plastic take the load, not try to grab the ground through it. Most people who say wrist guards "don't work" or "feel worse" haven't adjusted this part yet. It's worth practicing a few soft, controlled falls onto grass or a mat specifically to feel what a guard-first landing is supposed to feel like, ideally with an experienced skater or coach walking you through it the first time.

## What to actually buy

You don't need to overthink this. A guard with a firm splint, a comfortable strap system, and a fit that doesn't slide is the whole spec. Sizing runs by wrist circumference on most brands — measure yours rather than guessing, since a guard that's too loose lets the splint shift out of position exactly when you need it.

Expect a solid pair to run somewhere in the $25–45 range. That's a small cost against six to eight weeks in a cast, which is the realistic recovery time for a broken wrist in your 40s or 50s — often longer than it would have taken you at 16.

## Wear them every session, not just the hard ones

The pattern I see in clinic isn't skaters who get hurt attempting something ambitious without guards on. It's skaters who got hurt on an ordinary push, an ordinary day, because they'd left the guards in the car for "just a quick cruise." The fall that breaks a wrist rarely announces itself in advance. The guards only do their job if they're already on when it happens.
