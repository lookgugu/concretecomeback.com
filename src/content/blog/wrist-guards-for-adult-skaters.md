In clinic, the wrist is the joint I see most often after a first skating fall — usually a few weeks into someone's comeback, usually from a low-speed tumble that should have been nothing. It's rarely the trick that gets people. It's the reflex: you feel yourself going, a hand shoots toward the ground, and body weight lands straight through a joint that isn't built to take it.

Wrist guards exist to interrupt exactly that outcome, and there's real research behind why they matter — not just gear-shop advice.

## The mechanism, briefly

[How to fall without breaking your wrist](/blog/how-to-fall-without-breaking-your-wrist/) covers the falling mechanics in full — hands away, chin tucked, rolling across the fleshy parts of your body rather than stopping on a joint. That's still the first and best defense against a wrist injury, guards or not, and nothing here changes that advice.

This post is about the second line: the falls where a hand ends up on the ground anyway, despite good technique, and the actual research behind why a guard matters in that moment.

## What the research actually found

The number worth knowing comes from Schieber et al., "Risk Factors for Injuries from In-Line Skating and the Effectiveness of Safety Gear," published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1996 — a study of in-line skaters, not skateboarders. Researchers found that skaters who weren't wearing wrist guards had roughly ten times the odds of a wrist injury compared to skaters who were — an adjusted odds ratio of 10.4.

It's worth being precise about what that study covers: it's in-line skating, not skateboarding, and it's three decades old. Nobody has run the equivalent trial on skateboarders specifically. The injury mechanism the guard is designed to interrupt — a fall onto an outstretched hand — is the same mechanism in both sports, but the lab evidence on exactly how much a rigid guard changes the physics of that fall is messier than the real-world number above. Cadaveric forearm testing on guarded versus unguarded wrists has produced mixed results across different studies, guard designs, and impact speeds. The most widely cited null result — Giacobetti et al., *American Journal of Sports Medicine*, 1997 — loaded 20 pairs of cadaver forearms to failure and found no statistically significant difference in the force it took to fracture a guarded wrist versus a bare one. Two other cadaver studies in the same journal found a benefit, just not the one you might expect: Moore et al., 1997, saw fewer carpal fractures and ligament injuries in the braced wrists even though the radius still broke, and Staebler et al., 1999, measured lower bone strain at the distal radius and more energy absorbed before fracture with two commercially available guards. The population-level finding is the one worth hanging your hat on here; the lab work mostly explains why the protection isn't absolute — a guard changes how a wrist fails more reliably than whether it fails — not a reason to doubt that it helps.

## Why a rigid splint is the part that matters

Not everything sold as a "wrist guard" does this job. A padded glove without a splint protects your skin from road rash and does very little for the bone underneath.

The part doing the actual work is a rigid splint — plastic or composite — running along the palm and sometimes the back of the hand, that limits how far your wrist can bend backward when it lands and helps spread the load away from the joint. That's the main mechanism, and it isn't a guarantee: a forceful enough fall can still fracture a guarded wrist, as the mixed lab results above show. It's risk reduction, not immunity — which is exactly why the falling technique above still matters even with guards on.

## Guards and your falling technique

Wrist guards don't replace the tuck-and-roll technique in the guide linked above. Hands away from the ground, chin tucked, rolling rather than stopping — that's still the goal on every fall, splint or no splint.

What guards change is narrower. On the falls where a hand does make contact despite good technique — and even experienced skaters have some of these — the instinct to fight is the grip reflex: fingers spread, clawing at whatever's underneath them. A hand that lands and slides lets the splint do its job. A hand that claws or grips at the ground the way it would bare-handed can still twist the wrist against the edge of the splint rather than being stopped by it. The guard is there for the moment your technique runs out, not a substitute for the technique itself, and not something to deliberately train for by putting a hand down on purpose — the tuck-and-roll practice in the guide above, which keeps hands away from the ground altogether, is still the only falling drill worth rehearsing on purpose.

## What to actually buy

This site's [protective gear guide](/blog/safety-gear-guide/) covers specific brands and the full buying criteria. The one addition worth calling out here: guards don't all size the same way — some go by wrist circumference, others by hand or palm circumference around the knuckles — so measure against the specific model's chart rather than assuming, since a guard sized off the wrong measurement lets the splint shift out of position exactly when you need it. Expect to pay roughly $25–45 for a basic pair with a proper rigid splint, and more for the leather glove-style guards that guide also recommends — small money either way against six to eight weeks in a cast, which is the realistic recovery time for a broken wrist in your 40s or 50s, often longer than it would have taken at 16.

## The part that's entirely up to you

The study behind this is decades old and about a different sport, but the mechanism it measured is the one skateboarding shares: a fall onto an outstretched hand, and a rigid splint standing between that hand and a break. The pattern I see in clinic isn't skaters hurt attempting something ambitious without guards on — it's skaters hurt on an ordinary push, on an ordinary day, because the guards were in the car for "just a quick cruise." Guards only do their job if they're already on when the fall happens, and that's the one part of this that's decided before you push off, not during the fall itself.
