In August 2024, at the Paris Olympics, a 51-year-old skateboarder dropped into the Parc Urbain bowl and got a standing ovation from Tony Hawk. Andy Macdonald finished 18th overall in the preliminary round — one of 22 skaters split across several heats — and missed the cut for the eight-skater final. It didn't matter. He'd already made the point he'd spent two decades campaigning to make: skateboarding at the Olympic level doesn't have to belong to teenagers alone.

If you're 45 or 55 and quietly wondering whether you've aged out of a sport you loved, or one you're only now discovering, Macdonald's story is worth more than a highlight reel. It's a working example of what actually gets someone that far past the age most people assume is a hard ceiling.

## The résumé, briefly

Macdonald picked up a board at 12, in 1986, and turned pro in 1994. Over a career that's now spanned four decades, he's collected 23 X Games medals — eight of them gold, mostly in vert and vert doubles between 1996 and 2002 — and holds the record for the most X Games medals won in vert skateboarding. He's also won eight World Cup Skateboarding overall championships. He was, by any measure, one of the sport's defining competitors well before anyone was talking about his age.

Then, in 2024, more than 20 years after he first started lobbying for skateboarding's inclusion in the Games, he qualified for Paris — and became the oldest skateboarder ever to compete at an Olympics. Tony Hawk, 56 at the time and Macdonald's longtime friend and competitor, was in the stands cheering him on. When Macdonald closed his run with a backflip, the crowd gave him standing ovations every time he dropped in.

## He doesn't train the way you'd expect

Here's the part that surprised me when I first read about him: Macdonald doesn't run a formal training program. No structured weight room routine, no periodized off-board conditioning block. By his own account, his team has tried to get him into more conventional training, and it hasn't stuck — he skates instead, and treats skating itself as the training.

That's not a universal prescription — Macdonald has forty years of accumulated skill and conditioning most of us don't have, and this site has covered [strength training](/blog/strength-training-muscles-adult-skaters-neglect/) and [prehab](/blog/prehab-for-adult-skaters/) for good reason if you're earlier in your comeback. But it's a useful data point against the assumption that staying competitive at 50-plus requires an increasingly elaborate support structure. Sometimes the answer is closer to: keep doing the thing, consistently, for decades.

What he is candid about is the cost. Asked directly about the physical side of competing at his age, his answer was simple: when he falls, it takes longer to get up, and longer to heal. He's not pretending the body doesn't notice the years. He's just decided the trade is worth it.

## Clean living as the actual longevity secret

If there's one factor Macdonald points to more than any training regimen, it's a lifestyle he's kept consistent since turning pro: no drinking, no smoking, no partying. That's not a dramatic story, which might be exactly why it doesn't get repeated as often as it should. A career built on decades of sustained physical performance rewards boring, consistent choices more than any single training hack — the same argument this site has made about [consistency compounding longer than intensity does](/blog/judi-oyama-oldest-competitive-skateboarder/) in the context of Judi Oyama's slalom career.

You don't need to have Macdonald's genetics or his professional stakes for that lesson to transfer. The unglamorous, repeatable habits — sleep, moderation, actually showing up — do more for how your body holds up at 50 than most of what gets marketed as recovery.

## Mentally, he's just not acting his age

Macdonald has said he skates with teenagers all the time and feels right there with them mentally — he's not approaching the bowl like a 51-year-old doing something remarkable for his age, he's approaching it like a skater who happens to have been doing this for a very long time. Asked about his shot at Paris, his response wasn't about redemption or proving a point: "If they're giving away gold medals for whoever has the most fun, I got it wrapped up."

That framing matters more than it sounds like it should. A lot of returning adults quietly treat their own comeback as something to apologize for — a middle-aged person doing a young person's sport, tolerated rather than belonging. Macdonald competed at an Olympic Games with exactly the opposite posture, and the sport's biggest name in the stands stood up for it.

## What actually transfers to your comeback

You're very unlikely to skate an Olympic bowl. That's fine — almost nobody reading this is trying to. What's useful isn't the competitive result. It's the pattern underneath it:

**The skill compounds longer than you'd think.** Forty years of accumulated technique doesn't evaporate at 50; it's the reason a 51-year-old could hang with skaters half his age at the sport's highest level.

**The body needs more recovery time, not a different sport.** Macdonald's own words — falls take longer to heal now — aren't a warning to stop. They're a reason to [respect recovery](/blog/recovery-guide-for-adult-skaters/) and [manage joints deliberately](/blog/in-session-joint-management-guide/) rather than skate through it the way you might have at 20.

**The boring habits are the real edge.** No single supplement, gadget, or program substitutes for decades of consistent, moderate choices.

**Belonging isn't something you have to earn back.** Macdonald didn't shrink himself to fit a younger sport. He showed up as himself, at 51, and the sport made room.

If you're a few months or a few years into your own comeback, wondering whether you're too old for this, Macdonald's answer is already on record, delivered from an Olympic bowl in front of a standing crowd: age is the story people tell about you. It doesn't have to be the one you tell about yourself.
