I see a small, predictable spike in adult skaters coming through my clinic every autumn, and it isn't because people are skating more recklessly. It's because the same park that felt completely manageable in July starts behaving differently in October, and most people don't adjust for it until after something's already gone wrong. Autumn doesn't just mean cooler weather. It changes traction, light, and even how your wheels grip, often all at once.

Here's what actually changes, and what to do about each one.

## Wet leaves are not a minor inconvenience

A leaf on wet pavement isn't just slippery the way wet pavement is slippery — it's slippery the way ice is slippery. The physics is well documented in road-safety research: a wet leaf develops a thin water film over its waxy surface, similar to what forms on ice, and because the leaf itself isn't bonded to the ground the way ice is, it can slide against both the pavement and whatever's rolling over it, adding a second sliding surface on top of the first. Road-safety demonstrations put a car's stopping distance on wet leaves at roughly three times what it is on dry pavement — and noticeably longer than on plain wet pavement.

None of that research was done on skateboard wheels, but the underlying mechanism doesn't care what's rolling over it. A small, hard wheel making brief contact with a wet leaf has even less surface area to find grip than a car tire does. Practically, that means:

- **Treat any leaf-covered patch as you would visible ice** — go around it, not over it, even if it looks thin or dry-ish from a distance.
- **Damp, shaded corners hold leaf litter longest.** The parts of a park under trees or against a north-facing wall are worth a second look even after the rest of the surface looks clear.
- **Wet leaves get ground into a paste on concrete** that can stay slick for days after the leaves themselves have blown away or been swept. A clean-looking surface after a wet week is worth testing at low speed before committing to it at your normal one.

## Daylight is disappearing faster than you think

Around the September equinox, mid-latitude cities lose roughly two to three minutes of daylight every single day, and closer to four the further north you are. The session that started in full sun at 5:30 in July is now finishing in genuine dusk at the same clock time. The problem isn't romantic golden-hour lighting — it's the specific loss of depth perception and contrast that comes right before full dark, which is exactly when it's hardest to judge a crack, a lip, or another skater's speed and distance.

- **Check sunset time before you go, not after you're already at the park.** A quick look at your phone's weather app takes ten seconds and tells you exactly how much usable light you have left.
- **Build in a buffer.** Plan to be packing up 15–20 minutes before sunset rather than treating sunset as your hard stop — the last stretch of usable light degrades faster than people expect.
- **If a park has lighting, learn its actual coverage**, not just whether it has lights at all. Many "lit" parks only light the main bowl or street course, leaving entry ramps and edges dim. [The night-skating guide](/blog/night-skating-after-40/) covers how to scout a park's lighting gaps and what visibility gear is worth carrying.

## Cold changes how your wheels grip

Wheel hardness is rated for a reason, and most setups this site recommends for older skaters ([78a–87a, per the wheel-hardness guide](/blog/wheel-hardness-guide-for-adult-skaters/)) are chosen for their grip and shock absorption at typical riding temperatures. As pavement temperature drops through autumn, urethane wheels genuinely get harder and less compliant — the same wheel that felt grippy and forgiving in September warmth can feel noticeably skatier and less predictable on a cold October morning, particularly over the first few minutes before you've built any heat into them.

- **Give yourself a longer warm-up push before attempting anything you'd normally do without thinking.** A few extra minutes of pushing and carving lets you feel out how the wheels are actually gripping today, not how you remember them gripping last week.
- **Be more conservative on cold mornings specifically** — the first session of a cold day is when the mismatch between expected grip and actual grip is largest, before your body and your wheels have both had a chance to adjust.
- **If you're skating year-round into genuinely cold weather, this is worth revisiting again in winter** — the effect only becomes more pronounced as temperatures keep dropping. [The year-round skating guide](/blog/year-round-skating-guide-for-adults/) covers ice on covered concrete, longer winter warm-ups, and how to keep your balance through the off-season.

## The pattern underneath all three

Every one of these hazards has the same shape: something about the park changed since your last visit, and the visible surface doesn't necessarily show it. Wet leaves can look dry. Dusk can look like plenty of light. Cold wheels look identical to warm ones. That's exactly why autumn catches returning adult skaters off guard more than any other season — the risk isn't dramatic or obvious, it's a small mismatch between what your body expects and what the park is actually giving you.

The fix isn't to skate less through autumn. It's to spend the first few minutes of every autumn session actively checking, rather than assuming the conditions match what you remember from summer. A short, deliberate warm-up lap around the whole park — checking for damp patches, testing your wheels' grip, and clocking how much daylight you actually have left — on top of [the usual ten-minute warm-up](/blog/warm-up-routine-for-adult-skaters/), costs you almost nothing and catches most of what autumn is going to throw at you before it costs you something bigger.

## A note for the Southern Hemisphere

Everything above is written from the Northern Hemisphere, where September and October are autumn. If you're in Australia, New Zealand, or anywhere south of the equator, this is your spring, not your fall: daylight is lengthening rather than shrinking, and deciduous leaf-drop isn't a September event the way it is up north. Don't apply these specific months to your own skating. Do apply the underlying pattern six months from now, in your own autumn (April–May): watch for wet leaf litter in shaded, damp corners — which, south of the equator, tend to be south-facing rather than north-facing — check your local sunset time as the days shorten, and give cold wheels a longer warm-up as temperatures drop.
