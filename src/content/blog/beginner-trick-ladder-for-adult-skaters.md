The 8-week return plan on this site covers the shape of a comeback — flat ground, then gentle terrain, then real park sessions. What it doesn't do is answer a question I get constantly: once I can push, stop, and carve, what do I actually learn first? Second? What order makes sense, and how long should each one realistically take?

Most trick progressions online are written by and for teenagers who bounce back from falls in a way our bodies don't, and who have three unstructured hours a day to throw at a single trick. This is the same ladder, adjusted for adult schedules, adult joints, and adult patience.

## Why order matters more than it seems to

Tricks aren't a random list — each one builds a specific physical capability the next one depends on. Skip the order and you end up trying to learn a trick your body isn't ready to support, which is where most avoidable injuries happen. The ladder below is sequenced so each rung uses something the previous one taught you.

Before Rung 1, treat basic falling practice as done, not optional — tucking, rolling, and protecting your wrists and head, as covered in this site's falling guide — along with a helmet and wrist guards. Kick turns and manuals produce backward and sideways falls of their own, well before you reach the drop-in rung where gear gets a second, more detailed mention.

## Rung 1: Kick turns (both directions)

If you've done the flat-ground basics — push, stop, carve — the kick turn is the natural next step. It's the skill of pivoting the board on its back wheels while standing still or moving slowly, and it's foundational for almost everything that follows: changing direction on a ramp, adjusting your line quickly, and eventually dropping in.

**Realistic timeline:** most adults get a serviceable kick turn on flat ground within one to two weeks of regular short sessions. The frontside/backside direction you don't lead with will lag behind — that's normal, and worth deliberately practicing rather than favoring your stronger side.

## Rung 2: Tic-tacs

Tic-tacs are a string of small kick turns used to generate speed or adjust direction without pushing. They matter less as a standalone trick and more because they build the specific hip and shoulder rotation, plus the trust in briefly balancing on two wheels, that shows up again in almost every trick further up the ladder.

**Realistic timeline:** a week or two once kick turns feel automatic. Don't chase speed here — the point is the rotation pattern, not how fast you can move.

## Rung 3: Manuals

A manual — balancing on the back wheels while rolling, without pivoting — is where a lot of adults first feel genuinely wobbly again, since it isolates balance in a way flat pushing doesn't. It's also one of the more valuable skills to have, because manual balance underlies control on rougher terrain and quick recoveries when you catch an edge.

**Realistic timeline:** this is the first rung where adult timelines diverge meaningfully from teenage ones. Expect several weeks of inconsistent, wobbly attempts before you can hold one for more than a couple of seconds. That's not a sign you're doing it wrong — manuals take most beginners a while regardless of age.

## Rung 4: Drop-ins

Dropping into a quarter pipe or shallow bowl from the coping is less a trick than a threshold — it's the moment most returning adults associate with "really skating a park" rather than just rolling around its flat sections. It also tends to carry outsized fear relative to its actual difficulty, because it looks more committing than it is.

Don't treat this rung as a straight jump from flat-ground manuals. Before you stand on the coping, you need two things a manual doesn't teach: comfort riding transition (rolling in and out of the shallow end of a bowl or the bottom of a quarter pipe under your own push, without dropping in) and a practiced knee slide specifically — not the flat-ground roll-out bail, which is a different response to a different kind of fall. Spend a session or two deliberately bailing on purpose at low speed on transition, sliding out along the curve of the ramp on your knee pads, so that response is automatic before a real drop-in tests it. Gear matters more here than at any earlier rung. Look for a helmet rated to ASTM F1492 (the US standard for skateboarding and multiple-impact protection) or your region's equivalent multi-impact skate certification — that's the standard built for the repeated falls a drop-in habit produces, unlike CPSC/CE EN 1078/AS-NZS 2063, which only test a single hard impact (you'd need one of those too only if the same helmet doubles as your bike helmet). Regardless of certification, retire and replace any helmet after a real head impact rather than continuing to wear it. Pair that with wrist guards and full-cup knee pads (not the slim, low-profile kind) — together they're what let you slide out of a bad landing on transition instead of digging in.

Even with that groundwork, do this one with someone experienced nearby the first several times, on the smallest, mellowest transition you can find. There's no version of drop-ins that benefits from rushing or from an audience — and no version where a spotter substitutes for the transition riding, bail practice, and pads above.

**Realistic timeline:** the physical skill itself often clicks within a session or two once you commit; the mental barrier usually takes longer than the physical one. Give yourself permission to spend multiple sessions just standing at the top before you go.

## Rung 5: Ollies

The ollie — popping the board into the air without your feet leaving it — is the gateway to almost every flip and ledge trick that exists, which is why it's usually the first "real trick" most people chase. It's also genuinely harder to learn as an adult than most of what came before it, because it demands a quick, committed pop that hesitation actively works against.

**Realistic timeline:** be honest with yourself that this one can take months, not weeks, and that's fine. Short, frequent practice sessions (ten to fifteen minutes, several times a week) beat occasional long ones for this specific trick, because it's more about repetition count than session length.

## Rung 6: Basic grinds or rock-to-fakies

Once an ollie is reasonably consistent, most adults branch in one of two directions: ledge tricks (a basic 50-50 grind on a low curb or ledge) or transition tricks (a rock-to-fakie on a mini ramp, which uses the drop-in confidence you built earlier). Which one to pick isn't really a "which is easier" question — it's about which style of skating you've enjoyed more up to this point. Follow that instinct rather than a generic list.

**Realistic timeline:** highly variable, and genuinely optional. Some adults are perfectly happy stopping at ollies and manuals indefinitely, and that's a complete, satisfying version of skating — not an incomplete one.

## What this ladder isn't

It isn't a schedule with deadlines, and it isn't a claim that every adult needs to reach the top rung to count as a real skater. Plenty of returning adults get enormous satisfaction from rungs one through three and never chase an ollie at all. The value of the ladder is in the order and in having honest timelines to measure yourself against — not the ones written for teenagers with nothing else to do all summer — so you're not comparing your six-week-old manual to someone else's six-year-old one and calling it a failure.

Progress on this ladder is rarely linear. You'll nail a kick turn one session and feel like you've forgotten it the next. That's the actual texture of adult skill acquisition, not a sign the plan isn't working. Keep showing up in order, and the rungs above the one you're on will still be there when you're ready.
