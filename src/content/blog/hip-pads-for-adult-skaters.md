Ask a returning adult skater what protective gear they own, and you'll almost always hear the same list: helmet, wrist guards, maybe knee pads. It's the right list as far as it goes. But there's a gap in it that I see constantly in my physio practice, and it's the same gap I had myself for the first year back: nothing for the hip.

## Why the hip gets ignored

Part of it is habit. Wrist guards and helmets are the gear that gets talked about everywhere — they're the obvious answer to the two most common beginner falls, hands-out and head-first. Hip pads don't have the same visibility, partly because they're more associated with roller derby and downhill longboarding than with park and street skating, and partly because a fall onto your hip just doesn't feel as urgent a risk as a fall onto your wrist or skull.

The other part is comfort and vanity. Hip pads are bulkier under clothing than a knee pad or wrist guard, and a lot of adults — myself included, early on — quietly decide that looking a little odd at the park is worse than the small chance of needing the protection. That calculation gets revisited fast after the first hard hip landing.

## Why it matters more as you get older

Falls that land on the hip and the surrounding area are less common than wrist and knee impacts, but they carry a disproportionate cost when they do happen. A hard fall directly onto the hip, particularly onto concrete, can affect the same joint that becomes a major mobility concern later in life if it's seriously injured. A broken wrist is inconvenient for six to eight weeks. An injury to the hip joint or the bone around it, in a worse case, is a longer and more disruptive recovery — sometimes with lasting effects on mobility that a wrist fracture generally doesn't carry.

This isn't a reason for alarm. Direct hip impacts are a smaller share of skating falls than wrist and elbow impacts. It's a reason for proportionate protection, especially if you're skating transition — bowls, ramps, banks — where a slide-out or a fall from height is more likely to land you on your side rather than square on your hands.

## What actually exists

Hip protection for skating mostly comes in two forms, and both are worth knowing about rather than defaulting to the more visible option.

**Padded compression shorts.** These sit under your regular clothing and use foam or gel inserts over the hip bones and sometimes the tailbone. Brands that make skate- and derby-oriented versions, like Triple Eight and 187 Killer Pads, build these specifically to be worn discreetly under jeans or shorts rather than as visible external armor. This is the option most adult street and park skaters who do wear hip protection actually choose, precisely because it doesn't change how you look at the park.

**External hip pads.** Bulkier, harder-shelled protection worn over clothing, more common in downhill longboarding and roller derby, where impact speeds and fall angles are more severe. Overkill for most casual park sessions, but worth considering if you're specifically getting into downhill or you're recovering from a previous hip injury and want the extra margin while you rebuild confidence.

## Who should actually prioritize this

Not every adult skater needs hip pads on day one, and I wouldn't tell a beginner working on flat ground pushes and carves to prioritize this over a helmet and wrist guards, which matter more for the falls you're most likely to have early on.

Hip protection moves up the priority list specifically if:

- You're progressing into bowls, ramps, or any terrain where you're falling from height rather than from a standing push
- You've had a previous hip, pelvis, or lower back injury, from skating or anything else
- You have any existing bone density concerns — this is worth a conversation with your doctor, not something to self-diagnose, but it's a real reason to add protection rather than assume it doesn't apply to you
- You've already had one hard hip landing and noticed how long the resulting bruise and stiffness actually lasted

## Getting the fit right

The most common mistake with padded compression shorts is sizing them like regular underwear rather than like protective gear. You want the pads sitting directly over your hip bones with the shorts snug enough that they don't shift when you fall — a pad that's slid two inches out of position on impact is not protecting anything. Try them on and do a few practice falls onto a soft surface at home before trusting them at the park.

Expect a break-in period. Padded shorts feel bulky and slightly restrictive for the first few sessions. Most people stop noticing them within two or three sessions, the same adjustment period as new wrist guards or a new helmet.

## The gear list, updated

Helmet and wrist guards remain the non-negotiables for any returning adult skater, and knee pads are close behind for anyone skating anything beyond flat ground. Hip protection belongs on that list too, particularly once you're skating transition or you've got any history that makes a hard hip landing a bigger deal than it would otherwise be.

It's not the flashiest gear recommendation. It's also the one I wish someone had told me about before my first bad slide-out in a bowl, rather than after.
