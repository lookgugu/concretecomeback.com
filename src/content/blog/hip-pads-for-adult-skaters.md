Ask an adult returning skater what protective gear they've bought and you'll almost always hear the same three things: helmet, wrist guards, maybe knee pads. All correct. All worth buying. And all missing the one joint that takes the hardest hit when an adult skater falls the way adults actually fall — sideways, onto the hip.

I see this gap constantly in my physio practice. Patients will show up with wrist guards still in the packaging in their bag "for later" but have never even heard of a hip pad. It's not that hip protection is new. It's that nobody talks about it, so nobody buys it, so it stays a niche product most skaters don't know exists.

## Why the hip specifically

A sideways fall — the kind that happens when you catch an edge carving, or lose balance stepping off at the wrong moment — tends to land you on the meaty part of your hip, right over the bony point called the greater trochanter. At 20, that's a bruise. Past 40, and especially past 50, it's a different calculus: bone density has usually started declining, recovery from any fracture takes longer, and a hip injury has a well-documented tendency to cascade into a much longer disruption to your mobility and independence than a wrist or ankle injury of similar severity.

That's not a reason to be afraid of skating. It's a reason to treat the hip the way you already treat your head and your wrists: as a joint worth actively protecting rather than hoping to avoid landing on.

## What hip pads actually are

The category is usually sold as "impact shorts" or "hip protectors" — padded shorts, similar in cut to compression shorts, with foam inserts positioned directly over each hip and sometimes the tailbone. You wear them under your regular clothes, so from the outside you just look like you're skating in normal pants.

They don't prevent a fall. Nothing does, reliably, and any product that claims otherwise is overselling. What they do is disperse and absorb the impact energy when you do land on your hip, the same basic principle as a helmet for your skull or a wrist guard for your wrist — reducing the force that actually reaches the bone.

## What's improved

If your mental image of hip pads is the bulky, diaper-like protectors from decades of downhill and hockey gear, it's worth updating. The impact shorts sold for skating and other action sports today are built from thinner, more breathable foam than older designs, cut close enough to the body that they don't show under jeans or joggers, and light enough that most skaters forget they're wearing them within a session or two. That's the real barrier that's fallen away — not effectiveness, but the previous cost of looking and feeling like you're wearing armor to skate a flat parking lot.

## Who should actually wear them

I don't think every adult skater needs hip pads for every session. Where I do recommend them:

- **If you're relearning falling itself.** In your first few months back, before your reflexes for catching a fall have returned, you're more likely to go down in a way you don't control — which often means landing on whatever part of you happens to hit first, hip included.
- **If you skate bowls, ramps, or transitions.** These surfaces produce more sideways and backward falls than flat-ground street skating, where falls tend to be more forward and are more often caught by wrist guards.
- **If you have any reason to think your bone density is lower than average** — a prior fracture from a minor fall, a family history of osteoporosis, or a doctor who's already flagged it. This is a conversation worth having with your GP if you're not sure, not something to self-diagnose.

## How to choose a pair

Fit matters more than brand. Try them on under the pants you'll actually skate in — if they bunch, ride up, or feel restrictive at the hip crease when you crouch into a stance, you won't wear them past the first session. Look for a pair with pads that sit directly over the hip bone when you're standing in your normal skate stance, not just centered on the outer thigh. If you tend to fall backward onto your tailbone as often as sideways onto your hip, a version with a removable tailbone insert is worth the small extra cost.

## The honest pitch

Nobody skates better because they're wearing hip pads. You skate exactly the same. The only difference shows up on the one day you didn't plan for — the day you catch an edge you didn't see coming. That's the entire case for wearing any protective gear, and there's no good reason the hip should be the joint we leave out.
