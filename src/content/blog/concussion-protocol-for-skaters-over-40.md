Most adult skaters I talk to have a mental file for wrist and ankle injuries — what a bad one feels like, roughly how long it takes, when to worry. Almost none of them have the same file for a concussion, even though a head impact is the injury on this list most likely to actually change your life. Part of that is denial — a hard hit to the head is scarier to think about than a sprained wrist — and part of it is that most concussion advice online is written for teenage athletes, not a 45-year-old getting back into bowls, with return-to-play timelines and research that doesn't automatically transfer to a midlife body. That gap is worth its own guide.

## What's actually happening in a concussion

A concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by a rapid movement of the head — a direct hit, or a fall hard enough to snap the head forward or sideways even without striking it directly. The brain doesn't bruise the way a muscle does; the injury is more about disrupted chemical and electrical signaling between neurons, which is why a concussion can produce real symptoms — headache, fogginess, nausea, light sensitivity, balance problems — without anything showing up on a standard CT scan. "Normal imaging" doesn't mean "no injury."

## What the evidence on age and recovery actually shows

The honest picture is mixed, and worth stating plainly rather than oversimplifying either direction. A small 2015 brain-imaging study from Taipei Medical University, [published in *Radiology*](https://www.rsna.org/news/2015/october-november/older-patients-recover-more-slowly-from-concussion), the Radiological Society of North America's journal, compared 13 concussed patients aged 51–68 against 13 aged 21–30, scanning both once within the first month after injury and again six weeks after that first scan. The younger group showed a significant decline in symptoms like working-memory trouble between those two scans; the older group showed no such decline, and a different brain-activation pattern too — persistent under-activation where the younger brains had briefly over-activated and then settled. That's a real, specific signal — but it's drawn from only 13 older participants, so it's a lead, not a settled finding.

A larger body of evidence points the other way, with a caveat of its own. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* ([Putukian, Purcell, Schneider, and colleagues](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/12/798)) pooled the sport-related concussion literature and found that, once study-to-study variation and overlapping confidence intervals are accounted for, older and younger age cohorts had broadly similar recovery patterns — age by itself wasn't a strong predictor. The same review found your initial symptom burden, not your age, was the strongest predictor of how long recovery actually took. The caveat is that this is athlete research: its "older" cohorts are adult athletes, not a midlife population, so it speaks to the gap between a teenager and a young adult far more directly than to a 45-year-old.

Put plainly: there's no well-established rule that a 45-year-old's concussion recovers on a different timeline than a 25-year-old's. What there is, is genuine uncertainty, one small signal that older brains might compensate differently, and essentially no concussion research or return-to-play guidance written with a midlife recreational skater in mind. That combination is why "don't assume you get the fast youth-sports timeline just because nobody's specifically studied you" is the sensible default here — not because the science has proven adults recover slower, but because nobody can currently tell you that you won't, and pushing back into skating on an unverified fast timeline is exactly how a mostly-healed concussion turns into a second one.

## The red flags that mean the ER today, not a wait-and-see

Most concussions don't need an ambulance, but a specific set of signs does, because they can indicate an expanding bleed inside the skull rather than an ordinary concussion: repeated vomiting, a headache that keeps getting worse rather than easing, slurred speech, one pupil noticeably larger than the other, a seizure, increasing confusion or agitation, weakness or numbness in an arm or leg, or any period where you couldn't be woken up normally. Any loss of consciousness — actual or just suspected, even brief — or any gap in memory around the fall, including simply not being able to remember the impact itself, belongs in this same category: the [Amsterdam Consensus Statement](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/11/695) and [NICE head-injury guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng232/chapter/recommendations) both direct these straight to acute assessment, same as the signs above. [CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/signs-symptoms/index.html) is explicit that any of these means calling emergency services or getting to an emergency department right away — not waiting to get checked out later that day.

Short of those, still get evaluated within a day or two if you were hit hard enough to be dazed, confused, or just feeling "off," with full, clear memory of the fall and no question about whether you blacked out. The moment either of those is uncertain, treat it as the emergency category above rather than this one. "I probably don't need to go" is exactly the judgment a concussed brain is bad at making about itself — if someone else at the park thinks you should get checked, that's a reasonable tiebreaker.

## The protocol: why "I feel fine" isn't the test

Concussion management changed meaningfully with the 2023 Amsterdam Consensus Statement on Concussion in Sport — the update from the 6th International Conference on Concussion in Sport, published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* — which explicitly moved away from the old advice of complete rest. Current guidance calls for *relative* rest, not strict rest, during the first 24–48 hours — reduced screen time and mental exertion alongside your normal activities of daily living, not lying in a dark room doing nothing. Light activity is now recommended within that same window rather than avoided — a short walk, for instance — and a mild, brief flare from it (symptoms a couple of points worse on a 0–10 scale, settling back down within an hour) isn't a reason to stop; it's the version of "pushing through" this guidance actually allows.

From there, recovery moves through graded stages: light aerobic exercise, then moderate aerobic exercise, then sport-specific movement that carries no risk of a head impact — those three are where the same mild-and-brief tolerance from the first 48 hours still applies. Everything after that is a different tier: non-contact training drills, and then anything with real contact or fall risk (which for skating means essentially any normal session, since a fall is always possible), both require you to be back at your actual baseline first, with no flare tolerance, not just "manageable." Each stage typically takes at least 24 hours, and the full sequence takes at least a week even when everything goes smoothly — this isn't a same-day checklist, and feeling fine on one short walk doesn't mean you skip stages.

What this protocol doesn't hand you is the authority to clear yourself into that later tier. Current guidance is explicit that a healthcare professional — not the skater, and not a friend at the park — needs to sign off before you progress into non-contact training drills and anything beyond them, not just before the final return to skating. For an adult skater, that means an actual clearance from a doctor or a clinician experienced with concussion before you start running drills on a board, not just deciding on your own that you feel fine. "Let me just try a mellow session and see how it feels" is exactly the judgment this rule exists to take out of your hands, because a brain that's still recovering won't reliably warn you before you're compromised.

## Why a second concussion before the first one heals is the real danger

Taking a second head impact while still symptomatic from the first carries real risk of a longer, more complicated recovery than either injury would cause alone. The most severe documented outcomes from a second impact are concentrated in teenagers and young adults, not adults in their 40s and 50s, and the evidence for a distinct "second-impact syndrome" is itself debated — so this isn't a settled finding about what happens to an older brain specifically. What isn't in dispute is the basic logic: a brain that hasn't finished healing is less able to absorb another hit, at any age. This is the single best argument for being conservative about the return-to-skate timeline rather than optimistic about it — the cost of being wrong isn't a few extra weeks off the board, it's risking a materially worse injury.

## The gear side: helmet fit matters more than the sticker

A helmet doesn't prevent concussions outright — no helmet does, since they're built primarily to prevent skull fracture and reduce the force transmitted to the brain, not eliminate the rotational forces a glancing fall produces. But fit matters enormously: a helmet that sits too high, rocks side to side, or isn't buckled snugly loses a meaningful share of its protection before you've even fallen. Multi-directional impact protection system (MIPS) — a low-friction layer inside the helmet designed to let it rotate slightly on impact and reduce rotational force on the brain — is increasingly common in bike and multi-sport helmets, and it's worth looking for if you're replacing yours, alongside a proper skate-specific certification rather than a repurposed bike lid — [this site's protective gear guide](/blog/safety-gear-guide/) covers fit and certification in detail. [This site's piece on injury statistics](/blog/skateboarding-injury-statistics-for-adults/) has the fuller picture on what the evidence does and doesn't say about helmet protection specifically.

## The actual takeaway

A concussion isn't the injury to shrug off because the swelling looks manageable or the headache fades after a day. Give it the structured, staged return the way you would any serious injury, get immediate emergency care for the red-flag signs, get an actual clinical clearance before you return to anything with fall risk, and treat "when in doubt, sit it out" as the rule rather than the exception. Your wrist will heal roughly on schedule either way. The research on whether your brain's schedule is genuinely different after 40 is thin and mixed, not settled — and that uncertainty, not a proven slower timeline, is the honest reason not to assume the "back in a week or two" rule from youth sports applies to you.
