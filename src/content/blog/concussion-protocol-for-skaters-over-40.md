Most adult skaters I talk to have a mental file for wrist and ankle injuries — what a bad one feels like, roughly how long it takes, when to worry. Almost none of them have the same file for a concussion, even though a head impact is the injury on this list most likely to actually change your life. Part of that is denial — a hard hit to the head is scarier to think about than a sprained wrist — and part of it is that most concussion advice online is written for teenage athletes, not a 45-year-old getting back into bowls. The physiology is different enough at this age that it's worth its own guide.

## What's actually happening in a concussion

A concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by a rapid movement of the head — a direct hit, or a fall hard enough to snap the head forward or sideways even without striking it directly. The brain doesn't bruise the way a muscle does; the injury is more about disrupted chemical and electrical signaling between neurons, which is why a concussion can produce real symptoms — headache, fogginess, nausea, light sensitivity, balance problems — without anything showing up on a standard CT scan. "Normal imaging" doesn't mean "no injury."

## Why 40 and up changes the timeline

Younger athletes with concussions tend to improve steadily over the first few weeks. Older adults don't follow the same curve. A brain-imaging study presented by the Radiological Society of North America compared a group of concussed patients in their 50s and 60s against a group in their 20s, scanning both at four weeks and again at ten weeks post-injury. The younger group showed a clear drop in symptoms like working-memory trouble between those two check-ins. The older group didn't — their symptoms stayed roughly flat over that same stretch, and their brain activation patterns looked different too, suggesting a slower, more effortful compensation process rather than the faster recovery seen in the younger group.

That doesn't mean a concussion after 40 is permanent or catastrophic. It means the "I'll be back to normal in a week or two" assumption that circulates in youth sports doesn't transfer cleanly to this age group, and pushing back into skating on that timeline is exactly how a mostly-healed concussion turns into a second one.

## The red flags that mean the ER today, not a wait-and-see

Most concussions don't need an ambulance, but a specific set of signs does, because they can indicate an expanding bleed inside the skull rather than an ordinary concussion: repeated vomiting, a headache that keeps getting worse rather than easing, slurred speech, one pupil noticeably larger than the other, a seizure, increasing confusion or agitation, weakness or numbness in an arm or leg, or any period where you couldn't be woken up normally. Any loss of consciousness — actual or just suspected, even brief — or any gap in memory around the fall, including simply not being able to remember the impact itself, belongs in this same category: the [Amsterdam Consensus Statement](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/11/695) and [NICE head-injury guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng232/chapter/recommendations) both direct these straight to acute assessment, same as the signs above. [CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/signs-symptoms/index.html) is explicit that any of these means calling emergency services or getting to an emergency department right away — not waiting to get checked out later that day.

Short of those, still get evaluated within a day or two if you were hit hard enough to be dazed, confused, or just feeling "off," with full, clear memory of the fall and no question about whether you blacked out. The moment either of those is uncertain, treat it as the emergency category above rather than this one. "I probably don't need to go" is exactly the judgment a concussed brain is bad at making about itself — if someone else at the park thinks you should get checked, that's a reasonable tiebreaker.

## The protocol: why "I feel fine" isn't the test

Concussion management changed meaningfully with the 2023 Amsterdam Consensus Statement on Concussion in Sport — the update from the 6th International Conference on Concussion in Sport, published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* — which explicitly moved away from the old advice of complete rest. Current guidance limits strict rest to the first 24–48 hours, and even then it means reduced screen time and mental exertion, not lying in a dark room doing nothing. Light activity is now recommended within that same window rather than avoided — a short walk, for instance — and a mild, brief flare from it (symptoms a couple of points worse on a 0–10 scale, settling back down within an hour) isn't a reason to stop; it's the version of "pushing through" this guidance actually allows.

From there, recovery moves through graded stages: light aerobic exercise, then sport-specific movement with no contact or fall risk, then non-contact training drills, and only after that, anything with real contact or fall risk — which for skating means essentially any normal session, since a fall is always possible. Each stage typically takes at least 24 hours, and the full sequence takes at least a week even when everything goes smoothly — this isn't a same-day checklist, and feeling fine on one short walk doesn't mean you skip stages. The same mild-and-brief tolerance from the first 48 hours carries through these early, non-contact stages. What it doesn't carry into is the later stages: before anything with real contact or fall risk, you need to be back to your actual baseline, not just "manageable."

What this protocol doesn't hand you is the authority to clear yourself for that last stage. Current guidance is explicit that a healthcare professional — not the skater, and not a friend at the park — needs to sign off before you return to any activity with real contact or fall risk. For an adult skater, that means an actual clearance from a doctor or a clinician experienced with concussion before your first real session back, not just deciding on your own that you feel fine. "Let me just try a mellow session and see how it feels" is exactly the judgment this rule exists to take out of your hands, because a brain that's still recovering won't reliably warn you before you're compromised.

## Why a second concussion before the first one heals is the real danger

Taking a second head impact while still symptomatic from the first is the scenario that produces the worst outcomes in concussion research, with recovery times that stretch well past what either injury would cause alone. This is the single best argument for being conservative about the return-to-skate timeline rather than optimistic about it — the cost of being wrong isn't a few extra weeks off the board, it's a materially worse injury.

## The gear side: helmet fit matters more than the sticker

A helmet doesn't prevent concussions outright — no helmet does, since they're built primarily to prevent skull fracture and reduce the force transmitted to the brain, not eliminate the rotational forces a glancing fall produces. But fit matters enormously: a helmet that sits too high, rocks side to side, or isn't buckled snugly loses a meaningful share of its protection before you've even fallen. Multi-directional impact protection system (MIPS) — a low-friction layer inside the helmet designed to let it rotate slightly on impact and reduce rotational force on the brain — is increasingly common in bike and multi-sport helmets, and it's worth looking for if you're replacing yours, alongside a proper skate-specific certification rather than a repurposed bike lid. [This site's piece on injury statistics](/blog/skateboarding-injury-statistics-for-adults/) has the fuller picture on what the evidence does and doesn't say about helmet protection specifically.

## The actual takeaway

A concussion isn't the injury to shrug off because the swelling looks manageable or the headache fades after a day. Give it the structured, staged return the way you would any serious injury, get immediate emergency care for the red-flag signs, get an actual clinical clearance before you return to anything with fall risk, and treat "when in doubt, sit it out" as the rule rather than the exception. Your wrist will heal roughly on schedule either way. The direct evidence for a slower brain recovery comes from a small study of people in their 50s and 60s, not a blanket finding about everyone over 40 — but nothing in the research says a 45-year-old gets the faster, younger-adult timeline either. The honest practical lesson is the same: don't assume the "back in a week or two" rule from youth sports applies to you just because you're only in your 40s.
