The Los Angeles Olympics are scheduled for the summer of 2028, with skateboarding events set for July 18–27 of that year. Two years out feels early to care about an Olympics post, but it's actually the right time to pay attention — because what happens in the next two years, not the two weeks of the Games themselves, is what will actually affect your local park.

I've [written before](/blog/how-the-olympics-changed-skatepark-culture/) about how skateboarding's Olympic debut in Tokyo changed the culture around parks — less gatekeeping, more acceptance of visible beginners. LA 2028 is a different kind of inflection point, and it's worth understanding what's actually confirmed versus what's still just hope.

## Skateboarding just stopped being a guest sport

Tokyo 2020 introduced skateboarding to the Olympics as a new addition. Paris 2024 kept it on the program, which still wasn't a sure thing at the time. What's changed since then is bigger than either of those individual Games: World Skate has confirmed that skateboarding is now part of the permanent Olympic event schedule starting with LA 2028, rather than something that gets re-voted onto the program cycle by cycle.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A sport that might get dropped after one more Games is a hard thing for a national federation, a city parks department, or a sponsor to build multi-year plans around. A sport that's locked in long-term is a much easier thing to invest in. Permanent status doesn't guarantee a single new park gets built, but it's the kind of structural change that tends to make longer-term investment conversations easier to have.

## What Paris actually left behind

It's worth looking at what the last host city actually did, because "Olympic legacy" promises are easy to make and often modest in practice. For the Paris Games, a temporary skatepark called the Saint-Gobain Skate Game — about 400 square metres of 3D-printed concrete modules, built to resemble a giant pinball machine — went up on the Esplanade de La Défense for the duration of the competition.

As part of Paris 2024's official legacy program, the plan was to relocate that equipment after the Games to a park in Puteaux, roughly doubling the size of a park that was already there and making the new modules usable by skaters and scooter riders together. That's a real, concrete outcome of hosting the Games — and it's also a useful calibration for expectations. One city got one expanded park out of it, not a nationwide wave of new construction. "Legacy" projects tend to be real but limited in scale, and it's worth knowing that going in rather than expecting a skatepark boom to simply appear.

## What's actually committed in LA so far

On the LA side, the clearest funding commitment isn't skatepark-specific. As part of its agreement with the city, the LA28 organizing committee pledged up to $160 million toward youth sports programming aimed at improving access to sport generally for the city's young people — not new skatepark construction specifically. LA28 itself has pointed to the existing Venice Beach Skatepark as an example of the kind of youth-access legacy it's aiming for, which tells you the model is "support what's already working" as much as "build something new."

As of now, there's no publicly confirmed, dedicated skateboarding competition venue location locked in for the Games. That piece is still being worked out. None of this is a reason for cynicism — it's a reason to treat the next two years as the actual window that matters, rather than waiting for the opening ceremony to see what shows up.

## What this means for you, starting now

Nothing about an Olympics happening in a city automatically improves the park down the street from you, in LA or anywhere else. What does tend to happen in the run-up to a host city's Games is a wave of public input processes around parks and recreation — meetings, surveys, advisory commissions — as cities look for projects to point to as part of their Games story.

That's the actual opening for adult skaters, in LA or in any city hoping to ride the same wave of attention. If there's a parks and recreation commission or a skate advocacy group near you, this is a good two-year window to show up and ask for the specific things that make a park work for adults: [mellow entry slopes, beginner zones, good lighting, and smooth surfaces](/blog/inclusive-skateparks-all-ages/), not just headline features aimed at drawing elite competition. Advocacy lands much better before a design is finalized than after a ribbon-cutting.

## The realistic takeaway

LA 2028 is a genuine milestone for the sport — skateboarding's move to a permanent spot on the Olympic program is bigger than most of the headlines around it suggest. But an Olympics happening nearby is a spotlight, not a guarantee. Paris turned its spotlight into one meaningfully bigger park. What LA turns its spotlight into, and whether any of it reaches the kind of park that actually works for a returning 45-year-old, depends a lot on who shows up to ask for it over the next two years. That can be you.
