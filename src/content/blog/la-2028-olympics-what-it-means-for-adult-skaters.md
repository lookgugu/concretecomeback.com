The Los Angeles Olympics run July 14–30, 2028, with the four skateboarding events — men's and women's street and park — scheduled for July 18–27. Two years out feels early to care about an Olympics post, but it's actually the right time to pay attention — because what happens in the next two years, not the two weeks of the Games themselves, is what will actually affect your local park.

I've [written before](/blog/how-the-olympics-changed-skatepark-culture/) about how skateboarding's Olympic debut in Tokyo changed the culture around parks — less gatekeeping, more acceptance of visible beginners. LA 2028 is a different kind of inflection point, and it's worth understanding what's actually confirmed versus what's still just hope.

## Skateboarding is no longer a guest sport

Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024 both had skateboarding because the host city asked for it: under the IOC's rules, each organising committee can propose a handful of extra sports for its own Games, and both cities picked skateboarding. LA 2028 is different. In February 2022 the IOC Session voted skateboarding, surfing and sport climbing onto LA28's core programme of 28 sports — the list the IOC itself sets, rather than the host's wish list. World Skate, the sport's governing body, called it skateboarding becoming "a fully-fledged permanent Olympic sport."

One honest caveat on that word "permanent": the IOC said at the time that no sport is a permanent addition, and the programme is still approved Games by Games. Brisbane 2032's sport list hasn't been decided yet, and IOC president Kirsty Coventry has said it will be shorter than LA28's. So the accurate version is that skateboarding has moved from the host's optional pick to the IOC's own list, which is a real promotion even if it isn't a guarantee.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A sport that might get dropped after one more Games is a hard thing for a national federation, a city parks department, or a sponsor to build multi-year plans around. A sport on the core list is a much easier thing to invest in. Core status doesn't guarantee a single new park gets built, but it's the kind of structural change that tends to make longer-term investment conversations easier to have.

## What Paris actually left behind

It's worth looking at what the last host city actually did, because "Olympic legacy" promises are easy to make and often modest in practice. For the Paris Games, a temporary skatepark called the Saint-Gobain Skate Game — about 400 square metres of 3D-printed concrete modules, laid out to resemble a giant pinball machine — went up on the Esplanade de La Défense from July 18 to August 11, 2024.

As part of Paris 2024's official legacy programme, Saint-Gobain donated the modules to the neighbouring town of Puteaux, and after the Games they were moved to the Île de Puteaux to sit alongside the existing skatepark there — a roughly 500-square-metre extension the town said would about double the area, free to use and open to skaters, scooter riders and roller skaters alike. That's a real, concrete outcome of hosting the Games — and it's also a useful calibration for expectations. One town got one expanded park out of it, not a nationwide wave of new construction. "Legacy" projects tend to be real but limited in scale, and it's worth knowing that going in rather than expecting a skatepark boom to simply appear.

## What's actually committed in LA so far

The competition venue is settled, and it's temporary. LA28's venue plan, approved by the IOC in April 2025, puts both skateboarding disciplines at the Sepulveda Basin Recreation Area in the San Fernando Valley, on a purpose-built open-air course shared with BMX. Like Paris's, it's not a park that will still be there for you in 2029 — so the venue itself isn't the legacy, and anything that is will have to come from somewhere else.

The clearest funding commitment, meanwhile, isn't skatepark-specific. Under its agreement with the city, LA28 pledged up to $160 million toward youth sports programming — the money behind the city's PlayLA scheme, which runs low-cost seasons in Olympic and Paralympic sports, skateboarding and adaptive skateboarding included, for kids aged 3 to 17. The example LA28 keeps returning to in its own storytelling is the existing Venice Beach Skatepark: Sky Brown built her version of the LA28 emblem around her favourite spot there. That tells you the model is "support what's already working" as much as "build something new."

None of this is a reason for cynicism — it's a reason to treat the next two years as the actual window that matters, rather than waiting for the opening ceremony to see what shows up.

## What this means for you, starting now

Nothing about an Olympics happening in a city automatically improves the park down the street from you, in LA or anywhere else. What does tend to happen in the run-up to a host city's Games is a wave of public input processes around parks and recreation — meetings, surveys, advisory commissions — as cities look for projects to point to as part of their Games story.

That's the actual opening for adult skaters, in LA or in any city hoping to ride the same wave of attention. If there's a parks and recreation commission or a skate advocacy group near you, this is a good two-year window to show up and ask for the specific things that make a park work for adults: [mellow entry slopes, beginner zones, good lighting, and smooth surfaces](/blog/inclusive-skateparks-all-ages/), not just headline features aimed at drawing elite competition. Advocacy lands much better before a design is finalized than after a ribbon-cutting.

## The realistic takeaway

LA 2028 is a genuine milestone for the sport — skateboarding's move onto the IOC's own programme is bigger than most of the headlines around it suggest. But an Olympics happening nearby is a spotlight, not a guarantee. Paris turned its spotlight into one meaningfully bigger park. What LA turns its spotlight into, and whether any of it reaches the kind of park that actually works for a returning 45-year-old, depends a lot on who shows up to ask for it over the next two years. That can be you.
