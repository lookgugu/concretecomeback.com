As a physiotherapist, most of the adult skaters I treat didn't get hurt doing something advanced. They got hurt in the first five minutes of a session, on something ordinary, because their body wasn't ready yet. That's the injury a warm-up prevents — not the spectacular fall, but the ordinary one that happens because a joint or muscle was still cold when it was asked to work.

At 20, you could roll up and start skating immediately. At 45, that same habit is where a lot of avoidable strains and tweaks come from. Ten minutes fixes most of it.

## Why warm-up matters more after 40

Tissue elasticity decreases with age — tendons and ligaments are simply stiffer when they're cold than they were two decades ago, and it takes longer for blood flow and joint lubrication to catch up once you start moving. Skateboarding also asks a lot from a narrow set of joints right away: ankles absorbing uneven terrain, hips managing lateral load in carves, wrists ready to catch a fall. Warming those specific areas up, rather than just "getting loose" generally, is what actually reduces injury risk.

This is the "prehab" logic that's become standard advice in sports medicine: prepare the tissue for the demand before you place the demand on it, instead of rehabbing an injury after the fact.

## The 10-minute routine

Do the first eight minutes before you put your foot on the board, ideally right after you arrive at the park while you're still in street shoes. Change into your skate shoes before the final two minutes, once you're ready to step on the board.

### Minutes 0–2: Get blood moving

Light cardio first — brisk walking, marching in place, or a slow jog around the park. The goal isn't cardio fitness, it's raising your core temperature and getting blood into your muscles before you stretch or load anything.

### Minutes 2–4: Ankles and feet

Ankle circles, ten each direction, per foot. Follow with a few controlled calf raises and ankle rocks (rocking your weight from heel to toe). Your ankles do continuous micro-adjustment work while skating — they should not be doing that job cold.

### Minutes 4–6: Hips

Leg swings, front-to-back and side-to-side, ten per side, holding a rail or wall for balance. Follow with a few bodyweight squats and hip circles. Carving and turning load your hip abductors more than most everyday movement does, so this is worth the time even though it's easy to skip.

### Minutes 6–8: Wrists and shoulders

Wrist circles and gentle wrist flexion/extension stretches, both directions. A few arm circles and shoulder rolls. If you fall, your wrists and shoulders are often first to absorb it — they should already be moving, not stiff.

### Minutes 8–10: First pushes

Change into your skate shoes, step on the board on flat, open ground, and just push and glide, gently, for the last two minutes. No tricks, no features, nothing that requires commitment. This is where your nervous system reconnects with balance and board feel before you ask more of it.

## Prehab, not rehab

The mindset shift worth adopting is this: a warm-up isn't a chore that delays skating, it's part of the session. Ten minutes out of a ninety-minute session is a small cost against the alternative — a strained hip flexor or a tweaked ankle that costs you weeks, not minutes.

This is also where off-board work pays off over time. Regular hip and ankle mobility work outside of skating sessions means your warm-up has less ground to cover on any given day, and your baseline mobility stays higher between sessions.

## Make it a habit

The routine only works if you actually do it, every session, not just on days you remember. A few ways to make that more likely:

- Do it in the same order every time so it becomes automatic rather than something you have to think through
- Do the first eight minutes in your street shoes, then change into skate shoes for the final two — the shoe change creates a natural transition point into skating
- Skip nothing on days you're tempted to skip everything. A rushed two-minute version of this routine beats none of it.

Ten minutes is a small ask against everything a season of skating gives you back. Treat it as non-negotiable, the same way you treat your helmet.
