In a Reader's Digest interview published this July, Tony Hawk — 58 years old, still skating, still recognizably Tony Hawk — said something that landed harder than it should have coming from the most famous skateboarder alive:

> "You have to listen to your body. I am as guilty as anyone of not listening to my body and pushing through pain, to the point where I sabotage my own health. I don't do that anymore."

That's not a quote about retirement. It's a quote about recalibration. And it's worth sitting with if you're 45 and wondering whether the version of skating you're building for yourself counts.

## The line that matters

"I don't do that anymore" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Hawk isn't describing giving up skating — he's describing giving up a specific relationship to pain, the one where you push through it because stopping feels like losing. He's had his share of serious injuries over the years, including a broken femur that would have ended many people's skating for good. He's still out there. What changed isn't his commitment to skating. It's his willingness to override his body's signals to prove something.

That's a distinction adults returning to skating in their 40s and 50s should sit with, because most of us picked up the opposite habit somewhere along the way — the idea that backing off is the same as failing.

## What "still skating" actually means at 58

There's a version of this story that turns Hawk into an outlier, a genetic freak, someone whose continued skating has nothing to do with you. That's a comfortable story because it lets you off the hook. It's also not the useful read.

The useful read is that one of the most physically gifted athletes the sport has produced still had to learn, at some point, to stop treating his body as a tool that should just comply. If he had to learn it, everyone does. Nobody skates forever on willpower alone. What determines whether you're still skating in ten years isn't toughness — it's whether you learned to work with your body instead of against it.

## The permission adults need too

Most adult skaters carry some version of guilt about the tricks or features they've quietly stopped attempting. Maybe it's drop-ins on anything taller than you feel steady on. Maybe it's anything requiring a hard commitment to speed. Maybe it's just accepting that today isn't a stairs day.

That guilt is misplaced. It comes from measuring your current skating against either your younger self or against skaters who don't share your risk calculus — your job, your recovery time, the people depending on you. Hawk, at the top of the sport, still had to give himself explicit permission to stop pushing through pain. You're allowed to give yourself the same permission without a career's worth of achievement behind you first.

## Giving yourself the same permission

In practice, this looks less dramatic than Hawk's version. It might mean:

- Skipping a session, or ending one early, when something feels off rather than skating through it
- Sticking to features you're confident on instead of chasing whatever the teenager next to you is doing
- Treating "I didn't feel like pushing it today" as a legitimate reason, not an excuse you owe anyone an explanation for

None of this is about doing less skating. It's about skating in a way that's still available to you next year, and the year after that.

## The real lesson

The headline version of this story is "Tony Hawk is still skating at 58." The more useful version is quieter: the guy who built his identity on doing things nobody else could do has spent recent years learning to say no to his own instincts. That's not a decline. It's the actual skill that keeps you doing something you love for decades instead of burning out on it in your first year back.

If you've been quietly deciding which parts of skating you're willing to let go of, you're not backsliding. You're doing the thing Tony Hawk just told Reader's Digest took him this long to learn.
