Skateboard truck mounting holes follow one of two standard bolt patterns: the modern new-school pattern (`1.625 inches` wide by `2.125 inches` long, center to center) used on almost all decks since the mid-1990s, and the vintage old-school pattern (`1.625 inches` wide by `2.5 inches` long) found on 1970s and 1980s boards and some historic reissues. When a skateboard deck changes width, the four bolt holes must remain centered across the width of the board so the trucks track straight along the centerline; when a deck changes length or nose-to-tail shape, the bolt holes do not stay at a fixed fraction of overall length because they are drilled relative to the flat mounting pads inside the nose and tail kick bends.

Those measurements come up whenever an older skater pulls a 1980s deck down from the garage rafters, orders a wide shaped reissue, or wonders why some truck baseplates come drilled with six or eight holes instead of four. Understanding how truck hole spacing and deck centering work saves you from ordering hardware that will not bolt up cleanly.

## Old-school vs new-school truck hole dimensions

Every standard traditional kingpin (TKP) skateboard truck uses four mounting bolts arranged in a rectangle on the baseplate. Across decades of skateboarding, the horizontal width of that rectangle never changed, while the lengthwise spacing shortened in the 1990s as street skaters began riding steeper double-kick noses and tails:

### New-school mounting pattern (1.625" wide x 2.125" long)
Measured center-to-center across the holes, the modern standard is 1-5/8 inches (41.28 mm) wide by 2-1/8 inches (53.98 mm) long. Because the front-to-back footprint is 3/8 of an inch shorter than the old 1980s pattern, manufacturers can press the upward bend of the nose and tail kicks closer to the trucks without forcing the flat metal baseplate to ride up onto the curved wood of the kicktail.

### Old-school mounting pattern (1.625" wide x 2.5" long)
From the 1970s through the late 1980s, standard baseplates used the same 1-5/8-inch (41.28 mm) width paired with a longer 2-1/2-inch (63.5 mm) front-to-back span. You will still encounter this pattern on original vintage decks (see our guide to [refurbishing an old skateboard in your garage](/blog/refurbishing-an-old-skateboard/)), certain faithful 1980s pool and freestyle reissues, and some longboard decks.

### Why some truck baseplates have 6 holes
Look at the baseplate on a pair of Independent Stage 11s (in wider sizes like 159, 169, and 215) or Ace Classic/AF1 trucks and you will often see six holes instead of four. Because the crosswise width (`1.625 inches`) is identical on both standards, drilling a second pair of outer holes `2.5 inches` apart alongside the inner `2.125-inch` holes lets a single 6-hole baseplate bolt directly onto either a modern new-school deck or a vintage old-school deck.

## Why bolt holes stay centered across width, and how they shift along length

A common geometry question when comparing a narrow 7.75-inch street board with a 9.25-inch shaped bowl deck is whether changing a deck's width or length changes where the bolt holes sit relative to the center of the board. The answer splits into two axes:

### Across the width axis: always centered on the longitudinal centerline
Whether a deck is 7.5 inches wide or 10.0 inches wide (covered in [what board size older skaters should ride](/blog/what-board-size-for-adults/)), the `1.625-inch` horizontal spacing between the left and right bolt holes is always drilled symmetrically across the exact longitudinal centerline of the deck. If the bolt holes shifted off-center to the left or right when a board got wider, the wheels would sit closer to one rail than the other, causing uneven leverage between heel-side and toe-side carves (see our [truck bushings and durometer guide](/blog/skateboard-truck-bushings-durometer-weight-guide/)) and making the board pull sideways on flat ground.

### Along the length axis: anchored to wheelbase and kick pockets, not overall center
Along the nose-to-tail axis, truck holes are not drilled at a fixed percentage of total board length, and on directional boards the midpoint between the two trucks does not even sit at the geometric center of the wood:
- On a directional cruiser or 1980s pool shape (see [longboard vs cruiser vs popsicle decks](/blog/longboard-vs-cruiser-vs-popsicle-deck/)), the tail kick is often longer and wider than the stubby nose, so the front truck mounting holes sit much closer to the front tip than the rear holes sit to the tail tip.
- Even on a modern symmetrical popsicle deck, the nose is typically 0.25 to 0.5 inches longer than the tail (for example, a 7.0-inch nose and a 6.5-inch tail), placing the rear truck bolts slightly closer to the back edge than the front bolts are to the nose tip.
- When a deck series scales up in width and length, factories choose the inner-hole [wheelbase and deck length](/blog/skateboard-wheelbase-deck-length-stance-guide/) (typically `14.0"` to `15.0"`) to match the concave mold so that each baseplate sits dead flat on the level mounting pad just inside the transition where the nose and tail kicks bend upward.

## What to check before bolting trucks onto a reissue or cruiser deck

If you are building a new setup or swapping trucks at a local shop from our [skate shop directory](/directory/shops) such as [DLX in San Francisco](/directory/shops/us/dlx-san-francisco-ca/) or [Westside Skates in Lakewood](/directory/shops/us/westside-skates-lakewood-oh/):

### Measure the lengthwise hole spacing on any reissue deck
Hold a ruler across the front-to-back holes on the deck. If the centers sit `2-1/8 inches` (`2.125"`) apart, any modern 4-hole or 6-hole truck fits. If they sit `2-1/2 inches` (`2.5"`) apart, make sure your trucks have 6-hole baseplates before ordering.

### Never force angled bolts or drill unaligned holes by hand
If a 4-hole new-school baseplate does not match a 2.5-inch old-school deck, do not try to jam the bolts in at an angle or freehand new holes with a power drill. Even a two-millimeter misalignment twists the hanger off square so the board steers crooked on every push. Use a 6-hole baseplate or ask a skate shop with a proper drilling jig to align the mount cleanly.
