For about a year after I came back to skating, my sessions only happened when everything else in the week lined up — which meant they happened rarely. What actually got me skating consistently wasn't finding more free time. It was noticing I already had thirty free minutes, right after morning drop-off, that I'd been driving straight past on my way to work.

That gap between dropping your kid at school and needing to be somewhere else is one of the most underused pockets of time in a parent's week. It's already carved out, already protected by the school schedule, and it doesn't require explaining to anyone why you're taking it.

## Why this works better than "I'll skate when I have time"

Unscheduled skating loses to everything else on a given day — work, errands, being tired. A session that's attached to something you're already doing, at a time you're already out of the house, doesn't compete with the rest of your day the same way. You're not carving out new time. You're using time that already exists.

The other advantage is that it's solo. Family sessions with your kids are great, but they're a different kind of session — you're managing someone else's pace, encouragement, safety. A drop-off session is just you, for a set window, with nobody else's needs to track. That's not selfish. It's the difference between a session that recharges you and one that's still a form of parenting.

## Finding the right park for this

The park doesn't need to be your favorite park. It needs to be close to the school and quick to get in and out of.

Look for:

- **Something within a five- to ten-minute drive of drop-off**, ideally along your route to work — or at least not a detour off it — so the session doesn't add backtracking to your morning.
- **A park that's usually quiet at that hour.** Mid-morning on a weekday tends to be one of the emptier windows at most parks — school-age kids are in class, and it's before lunch crowds. That's a real advantage: more room, less waiting for features, less self-consciousness if you're still building confidence.
- **Somewhere with easy parking close to the entrance.** Thirty minutes goes fast, and you don't want to spend five of them looking for a spot.

## Keep a ready-to-go bag, not a car full of gear

The single biggest thing that makes this habit stick: don't make yourself pack a bag every morning. Pack one kit — board, helmet, wrist guards, a pair of shoes you don't mind skating in — and keep it by the door, ready to grab on your way out. The only decision each morning should be whether to swing by the park, not whether you remembered your gear.

Don't leave it sitting in the trunk long-term, especially in warm weather. A parked car can get hot enough to quietly degrade a helmet's foam liner and adhesives — with no visible sign that it's happened — and the same heat swings aren't good for a board's urethane parts either. Load the bag in when you leave and bring it back in when you're done, rather than treating the car as permanent storage.

Anything that requires assembly or planning the night before is a habit that survives about two weeks. Anything that's just "grab the bag on the way out" survives much longer.

## Structuring thirty minutes so it's actually enough

Thirty minutes sounds short, and it is — but it's enough if you don't waste it deciding what to do.

A workable shape:

1. **First 10 minutes**: a real warm-up — ankles, hips, wrists, then easy pushing and rolling. Don't shorten this to make room for more skating; if time is genuinely tight, cut the middle block instead. A cold body is a bigger injury risk than a shorter session.
2. **Middle 15 minutes**: pick one thing to work on and stay on it. This isn't the session for trying five different things. Repetition on one skill — a turn, a specific line, pumping through transitions — is what a short, frequent session is actually good for.
3. **Last 5 minutes**: cool down, catch your breath, walk back to the car without rushing. Arriving at work slightly out of breath and flustered undercuts the whole point.

Set a timer if you need to. It's easy to lose track and end up either cutting the session short out of anxiety about being late, or blowing past your window and starting the rest of the day rushed.

## The guilt question

A lot of parents' first instinct is that thirty minutes to yourself, on a weekday morning, is time that should go to something more "productive" — emails, errands, the gym. It's worth naming that directly: this is productive. A parent who's spent thirty minutes doing something just for themselves shows up better for the rest of the day, for their kids, and for their work, than one who skipped it to answer emails ten minutes earlier.

You don't need to justify it further than that.

## What this turns into over time

The value of the drop-off session isn't really any single thirty-minute block. It's what happens after two or three months of not missing it — skills that only build through repetition start showing up, and skating stops being something you're trying to fit in and becomes something that's just part of Tuesday.

It's a small habit. It's also one of the more reliable ways I've seen adults actually stay skating past the first excited month, instead of falling off once the initial motivation fades.
