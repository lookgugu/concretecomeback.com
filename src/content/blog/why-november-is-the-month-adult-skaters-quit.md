If you organise sessions long enough, you start to notice the calendar has a villain, and for adult skaters it isn't January. January gets blamed because it's when resolutions die in public, but the quitting actually starts in November. I've watched it happen for years in Portland: a group that's been showing up all autumn quietly thins out somewhere around the second week of the month, and by Thanksgiving half of them are gone for the season — and a good number of those never fully come back.

November isn't unlucky. It's a specific, predictable convergence of pressures that all land in the same two or three weeks, and almost nobody sees it coming until they're already in it.

## The convergence, not just one problem

Any single one of these would be manageable. Stacked together, they're what actually ends comebacks.

**The light goes first.** Clocks change, and the window for an after-work session that used to run until dusk now ends in near-dark. For a lot of adult skaters, that one scheduling shift removes the only slot they had. It's not that they stop wanting to skate — their available daylight just stops matching their available time.

**The surface stops cooperating.** Cold concrete is harder, literally — grip feels different, your board doesn't quite respond the way it did in September, and the first slick morning or wet leaf layer catches people who were skating confidently a month earlier. [Autumn's hazards](/blog/autumn-skating-hazards-for-adults/) are real, and November is where they stop being occasional and start being the default conditions.

**The novelty is fully gone.** If you came back to skating this year, you're now several months in — past the excitement of month one, likely past the [six-month plateau](/blog/why-adult-skaters-quit-at-six-months/) too. The thing that carried you through summer, pure enthusiasm, isn't available anymore. What's left is whether you've built habits sturdy enough to run without it.

**Holiday logistics start eating the calendar.** Travel planning, family obligations, and the general compression of a season that's suddenly full of commitments all begin in November, not December. The sessions that get cut first are the ones that don't feel essential yet, and for a lot of returning adults, skating hasn't been positioned as essential — it's been positioned as something they do when everything else is handled. In November, everything else stops being handled.

None of these four is dramatic on its own. Together, in the same three-week window, they're enough to end a comeback that survived the much harder early months.

## The five-part survival plan

**1. Move your session, don't cancel it.** If darkness has killed your evening slot, don't wait for motivation to find you a new one — pick a specific replacement before the old slot actually disappears. A lunch-break session, an earlier weekend morning, or [an indoor park](/blog/october-reset-building-your-skating-base/) if one exists near you. The goal isn't finding the perfect slot, it's making sure there's still a slot at all.

**2. Set a minimum, not a goal.** November is the wrong month to ask yourself to maintain summer-level frequency, and treating that as the bar just guarantees you'll feel like you're failing. Pick a minimum you can hit even on a bad week — one session, 20 minutes, whatever's realistic — and protect that minimum fiercely. A small, kept commitment beats an ambitious one you abandon by week two.

**3. Build in an accountability structure now, before you need it.** This is the single biggest lever I've seen work across years of running sessions: someone else expecting you to show up. If you don't already have [a regular skating partner or group](/blog/finding-other-adult-skaters/), November is exactly the wrong time to go it alone, because willpower is the resource most depleted by cold, dark, and a full calendar. Text someone. Commit to a specific day. Make not showing up cost you something social, not just internal.

**4. Have an indoor or bad-weather backup planned, not improvised.** Know, in advance, what you do when it's raining, below freezing, or dark by 4:30. That might be an indoor skatepark, a parking garage with decent lighting, flatground drills in a garage, or simply accepting a short list of "skate anyway" days. The point is having the answer ready before the night it's actually needed, when you're tired and the easy choice is to just stay home.

**5. Decide in advance that a quiet month isn't a quit.** The riskiest moment isn't missing one session — it's the story you tell yourself after missing three in a row. If November genuinely needs to be lighter for you, let it be lighter without deciding that means you're done. Write down, now, that a reduced November is part of the plan, not a failure of it. That one reframe is often the difference between someone who skates less for a month and someone who doesn't skate again until spring, if at all.

## What's actually at stake

I've seen this pattern often enough to know the people who quit in November rarely frame it as quitting. It's always "just taking a break until the new year," and for a real chunk of them, the new year never quite arrives — new habits are harder to restart than momentum is to maintain. The ones who get through it aren't the ones with more willpower. They're the ones who saw the convergence coming and made small, specific adjustments before they needed them, instead of hoping motivation would carry them through a month that was never going to give them any.

If you're reading this in October, you have a head start nobody gets by accident. Use it: pick your winter slot now, line up your accountability, and decide today what your minimum is. The version of you skating in March will be glad you did.
