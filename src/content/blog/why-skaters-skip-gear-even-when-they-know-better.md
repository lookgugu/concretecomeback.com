I treat a lot of adult skaters after the fact, which means I hear a lot of versions of the same sentence: "I know I should have had my wrist guards on." Not "I didn't know wrist guards help." Not "I don't believe in gear." Just — I knew, and I didn't, and then I did.

That gap between knowing and doing is the actual problem, and it's a more interesting one than "some people are careless." Most of the adults I see skating without gear aren't careless. They're making a specific, predictable set of decisions that feel reasonable in the moment and look obviously wrong in hindsight.

## The numbers are worse than people assume

A 2018 Hunter College observational study of skaters at 31 New York City skateparks, covering roughly 2,400 rides across all five boroughs, found that about 10% of riders wore a helmet and about 8% wore elbow pads, knee pads, or wrist guards. That's not a study of adults specifically, and skatepark culture varies by city, but it lines up with what most of us see with our own eyes at a session: gear is the exception, not the norm, even among people who'd tell you it works.

That matters because the evidence for the gear itself isn't in question. [The wrist guard study every returning skater should know about](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/) and the broader injury data in [skateboarding injury statistics for adults](/blog/skateboarding-injury-statistics-for-adults/) both point the same way: the two most common serious injuries in adult skaters are wrist fractures and head trauma, and they're also the two categories a helmet and a decent pair of wrist guards meaningfully reduce. This isn't a case of unclear science. It's a case of clear science that doesn't reliably change behavior.

## Why knowing isn't enough

**Comparative optimism.** Researchers who study helmet use in cyclists keep finding the same pattern, and it almost certainly generalizes to skating: people rate their own risk as lower than the average person's, specifically because they see themselves as more careful, more experienced, or more in control than whoever gets hurt in the statistics. You can hold "wrist guards prevent fractures" as a true fact and still quietly exempt yourself from needing them today, because today you're only doing easy stuff.

**It's never the planned fall that gets you.** Nobody straps on gear expecting to need it for the trick they're about to attempt on purpose. The falls that actually happen are the ones nobody saw coming — a wheel catching a pebble on what was supposed to be a slow warm-up lap. If gear only comes out for the moments that feel risky, it's absent for most of the moments that turn out to be risky.

**Friction, not philosophy.** A Canadian study of injured cyclists found that most unhelmeted riders didn't think helmets were useless or unnecessary — they just found them inconvenient or uncomfortable in the moment. That tracks with what I hear from skaters, too: it's not an argument against wrist guards, it's "I didn't feel like digging them out of my bag for a quick session." The barrier is logistical, not intellectual, which means an intellectual argument won't fix it.

**Looking like you're overdoing it.** This one is harder for adults than it was at 15, not easier. A lot of returning skaters are already self-conscious about being the oldest or least smooth person at the park, and full gear can feel like it's broadcasting "beginner" or "overly cautious" in a way that compounds that. [Building confidence at 40](/blog/building-confidence-at-40/) covers the broader version of this fear of looking silly — the gear version of it is specific and solvable, which is the useful part.

## What actually closes the gap

**Remove the decision, don't relitigate it.** The point in the moment where you weigh "do I really need guards for this" is the point where optimism bias wins, every time. The fix is to not have that conversation with yourself at all — gear goes on before you think about whether today's session warrants it, the same way a seatbelt goes on before you think about whether this particular drive is risky.

**Make the gear easier to grab than the decision to skip it.** If your wrist guards are buried in a closet, you'll skip them on the days you're in a hurry, which is most days. Keep them in your board bag, permanently, so getting dressed to skate and putting on gear are the same single action rather than two separate ones.

**Attach it to a fixed cue.** The same trick that works for warming up works here: pick a moment that happens every single session — lacing your shoes, stepping off the car, clipping your helmet — and do it in the same order every time until it's not a decision anymore.

**Say the quiet part about how it looks.** Skate culture, on the whole, does not look down on an adult in full gear. The riders who'd judge you for it are a smaller and louder minority than they feel like from the sidelines, and the ones who matter — the regulars who'll actually talk to you — read gear as "serious about this," not "scared of this."

None of this requires learning anything new about risk. You already know the numbers. The work is making the safe choice the default one, so it doesn't have to survive a negotiation with yourself every time you push off.
