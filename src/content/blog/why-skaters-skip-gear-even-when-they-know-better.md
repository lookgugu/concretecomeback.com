When a skateboard wheel stops against a pebble or slides out on a slick bank, the body's fastest reflex is to reach an outstretched hand toward the concrete. Most adult skaters know that wrist fractures and head impacts are among the main serious risks of a fall. Even so, observational research at public skateparks shows that helmets, wrist guards, and pads remain the exception during everyday sessions.

Looking at what the studies actually measured — and why riders leave protective gear in the car — makes it easier to set up a habit you will follow every time you skate.

## What the studies actually measured

In spring 2018, [Hunter College researchers Peter Tuckel and William Milczarski](https://hunter.cuny.edu/news/riders-in-new-york-city-skateboard-parks-are-skating-on-thin-ice-few-wear-helmets-or-protective-gear-a-new-hunter-college-study-finds/) observed roughly 2,400 skateboard rides across 31 New York City skateparks in all five boroughs. Only about 10% of observed riders wore a helmet, and roughly 8% wore wrist guards, elbow pads, or knee pads. That field study observed park users of all ages in one city rather than isolating returning adults over 40, so it is a snapshot of general skatepark norms rather than an adult-only survey.

The protective equipment itself has a clear, specific role in the injury literature. For wrists, a [1996 case-control study of in-line skaters by Schieber and colleagues in the *New England Journal of Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199611283352202) found that riders without wrist guards had roughly ten times the odds of a wrist injury compared with guarded riders (an adjusted odds ratio of 10.4). As [our breakdown of the wrist guard research](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/) and [our review of adult skateboarding injury statistics](/blog/skateboarding-injury-statistics-for-adults/) explain, that study covered in-line skating rather than skateboarding, and laboratory testing shows that a rigid splint helps spread load and reduce strain rather than making a wrist unbreakable. Likewise, a certified skate helmet reduces the severity of head impacts without preventing falls or eliminating every concussion risk. Gear is specific risk reduction for the moments when your balance correction or falling technique runs out of time.

## Why riders leave gear in the bag

When adults own a helmet and wrist guards and still skate bare-handed, the decision usually comes down to three practical traps:

- **Saving pads for "hard" tricks.** It is easy to treat a slow flatground warm-up or a quick cruise across a parking lot as low-risk and save pads for bowls or ramps. Because a wheel catching a pebble at rolling speed triggers the same outstretched-hand reflex as a fall on transition, grading gear by the planned difficulty leaves you unprotected on routine pushes.
- **Discomfort and inconvenience.** If wrist guards pinch your palm or stay buried at the bottom of a closet, any rushed afternoon becomes an excuse to skip them.
- **Self-consciousness at a busy park.** Returning in your forties or fifties already makes you feel conspicuous among younger regulars, and strapping on full pads when few other riders wear them can add to that hesitation ([building confidence at 40](/blog/building-confidence-at-40/) covers the wider social side of that discomfort).

## Making gear use automatic

Closing the gap between owning protective gear and wearing it means taking the negotiation out of the start of a session:

- **Tie your baseline gear to stepping on the board.** Decide ahead of time that a certified skate helmet and rigid-splint wrist guards go on before your first push on flat ground, and add knee pads and hip protection whenever you skate transition.
- **Check fit before you head to the park.** Comparing your hand or wrist measurements with the brand's sizing guide and testing how the straps feel before a session can help you find a guard that stays comfortable in your palm without crowding your thumb.
- **Pack your gear attached to your setup.** If you keep your board in the car for quick sessions after work, clipping your helmet strap around the front truck and sliding your wrist guards over the nose means you have to hold them before you can set the deck down on concrete.
