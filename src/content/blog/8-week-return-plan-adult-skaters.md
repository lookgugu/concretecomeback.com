When I came back to skating at 46, I had no plan. I bought a board, showed up at the local park on a Saturday afternoon, and got overwhelmed by teenagers dropping into a bowl I wasn't remotely ready for. I went home discouraged and didn't go back for three weeks.

What I needed was a structure — a way to know what "week one" and "week six" were supposed to look like, so I wasn't guessing every time I stepped on the board. This is the plan I wish I'd had. It's not rigid, and you should adjust it to your own pace, but it gives you a starting shape instead of a blank page.

## Before week one: set up right

Get a board that fits you — most adults do best on a deck 8.5" or wider, with softer wheels (78a–87a) that forgive rough pavement. Get a helmet and wrist guards now, not after your first session. You'll read more about gear elsewhere on this site; the short version is: don't skip this step to save time. It costs you more later.

## Weeks 1–2: flat ground only

This is the least exciting and most important phase. Find a smooth, empty stretch of pavement — a quiet parking lot, a driveway, an empty tennis court — and spend two or three short sessions a week just pushing, stopping, and turning.

Goals for this phase:
- Push comfortably with either foot without looking down at the board
- Come to a controlled stop without stepping off awkwardly
- Carve smooth, wide turns in both directions
- Get comfortable with your stance and where your weight sits over the board

Sessions of 20–30 minutes are plenty. This phase rebuilds the nervous-system connections that let everything after it feel natural instead of forced. Don't rush it, even if it feels too basic to justify calling it "training."

## Weeks 3–4: gentle terrain

Once flat ground feels automatic, introduce mild inclines and transitions — a gentle bank, a driveway with a slope, or the flattest, most mellow section of a local skatepark during an off-peak hour.

Goals for this phase:
- Ride down a gentle slope in control, at a speed you choose
- Get comfortable with your weight shifting as the ground angle changes
- Start visiting a skatepark, even if only to push around the flat sections and watch how others move through the space
- Practice getting on and off the board at slightly higher speeds than flat ground allowed

This is also the phase to start thinking about falling technique — tucking, rolling, keeping your arms bent — before you need it under pressure. Practicing it now, while the stakes are low, pays off later.

## Weeks 5–6: your first real park sessions

By now your balance and pushing should feel close to automatic. This is where you start treating the skatepark as your regular training ground rather than an occasional visit.

Goals for this phase:
- Go to the park two to three times this fortnight, ideally at quieter times
- Identify and use the mellow features — small banks, low quarter pipes, wide flat sections
- Start linking movements together: push, carve, approach a small feature, roll away from it
- Pay attention to how your body feels the next day, and treat soreness as information, not a problem to push through

This is usually the phase where returning skaters start to feel like skaters again rather than people relearning a skill. It's also where overconfidence creeps in — stay honest about what still feels shaky.

## Weeks 7–8: consolidation and your own direction

The final two weeks are less about a specific new skill and more about establishing a rhythm you can sustain past the program itself.

Goals for this phase:
- Settle into a regular session schedule — most adults do best with two to three sessions a week rather than daily skating
- Choose a specific direction that interests you: cruising, mellow transition, learning your first trick, or simply consistent park sessions
- Reassess your gear now that you have real mileage on it — deck size, wheel hardness, and shoe choice all become clearer once you've actually been skating for two months
- Notice what you've built: by week eight, most returning adults are pushing, carving, and handling mellow terrain with a level of ease that felt impossible in week one

## What to do if you fall behind the plan

Life gets in the way. If you miss a week, or if week three still feels like week one did, that's not a failure of the plan — it's just where you are. Repeat a phase instead of pushing forward on a shaky foundation. There's no prize for finishing in exactly eight weeks. The prize is still skating in month six.

The structure exists to remove the guesswork, not to become another source of pressure. Adjust the pace, keep showing up, and the plan will have done its job.
