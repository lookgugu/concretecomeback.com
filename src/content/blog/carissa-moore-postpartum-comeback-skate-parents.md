Carissa Moore stepped away from competitive surfing after the Paris 2024 Olympics, where she reached the quarterfinals two months pregnant. Her daughter, Olena Lililehua Untermann, was born in February 2025. In November 2025, the World Surf League announced she'd be returning in 2026 on a full-season wildcard — guaranteed entry into every Championship Tour event, starting at Bells Beach in April and running through the Pipe Masters in December. A five-time world champion doesn't need qualifying points to prove she still belongs on tour. She needed something else: a way back onto the clock after motherhood reset it.

I don't surf and this isn't a surfing site, but I run a group where "I used to skate every weekend, then I had a kid, and now I don't know how to fit it back in" is close to the single most common sentence I hear. Moore's comeback is a useful shape to study, not because her resources match yours, but because the specific problem she solved — rebuilding a physical practice around a new, permanent demand on her time and body — is exactly your problem too.

## The wildcard is the part worth noticing

A full-season wildcard means Moore didn't have to re-earn her spot through the normal qualifying grind while also sorting out childcare, recovery, and a body that had just done something entirely different for nine months. The WSL effectively said: come back on a realistic timeline, and we'll hold the door open.

Most skate parents don't get an institution to do that for them. But you can do it for yourself. The instinct after a long gap — whether it's three months with a newborn or three years of a toddler eating every spare hour — is to treat the return as a test you either pass or fail immediately. Moore's tour didn't ask her to win her first heat back. It asked her to show up. That's a lower, more honest bar, and it's the one you should set for your own first few sessions back.

## She didn't wait for "ready"

Moore's path back wasn't a single leap from new mother to tour competitor. Reporting around her return described her easing back into daily surfing before entering smaller qualifying events in Hawaii ahead of the CT season — building the base pattern back before adding competition on top of it. That's the same staged shape this site has pointed to before in [the 8-week return plan for adult skaters](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/): rebuild the movement, then the volume, then the specific skill, then whatever "competing" means for you, whether that's a contest or just keeping up with the group session you used to skate with.

The mistake a lot of skate parents make is waiting for a feeling of readiness that a new schedule simply doesn't produce. There is no morning where the kid is suddenly old enough and the laundry is suddenly done and you suddenly have the hip mobility you had in your twenties. Moore didn't wait for that morning either. She started with daily surfing — not competing, just moving — and let the rest build on top of it.

## The schedule change is permanent, and that's fine

Here's where the comparison has to be honest: Moore has a nanny, a team, and a sponsor-funded life built around getting her to the right beach on the right day. Most skate parents are negotiating nap schedules and one shared car. The gap between her logistics and yours is real, and no amount of inspirational framing closes it.

What transfers isn't the resources. It's the posture toward the problem. Moore's old schedule — the one where she could surf whenever a swell showed up — is gone permanently, not temporarily. She didn't get that version of her life back; she built a new one that fits around her daughter. [Finding skate time with a family at home](/blog/finding-skate-time-with-a-family-at-home/) is this site's version of that same negotiation: identifying the thirty-minute windows that actually exist in your week instead of mourning the open afternoons you used to have.

## What this looks like at your park

You're not rebuilding toward a World Surf League event. You're rebuilding toward whatever "back" means for you — a Saturday session, a group you used to show up for, a trick you could do before the gap. The practical version of Moore's comeback for a skate parent looks like this:

**Pick a floor, not a ceiling.** "I will push around the block twice a week" is a floor you can actually hit most weeks. "I will get back to where I was" is a ceiling that keeps moving and rarely gets touched.

**Let the first sessions be boring.** Moore's early sessions back were daily surfing, not contest heats. Your early sessions back should be flat-ground pushing and easy carves, not the line you were working on before the gap.

**Treat the new schedule as the real one.** The nap window, the daycare pickup time, the one evening your partner has coverage — that's not a temporary obstacle between you and your old skating life. It's the shape your skating life has now, and planning around it instead of against it is what actually gets you back on a board more than once a month.

**Stop comparing your return to your pre-kid baseline.** Compare this month to last month instead, the same way [building confidence after 40](/blog/building-confidence-at-40/) argues for comparing yourself to yourself rather than to who you used to be.

Moore's first event back is a season opener on the other side of the world from most of us, at a beach break most of us will never surf. None of that is the point. The point is that a five-time world champion, with every resource a comeback could ask for, still built her return the slow way — daily practice before competition, a staged rebuild before a real test. If that's the approach for someone with a team behind her, it's more than good enough for someone building a comeback around a crib and a car seat.
