At 46, my instinct when I came back to skating was to train the way I did at 19: show up, skate as hard and as long as I could, repeat the next day if I could still walk. It worked for about three weeks before my body sent that plan back with a note attached — sore knees, a tweaked lower back, and a level of fatigue that didn't clear overnight the way it used to.

What eventually worked was borrowing a framework from a completely different corner of sport: masters athletes. Competitors well into their 40s and 50s who are still performing at a genuinely high level, across endurance sports, cycling, surfing, and beyond, don't train more than they did at 25. They train according to a different set of ratios. That distinction — not "harder" but "smarter" — is the whole idea, and it translates directly to skating.

## The core shift: recovery is part of the training, not the absence of it

The mental model most of us carry over from being young is that skating is the training and rest is what happens in between sessions, a kind of dead time you tolerate until you can skate again. Masters athletes flip that. Recovery is treated as an active component of the plan, not a gap in it — because the adaptation that actually makes you better happens during recovery, not during the session itself. Skate hard without adequate recovery and you're not accumulating fitness, you're accumulating fatigue that eventually shows up as an injury or a plateau.

This matters more with age because recovery capacity itself changes. The same volume of skating that took 24 hours to bounce back from at 20 might take 48 at 45. That's not weakness, it's physiology — and a training plan that ignores it isn't being tougher, it's being inefficient.

## The 80/20 principle, applied to skating

A framework masters endurance athletes use widely is roughly 80% of training time at low, comfortable effort and 20% at genuinely hard effort — not the even split most casual athletes assume is optimal. Applied to skating, that means the bulk of your sessions should be easy: flatground pushing, mellow carving, working on technique you've already got, staying well within your comfort zone. The smaller slice of harder effort — trying something new, pushing pace in a bowl, a session where you're genuinely working — is what drives adaptation, but only if it's actually a minority of what you do.

The common mistake is running every session at high effort because that's what feels productive. It isn't. It's the fastest route to the fatigue-and-injury cycle that ends a lot of adult comebacks early.

## A concrete weekly framework

You don't need a rigid schedule, but having some structure beats "skate whenever I feel like it, as hard as I feel like it." A framework that works for most returning adults with a handful of sessions a week:

**One skill-focused session, kept short.** 30–45 minutes, high concentration, working on one specific thing — a trick, a line, a technique correction. Short and sharp beats long and unfocused; skill work degrades fast once you're tired, so stop while you're still sharp rather than grinding on when quality drops.

**One or two easy flow sessions.** Longer, lower-intensity time on the board — cruising, mellow carving, things you're already comfortable with. This is the bulk of your 80%, and it's where a lot of the real conditioning and comfort-on-the-board happens without the wear cost of pushing hard.

**One genuinely hard session, if your week has room for it.** This is where you push pace, try something at the edge of your ability, or spend real time in a bowl or transition. Treat it like the smaller, deliberate piece of the plan it is — not the default mode for every session.

**At least one full rest or active-recovery day between anything demanding.** Walking, stretching, mobility work — movement, but not skating. This is where the adaptation from your harder sessions actually consolidates.

## Autoregulation: the plan adjusts to you, not the other way around

The other principle masters athletes lean on heavily is autoregulation — adjusting a session based on how you actually feel that day rather than blindly following a fixed plan. Woke up stiff, slept badly, still feeling yesterday's session in your legs? That's a signal to shift a planned hard session into an easy one, not a reason to push through out of stubbornness. The plan is a framework, not a contract, and the athletes who sustain training for decades are the ones who treat it that way.

This is a harder habit to build than it sounds, because it runs against the instinct that skipping a hard session is a failure of discipline. It isn't. Training through fatigue you should have respected is what actually derails progress — an overuse injury costs you weeks, where one adjusted session costs you nothing.

## Building in deload periods

Masters athletes also plan lighter weeks deliberately, rather than waiting until something breaks to force one. Every four to six weeks, scale back volume and intensity across the board for several days to a week — shorter sessions, all easy effort, extra rest days. It feels like losing ground in the moment. It isn't. It's what lets you sustain the pattern for years instead of riding a boom-and-bust cycle of hard training followed by an injury-forced break.

## The long game

None of this is about training less out of caution — it's about training in a way that actually holds up over years instead of weeks. The adult skaters I see stick with this longest aren't the ones who go hardest in any single session. They're the ones who've internalized that recovery, restraint, and listening to what their body is telling them on a given day are just as much a part of getting better as the skating itself.
