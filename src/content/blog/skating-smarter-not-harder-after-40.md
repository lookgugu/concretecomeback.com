Getting the schedule right was only half the fix when I came back to skating at 46. [How many days a week, how much rest, how much sleep](/blog/recovery-days-for-adult-skaters/) — I sorted that out early and it helped. What kept wrecking me anyway was a different mistake: I ran every single session at the same high effort, treating "easy day" as a concept for other people. Three sessions a week of going as hard as I could sent my body the same overload signal as skating every day would have.

What eventually fixed that piece was borrowing a framework from a completely different corner of sport: masters athletes. Competitors well into their 40s and 50s who are still performing at a genuinely high level, across endurance sports, cycling, surfing, and beyond, don't train more than they did at 25. They train according to a different set of ratios — not how many days, but how hard each one actually is. That distinction — not "harder" but "smarter" — is what this post is about, and it's the layer that sits on top of getting your rest days right in the first place.

## The core shift: recovery is part of the training, not the absence of it

The mental model most of us carry over from being young is that skating is the training and rest is what happens in between sessions, a kind of dead time you tolerate until you can skate again. Masters athletes flip that. Recovery is treated as an active component of the plan, not a gap in it — because the adaptation that actually makes you better happens during recovery, not during the session itself. Skate hard without adequate recovery and you're not accumulating fitness, you're accumulating fatigue that eventually shows up as an injury or a plateau.

This matters more with age, though the evidence is thinner than the folk wisdom suggests. A 2008 review in the *Journal of Aging and Physical Activity* (Fell and Williams) found that older athletes consistently report slower recovery, and laid out plausible reasons for it — older muscle appears more susceptible to exercise-induced damage and slower to repair — while noting how few controlled studies actually compare trained older and younger athletes. In practice, the session that took a day to bounce back from at 20 may take two at 45. Planning for that isn't weakness, and a plan that ignores it isn't tougher, just less efficient.

## The 80/20 principle, applied to skating

The 80/20 split comes from sports physiologist Stephen Seiler's work on how elite endurance athletes actually train: across rowers, cyclists, runners and cross-country skiers, roughly 80% of sessions are done at low, comfortable effort and about 20% at genuinely hard effort, with very little in between (his 2010 review in the *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance* is the standard summary). That research was done on elite competitors rather than masters athletes specifically, but it's the pattern masters endurance coaching has since built on — and it's not the even split most casual athletes assume is optimal. Applied to skating, that means the bulk of your sessions should be easy: flatground pushing, mellow carving, working on technique you've already got, staying well within your comfort zone. The smaller slice of harder effort — trying something new, pushing pace in a bowl, a session where you're genuinely working — is what drives adaptation, but only if it's actually a minority of what you do.

The common mistake is running every session at high effort because that's what feels productive. It isn't. It's the fastest route to the fatigue-and-injury cycle that ends a lot of adult comebacks early.

## A concrete weekly framework

You don't need a rigid schedule, but having some structure beats "skate whenever I feel like it, as hard as I feel like it." A framework that works for most returning adults with a handful of sessions a week:

**One skill-focused session, kept short.** 30–45 minutes, high concentration, working on one specific thing — a trick, a line, a technique correction — at a physical intensity closer to your easy effort than your hard one. Skill work is mentally demanding, not necessarily physically hard; a slow, deliberate rep at low speed teaches the same movement as a fast one without spending your harder-effort budget. Short and sharp beats long and unfocused; skill work degrades fast once you're tired, so stop while you're still sharp rather than grinding on when quality drops.

**One or two easy flow sessions.** 45–60 minutes each, lower-intensity time on the board — cruising, mellow carving, things you're already comfortable with. Together with the low-intensity skill session above, this is where the bulk of your week — the 80% — actually lives, and it's where a lot of the real conditioning and comfort-on-the-board happens without the wear cost of pushing hard.

**One genuinely hard session, if your week has room for it.** 20–30 minutes of real hard effort within a longer session — this is where you push pace, try something at the edge of your ability, or spend real time in a bowl or transition. Keep that hard-effort chunk to roughly a fifth of your weekly time, the smaller, deliberate piece of the plan it's meant to be — not the default mode for every session.

**Rest days from the baseline you've already set.** This is where [recovery days for adult skaters](/blog/recovery-days-for-adult-skaters/) does the heavy lifting — how many days a week, sleep, mobility work between sessions. The one addition here: put your rest day right after the hard session, not the easy ones, since that's where the adaptation actually needs to consolidate.

## Autoregulation: the plan adjusts to you, not the other way around

The other principle masters athletes lean on heavily is autoregulation — adjusting a session based on how you actually feel that day rather than blindly following a fixed plan. Woke up stiff, slept badly, still feeling yesterday's session in your legs? That's a signal to shift a planned hard session into an easy one, not a reason to push through out of stubbornness. The plan is a framework, not a contract, and the athletes who sustain training for decades are the ones who treat it that way.

This is a harder habit to build than it sounds, because it runs against the instinct that skipping a hard session is a failure of discipline. It isn't. Training through fatigue you should have respected is what actually derails progress — an overuse injury costs you weeks, where one adjusted session costs you nothing.

## Building in deload periods

Masters athletes also plan lighter weeks deliberately, rather than waiting until something breaks to force one. Every four to six weeks, scale back volume and intensity across the board for several days to a week — shorter sessions, all easy effort, extra rest days. It feels like losing ground in the moment. It isn't. It's what lets you sustain the pattern for years instead of riding a boom-and-bust cycle of hard training followed by an injury-forced break.

## The long game

None of this is about training less out of caution — it's about training in a way that actually holds up over years instead of weeks. The adult skaters I see stick with this longest aren't the ones who go hardest in any single session. They're the ones who've internalized that recovery, restraint, and listening to what their body is telling them on a given day are just as much a part of getting better as the skating itself.
