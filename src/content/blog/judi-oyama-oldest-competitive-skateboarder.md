Judi Oyama is 66 years old, holds the Guinness World Record for oldest competitive skateboarder, and is currently training in Santa Cruz, California for the World Skate Games in Paraguay this October, where she'll both race and coach. She started competing professionally in 1975. She has been inducted into the Skateboarding Hall of Fame. And she set her current Guinness record at 64 — then went out and broke her own record since.

If you're 45, 55, or 65 and wondering whether your window for this sport has already closed, Oyama's career is the most direct answer available: no, and here's the discipline that proves it.

## The discipline matters as much as the person

Oyama competes in slalom skateboarding — racing a course of cones on a longboard, turning around each one as tightly and quickly as possible. It's easy to mentally file all skateboarding under "street and vert," the highest-impact, highest-fall-risk end of the sport. Slalom is a different animal.

There's no stair set, no rail, no vert ramp. The skill is carving — reading a line, shifting weight smoothly from edge to edge, generating speed through technique rather than through big, high-consequence tricks. The falls that happen in slalom tend to be lower-speed slides rather than the kind of impacts that come from missing a trick eight feet in the air. That doesn't make it risk-free — it's still a wheeled sport on a moving surface — but it's a legitimately different risk profile than most people picture when they hear "competitive skateboarding."

This matters for anyone reading this site. If your knees, back, or general risk tolerance make street skating's ledges and stairs feel like the wrong call at your age, slalom is a place where decades of competitive skaters — Oyama very much included — keep getting faster and more skilled well past the age most sports consider a career over.

## What she's actually training for

The World Skate Games slalom competition in Paraguay runs across three days in mid-October, with a practice day beforehand. Oyama is going as a competitor and a coach — she's not attending as a novelty entrant marking an anniversary, she's going to race against the field, including skaters decades younger.

That's the detail worth sitting with. This isn't "remarkable for her age" in the sense of completing a lap. It's competing on the same course, under the same conditions, for the same result, as everyone else on the start line. Age-adjusted categories exist in a lot of masters sports; Oyama's record is for going head-to-head in the open competitive field.

## What actually transfers to you

You are very likely never going to race at a World Skate Games. That's fine — almost nobody who reads this site is trying to. What transfers isn't the competitive outcome. It's the pattern underneath it.

**Longevity in this sport comes from picking the right discipline for your body, not from picking the discipline first and adapting your body to it.** Oyama didn't spend her fifties and sixties forcing herself to keep landing the high-impact tricks of her youth. Slalom rewards decades of accumulated technique — reading a course, carrying speed efficiently, controlling a board precisely — in a way that doesn't punish an older body the way repeated hard landings would.

If you're coming back to skating and gravitating toward flat ground, cruising, or carving rather than stairs and rails, that's not a compromise or a lesser version of "real" skating. It's the same instinct that's kept a 66-year-old competing at the top of her sport: match the discipline to what your body can sustain for years, not what looks impressive in a single session.

**Consistency compounds longer than intensity does.** Fifty years of skating, even with the inevitable gaps, adds up to a level of technique that a few intense years never could. If you're 47 and just getting back on a board, you are not behind Judi Oyama's clock. You're at the start of your own version of the same long game she's been playing since 1975.

## If slalom sounds interesting

You don't need a competitive longboard or a cone set to try the basic movement. Most skateparks have flat, open stretches of smooth concrete where you can practice carving turns at low speed — the same weight-shift, edge-to-edge motion slalom is built on, without a timer or a course. It's a genuinely good complement to the technique work in our [beginner trick ladder](/blog/beginner-trick-ladder-for-adult-skaters/), and a lower-impact place to build confidence than pushing straight into stairs or rails.

Oyama's story isn't inspiring because it's rare. It's useful because it's a real, ongoing data point: the ceiling on what an older body can do on a skateboard is much higher, and much later, than most of us assume before we see someone actually doing it.
