Most of the profiles on this site are about people who stepped away from skating and came back later in life. Dallas Oberholzer's story is different, and arguably more useful to readers of this site: he never stopped, he kept getting better into his forties, and at 46 he competed against skaters half his age at the Olympics. He's now in his early fifties and still at it — just not in the way you'd expect from someone who's already had an Olympic career.

## From Durban to Tokyo

Oberholzer was born in Durban, South Africa, in 1975, and started skateboarding in 1985, as a kid in a country where the sport was a fringe activity with almost no infrastructure behind it. He stuck with it through school and into adulthood, eventually earning a business and commerce degree from the University of Natal, Pietermaritzburg, in 1996 — skating the whole time as something closer to a lifelong habit than a career plan.

That habit turned into an Olympic berth in 2021, when skateboarding made its Olympic debut at the Tokyo Games. Oberholzer competed in the men's park event at 46 years old — the second-oldest competitor in the field, behind Denmark's Rune Glifberg — and finished 20th. For a sport whose competitive scene skews overwhelmingly toward teenagers, an Olympic appearance in your late forties is its own kind of statement: not a nostalgia act, but a genuine qualifier going head-to-head with athletes a third his age.

## The part that mattered more than the contest

The Olympics made Oberholzer briefly famous outside skateboarding. The more lasting part of his story started almost two decades earlier and has nothing to do with contest results. In 2002, he founded what's now known as the Indigo Youth Movement — a nonprofit that uses skateboarding as a way to reach kids in under-resourced South African communities, originally built around teaching the sport itself and evolving, over years of running it, into something closer to a life-skills and leadership program that happens to use a skateboard as the entry point. The organization has since built multiple skateparks and ramps in communities that otherwise had none, giving kids a place to skate that didn't exist before.

That work has continued well past his Olympic run. In February 2025, Oberholzer was among the coaches giving free skateboarding lessons to fifty young people in Langa, a township outside Cape Town, through a City of Cape Town initiative — sessions organizers described as surfacing real local demand for a structured, ongoing skate program in the area. Two decades after founding Indigo, he's still the guy showing up to teach a group of kids who've never stood on a board before.

## Why this matters for readers coming back in their 40s and 50s

It would be easy to read an Olympic skateboarder's story and feel like it has nothing to say to someone relearning to carry speed into a bank at 47. But the part of Oberholzer's story worth sitting with isn't the competition result — it's what an entire adult life built around skateboarding can look like when it isn't measured purely by how high you can still ollie.

He's proof that skateboarding doesn't have an expiration date built into its competitive structure the way some sports do, even at the sport's highest level. But the more directly useful lesson is about what skating gives back once you've been doing it long enough: a framework for building something durable around the sport rather than just a stream of personal milestones. Oberholzer didn't keep skating into his 50s purely to chase his own progress — a lot of his last two decades on a board have been spent handing the sport to kids who'd otherwise never have access to it.

That's a version of "staying in it" that most returning adult skaters can actually use, whether or not competing is anywhere on the radar. The [guide to finding other adult skaters](/blog/finding-other-adult-skaters/) on this site is aimed at the same instinct on a much smaller scale: the sport holds up better, and means more, once it's connected to a community rather than kept as a solitary habit. Oberholzer just happened to build his community from scratch, in neighborhoods that had nothing, and kept building it for over twenty years after most athletes would have called it a career.

## The takeaway

You don't need an Olympic berth to take something from this. The specific thing worth borrowing is the shape of a skating life that doesn't plateau into nostalgia once competitive relevance fades — one where the sport keeps generating new reasons to show up, whether that's your own progress, a session with friends, or, in Oberholzer's case, a kid in Langa standing on a board for the first time. At 51, he's still doing the same thing he was doing at 27: finding a reason to be at the park.
