A patient asked me recently whether she should get a human coach or just use one of the AI apps she'd seen advertised — upload a clip, get feedback on what's wrong with your kickflip, done. It's a fair question for anyone training alone, which describes most adult returners: you don't have a crew watching you, you don't have a coach cueing you in real time, and a phone propped against a backpack is a lot more available than a scheduled lesson.

I went and looked at what's actually out there before answering her, because "AI coaching" is one of those phrases that can mean something genuinely useful or very little at all, depending on the product.

## What exists right now

There's a real category of consumer apps aimed at this exact problem. **SkateAI** lets you upload a clip of a trick and get a frame-by-frame breakdown with notes on what happened; its paid tier adds ongoing coaching and progress tracking over time. **SkateGPT** works similarly — you record a trick, it analyzes the footage and flags what you did right and what to adjust. **Fervid** takes a different approach entirely: it's text-based, offering trick guidance through conversation rather than video review.

These aren't vaporware. They're real, available apps with real users. What I couldn't find, and want to be upfront about, is independent testing of how accurately any of them actually detect a technique fault versus a human coach watching the same clip. Every claim about accuracy or "coaching science" I found came from the company's own app store listing. That doesn't make the claims false. It means they're unverified, and you should treat them the way you'd treat any supplement label: plausible, not proven.

## What video analysis can plausibly do well

A camera doesn't get tired, doesn't get self-conscious on your behalf, and doesn't mind watching the same failed attempt eleven times in a row. That's a genuine advantage over training alone with no feedback at all. Frame-by-frame breakdown is also something a phone does better than a memory does — you genuinely cannot tell, in the moment, whether your back foot was a half-second late on a kickflip, and a slowed-down clip can show you clearly.

For an adult who trains alone, that's not nothing. Consistency problems — the kind where a trick works four tries out of ten and you can't tell what's different about the ones that land — are exactly the kind of pattern a frame-by-frame review surfaces that in-the-moment self-assessment misses.

## What it almost certainly can't do

No app, however good its video model gets, can catch you. It can't read a crowded bowl and tell you to bail two seconds before a collision. It can't feel that your front knee is tracking wrong in a way that's building toward an injury rather than just looking slightly off on camera — a physiotherapist or an experienced coach watching live picks up on compensations your uploaded ten-second clip won't show, because the clip only exists if you already decided the attempt was worth filming.

That last point matters more for adult skaters specifically than it would for a sponsored teenager. A lot of what actually goes wrong for returning adults is cumulative load and poor movement patterns building toward something — not the kind of single visible error a trick-analysis app is built to flag. Software built to answer "why isn't my kickflip landing" isn't built to answer "is this knee angle going to be a problem in six weeks," and conflating the two is the actual risk of leaning on an app alone.

## Where it fits for someone like you

Use it as a logging and feedback layer on top of training you'd be doing anyway, not as a substitute for a human set of eyes on your safety and your joints. A reasonable split: let an app help you see what a trick attempt actually looked like and track whether you're trending in the right direction between sessions, and keep a periodic check-in with a coach or an experienced skating friend for technique questions and features that intimidate you. If you haven't found that person yet, [how to find a skateboard coach who gets adult beginners](/blog/finding-a-skateboard-coach-for-adults/) is the place to start. Pain, a new injury, or anything involving an existing injury history is a different category entirely — that belongs with a physiotherapist or doctor, not a coach, a friend, or an app, however good its video analysis gets.

The honest answer I gave my patient: try one if the idea appeals to you, treat whatever it tells you as a second opinion rather than a verdict, and don't let a subscription replace the one thing no app does yet — someone physically present who can tell you to stop before you get hurt, not after.
