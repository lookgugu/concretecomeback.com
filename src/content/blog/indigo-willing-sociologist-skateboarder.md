Most of the comeback stories on this site are about willpower — someone deciding, at 40 or 50, that the sport isn't finished with them yet. Dr. Indigo Willing's story has that same shape, but she also happens to be one of the people whose day job is studying, academically, why stories like hers keep happening. That double vantage point — skater and researcher of skaters — makes her worth a closer look.

## Who she is

Willing holds a PhD in Sociology from the University of Queensland and has worked as an adjunct research fellow with Griffith University's Centre for Social and Cultural Research. Her academic work focuses on gender equality within skateboarding — how skate scenes include or exclude people, and what actually builds a sense of belonging in a subculture that, for most of its history, wasn't built with women or older beginners in mind.

That's not a detached research interest. She picked up a skateboard herself for the first time at 41, as a mother, and has kept skating since. In 2022, writer Aaron Gilbreath profiled her for Oldster Magazine's newsletter in a piece titled ["Talking with 50-Year-Old Skateboarder Dr. Indigo Willing"](https://oldster.substack.com/p/talking-with-50-year-old-skateboarder) — a conversation about what it's like to be both a lifelong-late-starting skater and one of the people studying the culture from the inside.

## Building the community before writing about it

Willing's community-building work in skateboarding didn't start with research — it started with organizing. She co-founded We Skate QLD (originally Girls Skate Brisbane), one of Australia's longer-running inclusive skate groups aimed at women and beginners, and co-founded Consent Is Rad, an initiative addressing consent and safety culture within skate scenes — a subculture that, like a lot of physically close-contact, informally organized communities, hasn't always had good norms around this by default.

None of that came out of nowhere. Willing is a [Vietnam War orphan who was adopted and raised in Australia](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/fitting-in-was-a-challenge-for-vietnamese-orphan-indigo-so-she-created-her-own-community/e5f7766d-9112-47f7-b23b-3b65da726127), and years before she ever set foot on a skateboard, she founded Adopted Vietnamese International (AVI), a network connecting adoptees from that generation. She received a Medal of the Order of Australia in 2006 for that work. By the time she started skating in her 40s, in other words, she'd already spent two decades building exactly the kind of community infrastructure — networks for people who don't automatically have an obvious "in" to a group — that she'd go on to build again inside skateboarding.

## What the research actually adds

It would be easy to read Willing's story as just another "it's never too late" comeback account, and it is one of those. But her academic lens adds something a purely personal story can't: a vocabulary for why belonging in a skate scene is so specific, and so fragile.

Anyone who's shown up at a skatepark as an adult beginner has felt some version of what her research names directly — the sense of being watched, the unspoken hierarchy of who "belongs" on which part of the park, the difference between a park that's merely open to you and one that's actually built for you to stay. Willing's work argues that this isn't incidental to skate culture, it's structural, and it's exactly the kind of thing that deliberately organized groups like We Skate QLD exist to counter. A welcoming local scene isn't a nice bonus on top of skating — for a lot of returning adults, it's the difference between a first session and a habit.

## Two things that are both true

What makes Willing's position genuinely unusual isn't the age she started, though 41 is a fair answer to anyone wondering if there's a cutoff. It's that she's simultaneously the person inside the culture, doing the unglamorous work of running sessions and building networks, and the person outside it with academic tools to explain why that work matters. Most people doing one of those jobs don't do the other.

For readers of this site, the takeaway isn't "go get a sociology PhD before you skate." It's smaller and more actionable: the community side of getting back into skating — finding a group, or starting one if none exists near you — isn't a soft add-on to the sport. It's load-bearing. If you haven't found your version of a welcoming local session yet, our [guide to finding other adult skaters](/blog/finding-other-adult-skaters/) is a reasonable place to start looking, or to start building one yourself, the way Willing did.
