Not everyone coming back to skating as an adult can book a coach every week, and a lot of adults don't want one — showing up to a lesson feels like a bigger commitment than they're ready for. The next best thing, and one most people already own, is a phone. Used properly, video is genuinely useful self-coaching. Used carelessly, it's just a highlight reel that tells you nothing.

Here's the difference between the two.

## Why Video Matters More Now Than It Did as a Kid

As a teenager, you learned mostly by feel and by copying whoever was better than you at the park that day. That works, but it has a blind spot: what a movement feels like and what it actually looks like are often two different things, and adults are worse at closing that gap by feel alone than kids are. You've got more self-consciousness, more overthinking, and less raw repetition to smooth things out through trial and error.

Video removes the guesswork. It shows you the actual shape of your stance, the actual timing of your weight shift, the actual moment your knees do or don't bend — not what it felt like, which is often wrong.

## Camera Placement That Actually Shows Something

A phone lying on the ground or held shakily in one hand while you skate past captures almost nothing useful. A few setups do the real work:

**Side-on, waist height, perpendicular to your line of travel.** This is the single most useful shot for pushing, carving, and stance. Prop the phone against something stable — a backpack, a low wall, a cheap tripod — at roughly the height of your hips, aimed at the section of ground you'll be riding across.

**Behind or ahead of your line, for approach and commitment.** For anything involving a drop-in, a bank, or committing to a feature, a shot from in front of or behind your path shows whether your shoulders and hips are actually facing where you're going, which is hard to judge from inside your own body.

**Handheld only for wide context, never for technique.** A friend filming you moving through a whole session on their own phone is nice for morale, not for analysis. Save the technique review for the stationary setups.

## Slow Motion Is the Actual Tool

Most phones now shoot slow motion at well beyond the frame rate you need — more than enough to break a half-second movement into something you can actually study. This is where the real coaching value is. At full speed, the moment your front foot lands wrong, or your weight arrives a beat late, or your knees stay locked through a bend, is invisible. Slowed down, it's obvious.

Film the same movement a few times in slow motion before you decide what you're seeing. One clip can be an outlier — a bad rep, a gust of wind, a moment you were distracted. A pattern across three or four clips is something worth acting on.

## What to Actually Look For

It's easy to watch your own footage and just judge whether it "looks good," which isn't useful feedback. Specific checkpoints work better:

- **On pushing:** is your front foot pointed roughly forward, and does your pushing leg fully extend, or is it cut short?
- **On carving:** are your shoulders and hips rotating together, or is your upper body twisting independently of your lower body?
- **On approaching a feature:** are your knees bending before impact, or are you arriving stiff-legged and absorbing everything through your back?
- **On balance:** where does your weight actually sit relative to where you think it sits? This is usually the biggest surprise in early footage — most adults are more weight-back than they realize.

Pick one or two checkpoints per session rather than trying to fix everything you notice at once. Trying to correct five things simultaneously usually means correcting none of them well.

## Building a Simple Routine

You don't need a production process. A workable version: film the same one or two movements — your push, your basic carve — every few sessions, from the same angle, at the same time in the session (after warm-up, not cold). Review it the same day while the physical sensation is still fresh enough to connect to what you're seeing. Keep clips loosely organized by date, even just in a phone album, so you can scroll back a month later and see the actual change rather than relying on memory, which flatters you less reliably than footage does.

## What Video Coaching Can't Replace

Self-filming is genuinely useful, but it has real limits. It can't spot you physically on a feature that scares you. It can't give you real-time correction mid-movement the way a coach standing next to you can. And it's a poor tool for anything genuinely high-consequence — a drop-in you're not confident about, a feature with real fall risk — where a second pair of eyes and hands matters more than a camera angle.

Use your phone for the technical, low-stakes work: stance, pushing mechanics, carving, the fundamentals you can safely repeat over and over. Save an actual coach, or at minimum an experienced friend spotting you in person, for anything where a bad rep has real consequences. The two aren't competing tools — video handles the volume work, a person handles the risk.
