If you've come back to skating as an adult woman and assumed events like this are for pros half your age, Exposure is the one to look at differently. It runs November 6–8 at Encinitas Community Park in California, and its divisions include intermediate and advanced skaters alongside open/pro — not an afterthought category, but a structural part of the event.

## What it actually is

Exposure is a skateboarding contest and festival for women, girls, trans and non-binary skaters, open to entrants from anywhere in the world, spanning bowl, street, and vert. It was founded in 2012 by Amelia Brodka — who went on to skate park for Poland at the Tokyo Olympics — and philanthropist Lesli Cohen, after the two noticed how few women were in skate events, media coverage, and the industry generally. The 2026 edition is the 15th, and the organisers bill it as the largest and longest-running women's skateboarding competition in the world. It's run by a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, admission is free, and the event doubles as a benefit for survivors of domestic violence — built specifically around visibility and participation for women in a sport that spent decades treating them as a footnote.

That framing matters for how you think about attending, competing, or just watching. This isn't a showcase built around a handful of elite athletes with everyone else as scenery. The division structure — open/pro, advanced, intermediate — exists because the event is organized around the idea that women's skateboarding includes more than its top tier.

## Who it's actually for

**If you skate at an intermediate level:** there's a division built for exactly that, and it's the entry point — there is no beginner division, so intermediate is the floor. You don't need contest experience or sponsor backing to enter. You need to be comfortable enough on the terrain you're entering to skate it in front of people, which is a different bar than "be great at it." Within each discipline you enter one division only, so pick the one that matches your skating now.

**If you have no interest in competing:** Exposure is also worth attending as a spectator. Watching a weekend of women's skateboarding across the whole range — intermediate skaters a season or two ahead of where you are, an advanced field, and the open/pro field at the top — is a different experience than watching a contest broadcast that only shows the best runs in the world. Recent editions have also run a free learn-to-skate clinic alongside the contest, which is the closest thing the weekend has to a beginner's on-ramp.

**If you're newer to the adult skating community generally:** events like this are one of the more reliable ways to meet other adult women who skate, particularly ones outside your immediate area. A shared weekend around a shared activity does more for building those connections than most other formats.

## What to actually do about it

**If you want to enter:** registration has run through The Boardr in recent years — check Exposure Skate's website directly for the current registration window and division requirements, since contest logistics like this change year to year and this post won't stay current on them. Decide which division fits your actual skating now, not the division you'd have entered fifteen years ago.

**If you want to attend and watch:** treat it as a trip worth planning around, not a maybe. Admission is free, so the cost is the travel. Encinitas in early November is workable weather for a weekend outdoors, and watching live skateboarding — especially the intermediate and advanced heats, where you can see skaters working through the same kind of progress you're working through — tends to be more motivating than watching it later on a phone screen.

**If you can't make it to California:** look for how the event is covered online in the days around it. Following specific skaters in the intermediate and advanced divisions, not just the eventual open/pro winners, gives you a more useful picture of what progress in this sport actually looks like at levels closer to your own.

## Why this is worth your attention specifically

A lot of the visibility skateboarding gets — Olympic coverage, X Games, the big street and vert names — skews toward a narrow slice of who's actually skating: young, elite, overwhelmingly male in the media coverage even when the field isn't. Events built specifically around women's participation, with real divisions below the top tier, are a corrective to that, not a niche sideshow.

If part of what's kept you from feeling fully part of this sport since coming back is not seeing yourself represented in what skateboarding usually shows you, an event like this is a concrete answer rather than an abstract one. It won't fix that feeling by itself. It's still worth seeing, entering, or following, because the representation gap closes a little every time an event like this gets attention.

## The larger point

You don't need to be good enough for Exposure's open/pro division to have a reason to care about it. The event's own structure — intermediate and advanced as real, named divisions rather than a courtesy category — is itself the argument that this sport has room for skaters who came to it, or came back to it, later and slower than a pro career would require.
