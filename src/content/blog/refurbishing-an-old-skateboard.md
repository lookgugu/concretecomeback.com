An old complete leaning in the corner of the garage looks like the simplest way back onto a skateboard. If that setup has been sitting since the early 2000s, the easy move is to spin the wheels by hand, bounce once on the deck, and assume it is ready to ride. Even if nothing is broken, a street setup from that era is often narrower—around 7.5 to 7.75 inches—with small, hard wheels that feel much twitchier than the [wider boards many older returners prefer today](/blog/what-board-size-for-adults/). More importantly, wood and urethane age quietly in storage, and a quick look from across the room won't tell you what has failed.

Before you judge the deck, peel off the old grip tape and unbolt both trucks. Grip tape loses its bite over the years anyway, and leaving it on hides surface cracks and peeling plies. Leaving the trucks bolted on hides the four mounting holes under each baseplate, which is where the wood takes the most stress.

With the bare deck in your hands, press along the edges, nose, tail, and mounting holes. Years of heat and humidity swings in a garage or basement can weaken the glue between the maple plies. If the board simply feels a little flat or has lost its old pop, that is a performance compromise rather than an immediate hazard. Any crack anywhere on the wood, any soft give, or any separation between the layers is a reason to retire the deck to the wall. A compromised deck can snap suddenly under your weight.

Metal trucks hold up to sitting far better than wood, so they are usually the part worth saving—if you take them apart first. The urethane bushings and the small urethane pivot cup inside the baseplate harden, crack, or crumble with age. A bad pivot cup lets the hanger rock loosely in the baseplate and makes steering unpredictable even when the kingpin nut is tight, so swap both the bushings and the pivot cups before you ride.

While the trucks are disassembled, check the metal itself. Make sure the kingpin isn't bent, cracked, or stripped, and check that the kingpin nut still tightens down firmly. Sight down each hanger and baseplate to see that nothing is bent or stress-cracked; light surface rust cleans off, but deep pitting or a crack means replacing the truck. Spin a wheel on each axle to check that the axle is straight and the threads and axle nuts hold tight, and replace worn mounting bolts.

Don't bother trying to save old bearings or twenty-year-old wheels. Bearings that have sat for years often develop rust or dried lubricant inside, and new bearings are cheap. Old urethane wheels also harden over time and can develop flat spots or small cracks around the core; press a thumbnail into the wheel, and if it feels overly hard or brittle, or shows any cracking, replace them.

Once you add up fresh grip tape, bearings, wheels, bushings, pivot cups, and hardware, rebuilding an old setup costs close to an entry-level complete, as our guide to [what skateboarding costs](/blog/how-much-does-skateboarding-cost/) breaks down. If the deck fails inspection, buying a new complete usually makes more sense than rebuilding around old trucks alone. And remember that a home check can only catch obvious damage—it cannot guarantee that twenty-year-old wood or metal won't fail. If you aren't sure what you are looking at, take the parts into a local skate shop for a second opinion, or start fresh on a new setup and keep the old deck for the memories.
