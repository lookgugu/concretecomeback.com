Mine was leaning behind a shelf of paint cans for close to twenty years before I pulled it back out. The graphic was still mostly there. Everything else needed a hard, honest look before I trusted it under me again.

If you've got an old board sitting in a garage, closet, or your parents' basement, it's worth tearing down before you either bin it or ride it as-is. Some parts age fine. Others age in ways you can't see from across the room, and finding out on the board is the wrong way to find out.

## Start with the deck

The deck is the part most likely to have quietly failed. Wood doesn't age gracefully in a garage — humidity swings, temperature changes, and just time all work against it.

Check for:

- **Delamination** — press along the edges and nose/tail. Any soft give, crackling sound, or visible separation between the layers of ply means the deck is compromised structurally, not just cosmetically.
- **Cracks**, especially near the truck mounting holes, where the board takes the most repeated stress.
- **Concave and flex that feel dead** — a deck can look intact and still have lost the snap and pop it had when new. That's not a safety issue, just a performance one, and it's a reasonable thing to accept on an old board if everything else checks out.

If the deck passes the flex and delamination check, it's usually fine to keep. If it fails either one, replace the deck. This isn't a part worth restoring — a cracked or delaminated deck can fail suddenly and without warning, and that's not a risk worth taking to save the sentimental value of the original wood.

## Bearings: replace them, don't debate it

Bearings sitting unused for years develop rust and dried-out lubrication, even if they look clean from the outside. They're also one of the cheapest parts on the board. There's no real case for trying to salvage old bearings — clean and re-lube them if you're curious, but plan to replace them regardless before you actually ride the board.

## Wheels: check the urethane, not just the tread

Old wheels often look fine and aren't. Urethane hardens and can develop flat spots or micro-cracking over years of sitting, especially if they were stored somewhere that got hot. Press a thumbnail into the wheel — if it feels noticeably harder or more brittle than a wheel you'd buy today, or if you see any cracking around the core, replace them. Flat-spotted or hardened wheels affect grip and roll in ways that matter more than they seem like they should, especially while you're relearning your balance.

## Trucks: usually salvageable, worth inspecting anyway

Trucks are metal, and metal holds up to sitting far better than urethane or wood. In most cases, old trucks are structurally fine. Still worth checking:

- **The kingpin and bushings** — bushings are rubber or urethane and can harden or crack just like wheels. They're a cheap swap and worth doing on principle if the board sat for more than a few years.
- **Rust on the hanger or baseplate** — surface rust is cosmetic and can be cleaned off. Pitting or rust that's eaten into the metal is a different story and is a reason to replace that truck rather than trust it.
- **Loose or stripped mounting hardware** — cheap to replace, not worth the risk of skating on it.

If the trucks pass those checks, they're one of the parts most worth keeping. A good set of trucks doesn't really go bad with age the way soft parts do.

## Grip tape: just replace it

Old grip tape loses its bite over years, and it's inexpensive enough that there's no real argument for keeping it. Peel it off, clean the deck surface, and put on a fresh sheet. This alone makes an old board feel dramatically more trustworthy underfoot.

## Bushings and hardware

Covered above with trucks, but worth repeating on its own: bushings are a small part that does a lot of work in how the board turns, and they're cheap enough that "when in doubt, replace" is the right call rather than trying to judge how much life is left in rubber you can't fully inspect by eye.

## What this actually costs you

You're not going to save much money restoring a board versus buying a new complete — new bearings, wheels, bushings, grip, and possibly a deck adds up close to what a budget complete costs. The case for refurbishing isn't really about money. It's about riding something with history, and about not sending a perfectly good set of trucks to a landfill because the deck under them gave out.

If the deck fails inspection and you're not attached to the original wood, the honest move is often a new complete rather than a rebuild — you'll get fresher everything for close to the same price. If the deck passes, a refurb is a satisfying, low-stakes project that gets you back on a board that means something, safely.

## The one rule that matters

Don't skip the inspection because the board "looks fine." Looking fine and being safe to ride are different questions, especially with wood and urethane that have been sitting for years. Take the twenty minutes to actually check each part before you take it to the park. It's a small step between a board that's been waiting in your garage and one you can trust under you.
