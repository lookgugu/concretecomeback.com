Everyone talks about gear when they talk about skating safely. Almost nobody talks about the thing gear is designed to work alongside: knowing how to fall.

Falling is not a failure state in skating. It's a regular, expected part of the sport, at every age and every skill level. What separates a fall that costs you nothing from a fall that costs you six weeks in a wrist brace is usually not luck — it's technique. And it's technique you can practice before you ever need it.

## Why this matters more after 40

At 20, an instinctive fall mostly just works out. Reflexes are fast, joints are forgiving, and the body absorbs mistakes it shouldn't have to. At 45 or 50, the same instinct — throwing a straight arm out to catch yourself — is exactly what turns a minor tumble into a fracture.

The most common serious injury among returning adult skaters is a broken wrist, and it almost always happens the same way: falling forward, arm out straight, palm down, full body weight landing through a locked joint. It's the single most natural thing to do when you feel yourself going down, and it's the worst possible response.

The good news is that the correct response is also learnable, and it doesn't take long to build the reflex.

## The core principle: get small, not stiff

The technique adult skaters need is close to what judo and gymnastics call a breakfall — the goal is to spread the impact across your body instead of concentrating it on one joint.

**Tuck, don't brace.** The instinct to stiffen up and brace for impact is exactly backwards. A stiff, locked body transfers force straight through to bone. A relaxed, tucked body absorbs and redirects it.

**Never a straight arm.** If your hands are going to make contact with the ground, let your elbows bend on impact. A bent arm absorbs force through muscle and motion. A straight arm transfers it directly into the wrist and the bones above it.

**Roll instead of stop.** If you're falling forward or sideways with any speed, let your body continue moving — roll through the shoulder and across the back rather than trying to stop dead. Skaters who "stick" a fall in place take far more force than skaters who roll out of it.

**Protect your head first, always.** If there's any chance of your head making contact, tuck your chin to your chest. This is the one instinct worth training above all others, and it's why the helmet rule is non-negotiable regardless of how good your falling technique gets.

## Practicing it before you need it

You don't need a skatepark to build this reflex. A few minutes on a soft surface — grass, a gym mat, even a carpeted floor — is enough to start training your body to respond correctly.

**Practice the tuck-and-roll from standing.** Crouch down, tuck your chin, and roll sideways across your shoulder onto your back, the way you'd fall in a backward tumble. Do this slowly, a few times, until it feels less foreign.

**Practice catching yourself on a bent arm.** From a kneeling position, fall forward slowly and catch yourself with bent elbows, letting your forearms take the load instead of your wrists. Feel the difference between that and a locked arm.

**Rehearse it mentally before every session.** Physios call this motor imagery — visualizing the correct movement primes your nervous system to actually perform it under stress. A minute of this before you push off is enough to make a real difference.

## What wrist guards change about all of this

Wrist guards don't replace this technique — they work alongside it. A rigid-splint wrist guard is built to stop your wrist from hyperextending if you do put a hand down, but it works best when you're also falling correctly. Wearing guards and still throwing a stiff, straight arm into the ground reduces the damage, but doesn't prevent it the way guards plus good technique does.

If you already wear wrist guards, this is still worth learning. If you don't yet, learning to fall well is a good reason to start wearing them today, not after the first bad one.

## When it actually happens

In the moment, you won't have time to think through a checklist. This is exactly why practice matters — the goal is to make tucking, rolling, and bending your arms an automatic response, not a conscious decision made mid-fall.

Most adult skaters who fall well aren't the most talented skaters at the park. They're the ones who took falling seriously as its own skill, early, before their first real spill forced the lesson on them. Take the ten minutes. Practice on grass. Your wrists will thank you.
