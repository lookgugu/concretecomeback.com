Almost every returning adult skater I see in clinic uses the same test to decide they're ready to get back on the board: does it hurt right now? If the answer is no, they go back to skating. It's the single most common mistake I see after an injury, and it's not because people are careless — pain-absence just feels like the obvious finish line. It isn't, and the gap between "doesn't hurt" and "actually ready" is where a lot of second injuries happen.

## Why pain-absence isn't the finish line

Pain is a useful warning system, but it's a lagging indicator, not a leading one. A joint can be pain-free at rest and during gentle daily movement while still lacking the strength, control, or range of motion to handle the sudden, unpredictable loads skating actually demands — a caught edge, an off-balance landing, a quick direction change to avoid something. Those are exactly the moments an under-prepared joint fails, and pain at rest tells you nothing about whether it will hold up under them.

This is why sports medicine generally has moved toward criteria-based return-to-play decisions rather than time-based or pain-based ones. Two people with the same injury can be ready at very different points, and the same person can feel pain-free weeks before they're actually ready to load the joint the way skating requires.

## The three things that actually predict readiness

### Range of motion

Compare the injured side to the uninjured one, through the joint's full normal range, without pain or a sense of tightness stopping you early. This sounds basic, but it's the piece people skip most often — a joint can feel fine through the small range of motion daily life requires while still being meaningfully restricted at the end ranges skating actually uses, like a deep crouch through a carve or full ankle flexion rolling over uneven ground.

### Strength symmetry

This is the one people trust least and need most. Compare the injured side's strength directly against the uninjured side — a single-leg calf raise to fatigue, a single-leg squat depth and control, a single-leg balance hold. You're not looking for perfection; you're looking for the injured side to be reasonably close to the uninjured one, generally within striking distance rather than obviously weaker. A joint that's visibly weaker on the injured side, even if it doesn't hurt, is a joint set up to compensate badly the first time it's asked to work hard.

### Mental readiness

This one gets dismissed as unscientific, and it shouldn't be. Hesitation at the exact moment of commitment — flinching before a drop, bailing early on a movement you're otherwise physically capable of — changes your body mechanics in ways that actually increase injury risk. An athlete who's physically recovered but still flinching is, in a very real sense, not fully recovered. If you notice yourself doing this, it's worth reading about [working through fear after a bad fall](/blog/fear-after-a-bad-fall/) alongside the physical return, not instead of it.

## Building your own version of this test

This self-check is scoped to common musculoskeletal complaints — a joint, muscle, or tendon that felt off and has since healed. It does not cover head injuries. If you hit your head or have any concussion symptoms — headache, dizziness, confusion, sensitivity to light — treat that as its own category entirely: symptoms settling down is not clearance to skate. Concussion return-to-sport needs a symptom-limited, graduated progression overseen by a clinician, not a home checklist, whatever your joints are telling you.

For the musculoskeletal case, you don't need a sports medicine lab to apply this. Before returning to full sessions after any joint or soft-tissue injury that kept you off the board for more than a couple of weeks, run through all three:

- Move the joint through its full range, comparing sides, with no pain or guarding at the end range
- Test strength side-to-side with a simple exercise that loads the injured area directly — calf raises for an ankle, step-downs for a knee, bridges for a hip, wrist push-ups or a plank hold for a wrist, a slow push-up or band press for a shoulder, a bent-arm hang or band curl for an elbow
- Notice, honestly, whether you flinch or hesitate imagining the specific movement that caused the injury in the first place

If any of the three is clearly off, that's your answer, even if the joint doesn't currently hurt. Waiting a bit longer and closing that gap is cheaper than reinjuring the same joint and starting the clock over — often from further behind than the first time.

## Even after you pass, ease back in

Passing this check means you're ready to start reloading the joint under real conditions, not that you should jump straight back into your pre-injury session intensity. Begin with flat ground and predictable movement, and only work back toward transition, drops, or anything demanding a sudden direction change once a session or two of easier riding confirms the joint holds up the way your checks suggested it would.

## When to bring in a professional

Treat the three checks above as warning signs, not a clearance you can grant yourself. Failing one tells you clearly that you're not ready. Passing all three is more limited than it feels — it rules out the obvious red flags, but a professional assessment covers ground this checklist can't: injury-specific diagnosis, actual stability testing rather than a felt sense of "not wobbly," proprioception, and functional tests built around the specific movement that hurt you. That gap matters most for anything beyond a mild strain — a sprain that never fully swelled down, a tendon that's been nagging for weeks, anything where you're not entirely sure how bad it was to begin with.

Get an actual clearance from a physiotherapist or your surgeon, rather than relying on this checklist alone, if you had a fracture, a joint that felt unstable, swelling that lasted more than a few days, a ligament or tendon injury that's taken more than a couple of weeks to feel normal, or you're returning after any surgery. This self-check is a reasonable sanity check for a minor, short-lived strain that's already resolved — it is not a substitute for a professional's eyes on anything more serious than that.

The instinct to trust "it doesn't hurt anymore" is understandable — it's the most obvious signal available, and for most of life, it's a perfectly good one. Skating just asks more of a joint than most daily activity does, which is exactly why it needs a better test than pain alone.
