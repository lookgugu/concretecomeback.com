I spent about six weeks stuck on the ollie the first time I tried to relearn it as an adult. Not stuck in the "still building confidence" way — stuck in the "I don't actually understand what my feet are supposed to be doing" way. What finally broke it open wasn't a session, or a friend, or a coach. It was a fifteen-second slow-motion clip that showed exactly when the back foot snaps and the front foot slides, frame by frame, on a loop I could watch as many times as I needed.

That's the thing nobody tells you about learning to skate as an adult: you probably don't have a crew of teenagers to absorb this stuff from by osmosis anymore, the way most of us did the first time around. Social media has quietly become the substitute coach for a lot of returning adults, and it's worth understanding what it's actually good for — and where it can work against you.

## Why it fills a real gap

When you were fifteen, you learned by watching whoever was better than you at the park that day, badly imitating them, and getting instant, informal feedback ("no, further forward"). Most adult returners don't have that anymore. We skate alone or with one or two other adults, at off-peak hours, without a built-in pool of slightly-better peers to copy.

Short-form video fills exactly that hole. A well-made tutorial clip is, in effect, a patient teenager who'll show you the same motion fifty times without getting bored. The slow-motion and frame-step features built into TikTok and Instagram are genuinely useful teaching tools, not just entertainment formats — you can see foot placement and timing that's invisible at full speed, in person, from ten feet away.

## What's actually worth your time

**Slow-motion breakdowns of a single trick.** Search the trick name plus "slow motion" or "breakdown" rather than just the trick name alone — you'll get more instructional content and fewer highlight clips.

**Longer YouTube tutorials for anything you're stuck on.** Short-form video is great for isolating one moment of a trick; it's bad at explaining the two weeks of prerequisite balance work that usually needs to happen first. YouTube's longer format is better for that context, and it's where most experienced coaches who post instructional content actually put their real explanations.

**Content made by and for adult beginners.** Search "adult beginner" or "over 40" alongside a trick or skill name. This corner of skate content has grown a lot in the last few years, and it's the difference between watching someone explain a manual at your actual starting point versus watching a 16-year-old who's been skating since age six make it look trivial. Creators like Oorbee Roy (@auntyskates), a Toronto mother of two who took up skateboarding at 43, are a good example of what this content looks like when it's made with an adult beginner's body and timeline in mind rather than a teenager's.

**Your own filmed attempts.** This one's easy to skip but it's the highest-value use of your phone at the park. Film yourself attempting the trick, then watch it next to a tutorial clip at the same speed. The gap between what you feel you're doing and what you're actually doing is usually bigger than you'd guess, and video closes it faster than any verbal cue.

## The comparison trap

Here's the part that actually derails people. Social media algorithms are optimized for engagement, not for showing you content that matches your skill level or your body. Left on autopilot, your feed will happily serve you a twelve-year-old landing a trick first try, right after you've spent forty minutes failing at the same thing. That's not useful information about your progress. It's just what the algorithm learned gets watched.

A few things help:

- **Curate deliberately.** Actively follow adult-skater accounts and unfollow or mute ones that only post highlight reels of prodigies. Your feed reflects your inputs more than you'd think, and it reshapes itself within a couple of weeks of consistent unfollowing.
- **Treat timelines as informational, not comparative.** A tutorial telling you a trick "usually takes a few weeks" is useful data. A comment section full of people who apparently landed it in one session is not a control group — it's a highlight reel of the people who bothered to post.
- **Notice when you're scrolling instead of skating.** It's easy to spend twenty minutes watching skate content as a substitute for actually pushing around on your board. Content is a supplement to sessions, not a replacement for them.

## What it can't replace

A video can show you the mechanics of a drop-in. It can't spot you the first time you try one, tell you your weight is too far back before you feel it, or talk you down when the fear response kicks in on the coping. For anything with real fall risk — drop-ins, grinds, anything on transition — pair what you learn online with an actual person nearby, whether that's an experienced friend, a local adult skate session, or a coach who works with adult beginners.

Used well, social media is one of the better tools available to a returning adult skater: patient, endlessly repeatable, and increasingly made by people who look like you rather than people who look like who you used to be. Used carelessly, it's a highlight reel that makes your very normal learning curve feel like failure. The difference is almost entirely in what you choose to follow.
