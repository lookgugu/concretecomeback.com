Every generation thinks 40 feels different than it used to, and every generation is partly right for the wrong reasons. But there's a real, physical version of that claim for the millennials now showing up in my clinic and at the skatepark, and it's worth taking seriously rather than waving off as generational grumbling: the joints I'm looking at on a 40-year-old today didn't get worn down the same way a 40-year-old's did a generation ago, and that changes what preparation actually looks like.

## A different kind of wear

Someone who turned 40 doing decades of manual trades work — construction, farming, factory lines — arrives with joints shaped by sustained, repetitive, often asymmetric physical load. That's its own injury profile, and it's the one most "returning to sport after 40" advice was quietly written around.

A lot of millennials hitting 40 now didn't get that kind of load. What they got instead was years of desk work, a phone held in one hand more often than the other, a laptop angled the same way every day, and long stretches of sitting broken up by sport in bursts rather than daily physical labor. That's a different stress pattern: less raw wear-and-tear, but more postural tightness, more asymmetry from habitual device use, and less of the baseline full-body conditioning that comes from a physically active working life. A body that's been mostly sedentary with occasional weekend intensity carries risk in a different place than one that's been steadily worked hard for twenty years.

Neither pattern is worse across the board. They're just different, and a generic "ease back in after 40" plan built around one doesn't automatically fit the other.

## The weekend-warrior history

The other piece of this is activity pattern, not just posture. A lot of people now in their late 30s and 40s grew up playing organized sport seriously through school and college, then mostly stopped once work and family took over — picking activity back up in short, intense bursts rather than maintaining it steadily. That history leaves you with decent underlying coordination and sometimes real past fitness, but joints and connective tissue that haven't been asked to do much in years. Tendons and ligaments adapt to load slowly, over months, regardless of how fit your heart and lungs still feel. If your last consistent athletic period was your 20s, your cardiovascular memory is ahead of your connective-tissue readiness, and that gap is exactly where injuries happen — not because you're unfit, but because what used to tolerate load gracefully hasn't had to in a long time.

## What this means for how you prepare

If your 40-year-old body has spent more time at a desk than on a job site, the prehab priority isn't the same as it would be for someone coming from manual labor. A few adjustments worth making:

**Address postural asymmetry before you address strength.** Years of one-sided device use and desk posture tend to produce specific, predictable tightness: rounded shoulders, a stiff thoracic spine, hip flexors shortened from sitting. On a skateboard, a stiff upper back limits the rotation you need to pump a turn or spot a landing, and tight hip flexors change how you crouch into a stance. A few minutes of thoracic rotation and hip-flexor work before you ever touch a board addresses something a purely strength-focused warm-up skips.

**Don't trust your cardio fitness as a proxy for joint readiness.** If you've kept up running, cycling, or the gym, your conditioning may be genuinely good. That's useful, but it doesn't mean your ankles and wrists are ready for the specific demands of rolling, carving, and catching a fall. Build tissue tolerance for skating specifically — the [prehab routine for adult skaters](/blog/prehab-for-adult-skaters/) is designed for exactly this gap between general fitness and sport-specific readiness.

**Expect your weak points to show up in different places than an older generation's did.** Where a manual-labor background often shows up as lower back and shoulder wear from repetitive lifting, a desk-and-device background more often shows up as wrist and neck stiffness and hip tightness from sitting. Pay attention to your own pattern rather than assuming you'll have your parents' trouble spots. [The orthopedic checklist for skaters over 40](/blog/orthopedic-checklist-for-skaters-over-40/) gives you a way to actually track that instead of guessing.

**Build the load gradually either way.** Whatever shaped your joints before 40, the basic rule doesn't change: tissue adapts to load slowly, and a sudden jump in skating volume is still the biggest injury risk regardless of your work history. A different starting point doesn't excuse skipping a gradual ramp-up.

## The upside nobody mentions

There's a genuine advantage buried in this, too, for someone whose joints are otherwise healthy. A body that's spent more time sitting than laboring hasn't accumulated the same cumulative joint wear a lifetime of manual work produces, and when what you're dealing with really is just tightness and deconditioning, both respond well to consistent, targeted work over a few months — often a higher ceiling than it feels like from the couch. That pattern doesn't hold for everyone, though. Osteoarthritis, an old injury that never fully rehabbed, or joints that have carried significant excess load for years can all be present under the same sedentary history, and none of those respond to "just get moving again" the way ordinary tightness does. A desk job tells you something about your posture and conditioning. It doesn't rule out a structural issue, and it's not a substitute for getting an unfamiliar or persistent symptom actually assessed.

The point isn't that one generation has it easier. It's that "listen to your body" is good advice that means something slightly different depending on what your body has actually been doing for the last twenty years. Know your own pattern, prepare for that pattern specifically, and the generic advice written for someone else's joints stops being a trap.
