I get asked about [collagen](/blog/collagen-peptides-tendon-recovery/) and magnesium regularly in clinic. Creatine comes up less often, which is a little backwards — it's the single most studied supplement in sports science, and recent research specifically on older adults gives it a stronger evidence base than most of what's actually trending.

This isn't a supplement that will help you land anything sooner. What it can do is support the strength training that makes your body more durable for skating — which, for most adults coming back to the sport, matters more than any single trick.

## What creatine actually does

Creatine is stored in muscle tissue and used to regenerate ATP, the molecule your muscles burn for quick, forceful effort — the kind of short, explosive output involved in a hard pop, an ollie, or catching yourself out of a wobble. Supplementing increases the amount of stored creatine available for that system, which is the mechanism behind most of its measured effects: slightly more capacity for repeated short bursts of effort, and — more relevantly here — better results from resistance training.

That second part is the one that matters for this audience. Creatine on its own doesn't build muscle. Paired with regular resistance training, it consistently helps people build more of it than resistance training alone.

## What the research in older adults actually shows

Supplement marketing has a habit of overselling thin evidence, so it's worth being specific about what's solid and what isn't.

**Lean mass and strength:** this is the well-supported part. Multiple reviews of randomized trials in older adults find that creatine combined with progressive resistance training produces greater gains in lean muscle mass and strength than resistance training by itself. This effect shows up fairly consistently across the research, which is more than can be said for most supplements marketed to this age group.

**Bone density:** this is the overstated part. Despite some of the current wellness coverage implying creatine supports bone health broadly, the trial evidence specifically on bone mineral density in older adults is weak to neutral — any benefit appears to be indirect at best, likely through the muscle and strength gains rather than a direct effect on bone itself. If bone health is your primary concern, [weight-bearing training](/blog/strength-training-muscles-adult-skaters-neglect/) and your doctor's guidance matter far more than this supplement does.

**Who it actually helps:** the benefit shows up specifically for people who are also doing resistance training. If you're not lifting anything regularly — and plenty of adult skaters aren't, between skate sessions and the rest of life — creatine alone isn't going to do much for you. It's an amplifier for a training stimulus, not a substitute for one.

## The practical basics

Creatine monohydrate is the form almost all of the research is based on, and it's the one worth buying — more expensive "advanced" forms don't have better evidence behind them. A typical maintenance dose is in the range of 3 to 5 grams daily, taken consistently; timing relative to your workout or skate session matters far less than most product marketing suggests. Some people do a short loading phase of higher daily doses for about a week to saturate muscle stores faster, but it's optional — skipping it just means it takes a few weeks longer to reach the same place.

Drink enough water. Creatine draws some water into muscle cells, and staying adequately hydrated is the main thing that keeps that from feeling uncomfortable.

## Who should talk to a doctor first

Anyone with existing kidney disease, or on medications that affect kidney function, should check with their doctor before starting creatine — the kidney concerns around creatine in healthy adults are largely overstated in casual conversation, but they're real enough for people with existing kidney issues that it's not worth guessing. The same goes if you're managing any chronic condition or taking regular medication; a quick conversation with whoever manages your care costs you nothing and rules out an interaction you might not think to ask about otherwise.

Mild, manageable side effects — some water retention, occasional stomach discomfort if you take a large dose all at once rather than splitting it — are the most common complaints, and splitting the dose or taking it with food usually resolves them.

## The honest summary

Creatine won't make you skate better by itself. What it does is make the resistance training you're already doing, or should be doing, somewhat more productive — and for an adult body managing [recovery](/blog/recovery-days-for-adult-skaters/) and trying to stay durable enough to keep showing up at the park for years, "somewhat more productive strength training" is a genuinely useful thing to have on your side. It's not magic, and it's not useless. It's a reasonable, well-studied supplement that does one specific job, for the specific group of people actually doing the training it supports.
