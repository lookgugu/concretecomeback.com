I watch it happen at our sessions a couple of times a month. A dad shows up with his kid, kid goes off to the bowl with friends, and the dad stands at the edge with a board under his arm — a board he clearly rides, because his shoes are worn through in the right spots and his pushing looks like muscle memory, not imitation. Ask him if he skates and he'll say something like "oh, not really, I just come out here with my son." Watch him for twenty more minutes and he'll push off, carve a lap, and look genuinely happy doing it.

That gap — between what someone visibly does and what they'll admit to being — is the whole subject of this post.

## "Skate dad" became a real category without most dads noticing

Somewhere in the last few years, "skate dad" turned into an actual identity with its own visibility: gift guides built around it, social accounts built around it, a recognizable aesthetic. That's a real shift in how the culture sees this. And yet a lot of the dads the label is describing still talk about their own skating like a footnote to their kid's hobby, as if the board under their arm is a prop for supervision rather than something they ride because they want to.

I don't think that's modesty, exactly. I think it's permission that hasn't caught up to the label yet.

## Why the half-apology shows up

Part of it is straightforwardly generational — a lot of these dads grew up with a version of skating that was coded as a teenager's thing, and claiming it as a 40-something feels like it needs a justification attached ("I'm just getting some exercise," "it's for bonding with my kid"). Part of it is that parenting itself comes with an unspoken rule that your own wants move down the list, and a hobby that's visibly just for you can feel like it's breaking that rule even when nobody's actually enforcing it.

And part of it, if I'm honest, is that "I skate with my son" is a safer sentence than "I skate." The first one has a built-in excuse. The second one is just a fact about who you are, and facts about who you are can be judged in a way that excuses can't.

## What changes when you drop the apology

Nothing about your actual riding changes the day you start calling yourself a skater instead of a guy who tags along. But a few things around it do.

**You stop waiting for permission to practice on your own.** If your skating only exists in the frame of "time with my kid," you don't have an obvious reason to go to the park without them, work on something on your own schedule, or take a session seriously enough to warm up properly first. Owning it as your own thing gives you a reason to show up solo sometimes, which is usually where real progress happens.

**Your kid gets to see you as a learner, not just a supervisor.** There's something genuinely valuable for a kid in watching a parent fall, get frustrated, and keep trying at something that isn't already mastered — it's a different lesson than watching a parent calmly manage their homework or their bedtime. "Dad who skates" models persistence in a way "dad who watches me skate" doesn't.

**You stop treating every session as a teaching opportunity.** This is the one I see trip people up most. Once you've decided your presence at the park is justified by your kid's development, it's hard to resist narrating, correcting, and coaching the whole time — which neither of you actually wants from every single session. [Skating with your kids without making it awkward](/blog/skating-with-your-kids/) covers that specific dynamic in more depth, but the short version connects directly here: owning your own skating gives you permission to just ride sometimes, instead of always supervising.

## This isn't really about the label

You don't need a hoodie that says "skate dad" or a TikTok account to make this real. The actual shift is small and happens in how you answer one question: when someone asks if you skate, do you say yes, or do you explain your kid first?

Say yes. Not instead of being a dad who skates with his kid — in addition to it.
