A patient in his late forties once asked me, half-joking, whether he should get his knees scanned before he started skating again — "just to see what I'm working with." I used to think that question came from unnecessary anxiety. Having read more of the imaging research since, I think it's actually a reasonable instinct, even if the answer isn't a blanket "yes, go get scanned."

Here's what the evidence actually shows, and what it should change about how you think through a comeback.

## The finding that surprises people

A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis published in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* pooled data from dozens of studies — more than five thousand knees across several thousand adults — looking at cartilage defects visible on MRI in people with no knee pain and no history of injury. In adults under 40, cartilage defects showed up in roughly one in ten knees. In adults 40 and older, that number jumped to somewhere around four in ten.

Read that again: a large share of pain-free, uninjured adults past 40 are walking around with visible cartilage damage they have no symptoms of and no reason to suspect. Individual studies vary quite a bit around that figure — some report lower rates, a few report considerably higher ones, largely depending on scan strength and how strictly "damage" gets defined — but the direction is consistent across the research: cartilage wear is common, it accumulates with age, and pain is a poor indicator of whether it's present.

## Why this doesn't mean what it sounds like it means

The instinctive reaction to that statistic is alarm — "my knees might already be damaged and I'm about to put more stress on them." That reaction, while understandable, gets the implication backwards for two reasons.

**Pain-free cartilage changes are often simply part of aging joints, not an active injury in progress.** A defect that's been stable for years, causing no symptoms, isn't the same thing as a fresh tear that's actively deteriorating. The research here is describing prevalence, not predicting imminent failure. Plenty of people carry these findings for decades without them ever becoming symptomatic.

**Structural findings on a scan correlate surprisingly weakly with pain and function.** This is one of the more counterintuitive and well-established points in orthopedic imaging research generally: how a joint looks on MRI and how it actually performs for the person attached to it are two different questions, and the second one matters more for deciding what you can safely do. Plenty of people with meaningfully abnormal-looking scans function completely normally, and plenty of people with pain have unremarkable scans. A picture of your cartilage doesn't replace an honest assessment of how your knee actually behaves under load.

## So should you get a baseline scan?

For most returning adult skaters without current symptoms, no — not as a routine step before starting. An MRI is expensive, not always covered for a screening purpose with no symptoms driving it, and given how common incidental findings are, a scan is more likely to hand you something to worry about than something actionable to change your plan with. Worrying about a defect that was never going to bother you is its own real cost.

Where a scan does make sense is if you already have specific symptoms — persistent swelling, a catching or locking sensation, pain that doesn't track with activity in a way you can explain, or a knee that feels unstable rather than just achy. Those are reasons to see a doctor and let them decide whether imaging adds anything, not reasons to self-refer for a scan on your own initiative.

## What to actually do with this information

The useful response to "cartilage wear is common after 40" isn't a scan. It's a shift in how you build your return.

**Load gradually, and mean it.** Cartilage, unlike a ligament or a muscle, doesn't have much of a direct blood supply and adapts to load more slowly than other tissues. That's an argument for the same gradual buildup this site recommends for everything else — progressive loading over weeks, not a first session that jumps straight to drop-ins — but it's worth knowing that cartilage specifically is one of the slower-adapting tissues you're asking to keep up.

**Pay attention to how a knee feels the next day, not just during the session.** Cartilage irritation often shows up as next-day stiffness or mild swelling rather than sharp pain in the moment. If a knee feels notably different the day after a session compared to how it felt during it, that's worth noting and adjusting for, not ignoring because "it didn't hurt at the time."

**Don't let the statistic become a reason not to start.** This is the part I actually want to land: the research shows cartilage changes are common in people who have no idea they have them and experience no limitation from them. That's an argument for sensible loading, not for sitting out. Skateboarding, done with the kind of gradual build this site recommends, is weight-bearing and joint-friendly in ways that compare reasonably well to higher-impact sports — avoiding it out of fear of a defect you probably don't even know you have is very likely a worse trade than skating it sensibly.

**If you're already managing a diagnosed cartilage issue, this is different advice.** Everything above is about asymptomatic, undiagnosed wear in the general population. If you have an actual diagnosis — a known tear, documented osteoarthritis, a joint your doctor has already flagged — that's a conversation to have directly with your physiotherapist or orthopedist about what load your specific knee can handle, not something to extrapolate from a population statistic.

## The honest takeaway

Most adults past 40 are carrying some cartilage wear they don't know about, and most of them are fine. That's not a reason for alarm, and it's not a reason to skip medical attention if something actually feels wrong. It's a reason to build your comeback the way this site already recommends — gradually, paying attention to how your joints respond, and without assuming pain-free means damage-free, or that damage-you-don't-feel means damage you need to fix before you start.
