Pushing, pumping a transition, and stepping off a run all ask the knee to bend under weight. Returning to a board after forty often brings up a practical question before the first session: after decades of everyday wear, should you get your knees scanned first to see what is going on inside?

Answering that question starts with what knee imaging actually measures — and why a finding on an MRI is not the same thing as a symptomatic injury.

## What knee MRI studies find in pain-free adults

A [2019 systematic review and meta-analysis by Adam Culvenor and colleagues in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine*](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/20/1268) pooled data from 63 studies covering 5,397 knees in 4,751 adults who had no knee pain and no history of knee injury. Across those pain-free groups, MRI showed cartilage defects in 11% of knees in adults under 40 and 43% in adults aged 40 and older. Meniscal tears appeared in 4% of pain-free knees under 40 and 19% at 40 and above.

Two limits of that review matter before applying it to skateboarding. First, prevalence varied across individual studies depending on scanner strength and how strictly researchers graded minor surface changes, and the pooled data captured one-time snapshots rather than tracking what happened if those adults started an impact sport. Second, an MRI records tissue appearance, not pain or function. Seeing worn cartilage in over four out of ten pain-free knees past 40 shows that structural wear often accumulates quietly with age without causing symptoms. Conversely, a painful knee can look unremarkable on a scan.

## Imaging versus symptoms

Because incidental cartilage wear is so common after 40, ordering a routine "baseline" MRI on a pain-free knee rarely helps plan a return to skating. Whether imaging is useful depends on a physical examination and clinical history, not age alone. Without symptoms to explain, a scan often turns up an age-related change that needs no treatment while creating unnecessary hesitation around normal movement.

What warrants seeing a doctor or physiotherapist before you skate is how the knee behaves, not a birthday. Get the joint examined if you notice persistent swelling, locking or catching mid-bend, a feeling that the knee is giving way under weight, or pain that stays sharp or worsens as you move. Those signs are reasons for a clinical assessment — where a professional can decide if imaging would change your care — rather than a checklist for self-diagnosis. ## Building load sensibly

For an older skater whose knees feel and move normally, the high rate of silent cartilage wear in population studies is neither proof of harm nor a guarantee of safety under skateboarding impact. Building session length and impact gradually is a prudent approach when returning to the board, rather than jumping straight into heavy drop-ins or stair sets.

When you add board time, pay attention to how your knees feel the day after a session as well as during it. A quiet morning doesn't prove that cartilage load was ideal, since structural changes can exist without pain, but new next-day swelling or stiffness that doesn't ease after warming up is a useful sign to scale back volume and get lingering symptoms checked.
