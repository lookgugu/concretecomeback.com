Almost every standard skateboard truck sold in a shop ships with medium urethane bushings rated around 88A to 90A. Those cushions are calibrated for an average core-market skater weighing roughly 130 to 155 pounds (59 to 70 kg). If you step onto a brand-new set of trucks in your 40s or 50s weighing 180, 200, or 220 pounds, stock bushings will feel mushy, dive into wheel bite on your first hard carve, and struggle to rebound to center.

The instinctive fix is to grab a skate tool and crank the kingpin nut down until three or four threads of the bolt stick out the top. That stops the wheel bite for an afternoon, but it ruins how the truck turns and destroys the bushings within a few sessions. Spending $10 to $15 on aftermarket bushings matched to your actual body weight is the single highest-return hardware upgrade an older skater can make.

## Why tightening stock bushings does not work

A skateboard truck steers by pivoting its hanger around a urethane top bushing (the roadside cushion, which controls resistance as you lean into a turn) and a bottom bushing (the boardside cushion, which bears your standing weight and pushes the hanger back to center).

When you crank the kingpin nut down to compensate for bushings that are too soft for your weight, you pre-compress the urethane before you even stand on the board. Three problems follow:

### You eliminate the progressive turn zone
Instead of leaning smoothly into an arc, the pre-crushed truck feels locked upright until your weight overcomes the preload, at which point it tips suddenly. That twitchy, all-or-nothing feel makes [learning to carve smoothly](/blog/carving-first-skill-adult-returners-skip/) much harder than it needs to be.

### The cup washer bites into the urethane
Over-tightening presses the metal top washer deep into the side of the roadside bushing. Within a few hours of riding, the washer slices a ring into the urethane or pops the bushing lip out over the washer edge, leaving the truck permanently crooked.

### Zero rebound back to center
Crushed polyurethane loses its elastic rebound. After you finish a heel-side turn, the hanger stays tilted instead of springing cleanly back to flat.

## Matching bushing durometer to your body weight

A correctly matched bushing sits snug under the kingpin nut with only one to two threads of the kingpin showing above the nylon lock ring. The resistance comes from the hardness (durometer on the Shore A scale) of the urethane itself, not from mechanical preload.

Use these weight brackets as a practical starting point for traditional street and park trucks (Independent, Ace, Thunder, Venture, Slappy):

### Under 155 lbs (under 70 kg): 85A to 90A (Soft to Medium)
Stock 90A bushings (such as the standard orange cushions in Independent Stage 11 trucks) will feel supportive right out of the box, or even slightly firm until broken in. Lighter older skaters who want effortless low-speed carving can step down to 86A or 88A.

### 160 to 190 lbs (73 to 86 kg): 91A to 94A (Medium-Hard)
This is where stock bushings start giving up under a full-weight carve. Swapping to a 92A or 94A aftermarket bushing (such as Independent 92A blue or 94A black cylinder cushions, or Bones Hardcore Medium/Hard) restores a progressive lean without needing to crank the kingpin nut past flush.

### 195 lbs and above (88 kg+): 95A to 98A (Hard to Extra-Hard)
Skaters in this bracket should skip stock cushions immediately. A 96A bushing (such as Independent 96A yellow, Doh-Doh 95A/98A, or Riptide 95A APS) supports your full standing load over the boardside cushion while still allowing a controlled carve when you press into the rail.

## Bushing geometry: cone vs. barrel

Hardness is half the equation; the profile of the two urethane pieces is the other half.

### Barrel + Cone (Standard street/park setup)
Most trucks ship with a cylindrical **barrel** bushing on the bottom (boardside) for stability and a tapered **cone** bushing on the top (roadside) for quick turn initiation. Because the cone has less urethane volume at the narrow end, it offers less resistance when you start leaning.

### Barrel + Barrel (Double-barrel stability setup)
If you ride a [wider 8.5-inch to 9.0-inch deck](/blog/skateboard-deck-width-guide-for-adults/), weigh over 185 pounds, or feel ankle wobble when setting up for a drop-in or rolling transitions, replace the top cone with a second barrel bushing. A double-barrel setup puts more urethane under the top washer, giving you a wider, calmer center point to stand on while keeping a smooth progressive carve once you commit your hips to the turn.

### Flat washers vs. cup washers
Cupped metal washers wrap around the top edge of the bushing and restrict how far it can deform, increasing stability at the end of a turn. Flat washers let the bushing flare freely for a deeper turn radius. If a new set of medium-hard bushings feels just slightly too restrictive, swapping the top cup washer for a flat washer opens up the turn without dropping a full durometer grade.

## Installation and break-in tips

When you pick up new bushings at a [core skate shop](/directory/shops/), check that the bushing height matches your truck brand. Independent, Ace, and Venture use standard height bushings, while Thunder trucks and low-geometry trucks use slightly shorter cushions; forcing a tall bushing onto a short kingpin leaves the nylon lock ring unengaged.

Tighten the new kingpin nut until it sits flush with the top of the kingpin bolt and no urethane is bulging over the washer lip. New bushings always feel slightly stiff for the first twenty minutes as the urethane seats into the hanger pocket. Skate two short flatground sessions before adding a quarter-turn of tension, and add a 1/8-inch riser pad if you run wheels 56mm or larger so your bushings can do their job without wheel bite.
