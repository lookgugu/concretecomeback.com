I've written before about [why a skate session clears your head at 45](/blog/skateboarding-mental-health-over-40/) and about [what skating does for your confidence off the board](/blog/skateboarding-confidence-transfers-off-the-board/). This post is about something narrower than either of those, and it took me longer to notice because it doesn't show up as a mood lift or a confidence spike. It shows up as a feeling of being a specific, separate person for ninety minutes — not more relaxed or more confident, just differently yourself.

The research that put a name to this is Dr Paul O'Connor's work at the University of Exeter, published in the book *Lifestyle Sports and Identities* after thirty interviews with middle-aged skateboarders in the UK and Hong Kong. One of his central findings is that skateboarding gave his subjects an identity separate from their family and work roles. People didn't just say skating helped them relax or stay fit. They said it gave them access to a version of themselves that the rest of their life didn't have room for.

O'Connor's study didn't set out to compare skateboarding against other forms of exercise — it's interviews with skateboarders, not a head-to-head trial — so it doesn't actually establish that mainstream exercise offers less of this. That comparison is my own read, not his finding: most mainstream adult exercise doesn't look built to generate a separate identity the way skating's distinct subculture does, but that's an inference worth labelling as mine rather than something the study itself measured.

That distinction is worth sitting with, because it's not the same claim as "exercise is good for your mood." It's a claim about identity specifically — about having more than one self available to you.

## What "identity" means here, concretely

At work, you're someone's manager, or someone's employee, or the person who handles a particular account. At home, you're a parent, a partner, a sibling, an adult child managing your own parents' needs. Those roles aren't bad. They're just roles — defined by other people's expectations, built up over years, hard to set down even when you want to.

At the skatepark, none of that history is visible. Nobody there knows what you do for a living unless you tell them. Nobody there has twenty years of context about who you've been. You're just someone who skates, at whatever level you currently skate at, and that's the entire relevant fact about you in that space.

O'Connor's interviewees described this as more than a nice break from routine. For several of them, it functioned as genuine identity repair after things like relationship breakdowns, career setbacks, or periods of addiction — a way to access a version of self that wasn't defined by the role that had just collapsed. That's a heavier claim than "it's a good hobby," and it's worth taking seriously rather than filing it alongside every other wellness recommendation.

## Why most exercise doesn't do this

A gym membership doesn't usually give you a new identity. You're still recognizably the same person on a treadmill that you are in a meeting — same posture toward the activity, same relationship to performance and competence, often the same people around you. Running a familiar route past familiar landmarks doesn't ask you to become anyone different.

Skateboarding is structurally different in a way that matters here. It has its own vocabulary, its own informal etiquette, its own measures of progress that have nothing to do with job title or income or how your week at work went. Walking into a skatepark for the first time as an adult means walking into a space where your existing social credentials don't transfer. That's uncomfortable at first — this site has written plenty about [first-session anxiety](/blog/first-session-back-skatepark-anxiety/) — but it's also exactly the mechanism that makes a separate identity possible. You can't become someone new in a space that already knows who you are.

## Why this lands differently in midlife

At 25, most people already have access to multiple identities without trying. Work is still new enough to feel like exploration rather than routine. Friend groups are still forming. There's room to be more than one thing because very little has calcified yet.

By your forties, the roles tend to have hardened. You've been "the reliable one" at work for a decade. You've been a parent for so long that it's hard to remember what you sounded like before it. Those identities aren't false, but they can crowd out the sense that there's anyone else in there. O'Connor's research suggests skateboarding's value isn't that it adds a hobby to a crowded life — it's that it reopens access to a self that midlife tends to narrow down to almost nothing.

## What this isn't

It's worth being precise about what the research actually supports. This is thirty interviews, not a clinical trial — a description of what people reported experiencing, not proof of a measurable effect. It's also not a replacement for addressing whatever is actually wrong if a role genuinely needs to change — a bad job, a strained relationship, something that needs its own attention rather than an escape hatch. O'Connor's subjects used skating alongside real life changes, not instead of them.

## What to actually do with this

If this resonates, a few things seem to help protect the effect rather than dilute it:

**Let the skatepark stay uncredentialed.** Resist the urge to mention your job or your age to establish context. Part of what makes the space work is that it doesn't need that information.

**Notice the shift, don't force it.** You can't manufacture a separate identity by trying hard at it. It tends to show up as a side effect of showing up consistently and letting the space do what it does.

**Don't turn it into another performance.** If skating starts picking up the same self-evaluation anxiety that follows you from work — am I good enough, am I behind, am I progressing fast enough — you've imported the old identity into the new space. That's worth catching.

Most of what gets written about adult skateboarding focuses on what it does for your body or your mood, and both of those are real. What O'Connor's research points to is quieter and, for a lot of returning adults, more important: a version of you that the rest of your life has no claim on, available for ninety minutes most weeks, for as long as you keep showing up for it.
