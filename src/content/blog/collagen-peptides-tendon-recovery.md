I wrote a broader piece on this site about joint supplements generally — glucosamine, omega-3s, turmeric, the whole shelf. Collagen got a paragraph in that one, which undersold it. For tendons and ligaments specifically — the tissues most likely to be nagging at you after a hard landing or an ankle roll — collagen is the supplement with the most specific, mechanistically sensible evidence behind it, and it deserves its own protocol rather than a passing mention.

This is that protocol: what the research actually supports, how to time it, and what it's realistic to expect.

## Why tendons and ligaments are a different problem

Muscle repairs itself relatively quickly because it has a rich blood supply. Tendons and ligaments don't — they're built from densely packed collagen fibers with comparatively poor circulation, which is exactly why a rolled ankle or a cranky Achilles tends to linger for weeks or months rather than days. Skating loads these tissues in a specific way: repetitive impact through the ankles and knees, and sudden sideways or twisting loads through the wrists on falls. That's a lot of collagen-dependent tissue getting asked to do a lot of work, with a relatively slow repair mechanism supporting it.

That mismatch is the actual case for paying attention to collagen specifically, rather than joint supplements generally.

## What the research supports

Hydrolyzed collagen peptides — collagen broken down into a more easily absorbed form — reach peak levels in your bloodstream about 45 to 60 minutes after you take them. That timing matters, because the research points to combining that window with mechanical loading: taking collagen shortly before the activity that loads the tissue, rather than at a random time of day disconnected from your session.

Vitamin C's role is more specific than "general immune support." It's a required cofactor for the enzymes that stabilize newly formed collagen and hold its structure together — without adequate vitamin C, the body's collagen-building machinery doesn't function properly, which is part of why collagen and vitamin C are usually studied together rather than collagen alone.

Controlled trials in tendon conditions like patellar and Achilles tendinopathy have generally found that collagen paired with a structured loading exercise program outperforms exercise alone for pain and function scores over a period of months, not days. That's an important detail: the supplement was never tested as a standalone fix. It was tested as an addition to appropriate loading exercise, which is doing real work in every trial where collagen showed a benefit.

## A realistic protocol

Based on how these trials are typically structured, a reasonable approach looks like this:

- **10–15 grams of hydrolyzed collagen peptides** (a scoopable powder, usually flavorless, dissolves in water or coffee)
- **Alongside 50mg or more of vitamin C** — a small glass of orange juice covers this, or a low-dose supplement if you're not near citrus
- **Taken 45–60 minutes before your skate session or rehab exercises** — this is the timing that lines up with peak blood collagen levels and mechanical loading
- **Daily, consistently, for at least 8–12 weeks** before judging whether it's helping

That last point is the one people skip. Taking collagen occasionally, or only on days your ankle feels bad, isn't the protocol that's actually been studied, and it's unlikely to produce the same result.

## Who this is actually for

This makes the most sense for adults managing a slow-recovering strain or sprain, ongoing tendinopathy (a nagging Achilles or patellar tendon that flares with activity), or general connective-tissue resilience given how much repetitive load skating puts through ankles, knees, and wrists. It is not a treatment for an acute injury — a fresh, significantly swollen sprain or anything with sharp, severe pain needs an actual diagnosis before you're thinking about supplements at all. See a doctor or physiotherapist first; add collagen to a recovery plan, not in place of one.

## What it won't do

Collagen doesn't reverse existing tendon damage overnight, and it isn't a substitute for the loading exercises that are the actual proven treatment for tendinopathy — it's a supporting addition to them. If you're not also doing the structured strengthening work a physiotherapist would recommend for a tendon issue, the supplement alone is unlikely to move the needle much. Set your expectations at "one useful piece of a recovery plan," not "the fix."

## Safety notes

Hydrolyzed collagen is generally well tolerated, but check the source if you have food allergies or dietary restrictions — most commercial collagen is derived from bovine or marine (fish) sources, and occasionally porcine. Vitamin C at the doses used here is low risk for most adults, though very high doses from supplements generally (well beyond what's in this protocol) can cause digestive upset in some people. As with any new supplement, if you're managing a chronic condition or taking regular medication, it's worth a quick check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting — not because collagen has significant known interactions, but because that's a five-minute conversation worth having before you start rather than after.

Give it the full eight to twelve weeks, pair it with actual loading exercise, and treat it as what it is: a reasonably well-supported, low-risk addition to a recovery plan — not a replacement for one.
