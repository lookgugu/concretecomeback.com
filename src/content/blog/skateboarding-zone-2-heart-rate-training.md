A patient asked me last month whether her skate sessions "counted" as Zone 2 training. She'd been hearing the term everywhere — podcasts, fitness newsletters, her cardiologist — and wanted to know if she needed to add a separate cardio session on top of skating, or if skating already covered it.

The honest answer is: it depends entirely on how you skate. Which is actually useful information, because it means you can adjust one thing and get a very different training effect out of the same session.

## What Zone 2 actually means

Heart rate training divides effort into zones, usually numbered 1 through 5, based on percentage of your maximum heart rate. Zone 2 sits around 60–70% of max HR — low enough that you can hold a conversation in full sentences, high enough that you're clearly working. It's the pace you could sustain for an hour or more without your form falling apart.

The reason Zone 2 gets so much attention is that sustained time in that range is a particularly efficient way to build your aerobic base: it trains your body to burn fat efficiently for fuel and improves how well your mitochondria — the energy-producing structures in your cells — do their job. Short, hard efforts build aerobic fitness too, through a different mechanism — that's part of why interval training works — but they don't accumulate the same kind of steady, low-fatigue volume. Zone 2's advantage is that you can sustain it for a long time, repeatedly, over weeks and months, without digging into the recovery cost a lot of hard intervals would.

None of this makes higher-intensity effort useless. It's a different tool for a different adaptation, and most well-rounded training includes both.

## Finding your zones

The rough formula most people start with is 220 minus your age, which gives an estimated maximum heart rate. At 50, that's roughly 170 beats per minute, putting Zone 2 somewhere around 102–119 bpm. It's a blunt estimate — individual max heart rate varies more than the formula admits — and it tends to run low for older adults specifically. When Hirofumi Tanaka, Kevin Monahan and Douglas Seals pooled data from 351 studies and then measured 514 healthy people in the lab ([*Journal of the American College of Cardiology*, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0735-1097(00)01054-8)), the line that fit best was 208 minus 0.7 times your age. At 50 that gives 173 rather than 170, and the gap widens with each decade. Either version lands you within a few beats, so treat the number as a starting point, not a precise target.

One exception matters more than the formula: if you take a beta blocker or another medication that lowers your heart rate, or you're managing a heart condition, percentage-of-max targets don't apply to you. Your peak heart rate is capped by the drug, not your age, so a watch will tell you you're loafing when you're working hard. Use the talk test below for day-to-day pacing, and get your actual training range from the cardiologist or physio who knows your history.

If you don't want to do math or wear a chest strap, the talk test is a decent substitute: if you can speak in full sentences without gasping, but singing would be a stretch, you're probably in or near Zone 2. If you can chat easily without any elevated breathing, you're below it. If you're answering in short phrases, you've moved past it.

A basic fitness watch with wrist-based heart rate is accurate enough for this purpose. You don't need lab-grade equipment to train Zone 2 meaningfully.

## Where a typical skate session actually lands

This is where skating gets interesting, because it isn't one intensity — it's several, depending on what you're doing and how continuously you're doing it.

**Casual cruising and easy pushing** — rolling along mostly flat ground, pushing every so often — tends to sit in Zone 1 to low Zone 2. Pleasant, sustainable, but probably not enough stimulus on its own if Zone 2 is specifically what you're after.

**Steady pumping through transitions** — working a mini-ramp or bowl continuously, generating speed through your legs rather than pushing with your foot — is where most adults land solidly in Zone 2, and it's genuinely comparable to a brisk uphill walk or an easy jog in terms of sustained effort.

**Hard bursts** — a run at a feature, a session sprint, anything with real exertion packed into 10–20 seconds — will spike you into Zone 3 or 4 briefly before you recover. That's not a problem. It's just a different training stimulus, closer to interval work than steady-state cardio. [Skateboarding already sits at a solid moderate-to-vigorous intensity](/blog/skateboarding-counts-as-real-exercise/) by the numbers exercise physiologists use — the question here is just which part of that range a given session hits.

The catch is the stopping. A typical park session involves a lot of standing around between runs — waiting your turn, chatting, resetting. Every pause drops your heart rate back down, which is great for recovery but means the session as a whole may spend less continuous time in Zone 2 than the effort level suggests.

## Structuring a session to actually hit it

If you want a session that's deliberately built around Zone 2, a few adjustments make a real difference:

**Keep moving.** Favor pumping and carving over standing and resetting. A flow section or a bowl with continuous lines gives you far more sustained heart rate time than a technical street spot where you're stopping to reset a trick every 30 seconds.

**Pick terrain with mild, continuous elevation change.** Rolling in and out of a shallow bowl or a mellow transition keeps your legs working the whole time, which is what generates the sustained effort Zone 2 needs. Dead-flat ground rarely gets you there without a lot of pushing.

**Check in periodically.** Glance at your watch or run the talk test every few minutes. If you've drifted well above Zone 2 and you're specifically trying to build aerobic base today, ease off the pumping for a lap or two rather than sprinting the whole session.

**Give it real duration.** Zone 2 benefits come from time spent in the zone, not from hitting it briefly. Thirty to forty-five minutes of mostly continuous movement is a reasonable target if this is the goal for the day.

## You don't have to pick one mode every time

None of this means every session needs to be a structured Zone 2 workout. Some days you're there to work on tricks, hang out with friends, and stop constantly between attempts — that's a perfectly good use of a skate session, just not primarily an aerobic one. Other days, especially if you're specifically trying to build cardiovascular base or you're easing back into fitness after time off, it's worth shaping the session around continuous movement instead.

The useful takeaway isn't "skating is Zone 2" or "skating isn't Zone 2." It's that skating covers a wide range of the intensity scale, and which part of it you land in is mostly a function of how continuously you move — which, unlike your heart rate itself, is something you get to choose.
