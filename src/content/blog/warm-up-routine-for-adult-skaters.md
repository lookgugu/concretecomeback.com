At 16, warming up meant pushing to the park and doing a couple of easy laps before trying anything. Your joints were ready before your brain had even decided to warm up.

That's not how it works anymore. The tissue around your joints is less elastic than it was, your nervous system takes longer to switch from "sitting at a desk" to "reacting on a moving board," and the first ten minutes of a session are exactly when most adult skaters tweak something — not because the trick was hard, but because the body wasn't ready for it yet.

A proper warm-up fixes this. It doesn't need to be complicated, and it doesn't need equipment. It needs about ten minutes and a little consistency.

## Why this matters more now than it did at 16

Warming up does two things: it increases blood flow to the muscles and connective tissue you're about to load, and it primes the nervous system pathways that control balance and quick reaction. Both of these take longer to activate as you get older. Skip the warm-up and you're asking joints and tendons that are still "cold" to absorb the exact kind of sudden, unpredictable load that skating produces — a foot catching wrong, an unexpected wobble, a heavier-than-expected landing.

The four areas that fail first in adult skaters are the ankles, the wrists, the hips, and the knees. Not coincidentally, those are the joints skating asks the most of. The warm-up below targets all four.

## The routine

Do this before every session, not just the ones where you're planning something ambitious. It works just as well before a mellow cruise as before a park session.

### 1. Ankle circles and rocks (2 minutes)

Stand with your feet hip-width apart. Rock slowly from your toes to your heels ten times per side, then do ten slow ankle circles in each direction, per foot. Skating asks your ankles to make constant small corrections — this wakes up that range of motion before you're relying on it at speed.

### 2. Wrist circles and extensions (1 minute)

Wrists get almost no attention in most warm-up routines, which is a mistake — they're one of the joints most commonly injured when adults skate. Circle each wrist ten times in both directions, then gently extend each hand back (like you're pushing against a wall) and hold for ten seconds. This is mobility work, not fall preparation: the goal is a wrist that moves freely under your wrist guards, not a wrist you're training to catch yourself with. Actually bracing a fall by extending your wrist is how fractures happen — practice falling safely instead, and let your wrist guards do the job they're designed for.

### 3. Hip circles and leg swings (2 minutes)

Stand near something you can hold for balance. Swing one leg forward and back ten times, then side to side ten times, then switch legs. Follow with ten hip circles in each direction, hands on hips. Your hips do most of the work in carving and weight shifts — tight hips translate directly into a stiffer, less controlled ride.

### 4. Bodyweight squats (2 minutes)

Fifteen slow bodyweight squats, controlled on the way down. This warms up the knees and quads through the same range of motion you'll use absorbing impact and staying low through turns. Keep your weight through your heels and don't let your knees cave inward.

### 5. Balance holds (2 minutes)

Stand on one foot for 20–30 seconds, switch, repeat once per side. If that's easy, close your eyes for the last ten seconds of each hold. This activates the small stabilizer muscles around your ankles and hips that skating relies on constantly and that most other warm-ups ignore entirely.

### 6. Easy pushing and rolling (1 minute)

Get on the board and push gently for a minute — flat ground, no tricks, no features. This is the transition step: it takes what you just activated off the pavement and puts it under the actual demands of standing on a moving board.

## Fitting it into a real session

Ten minutes feels like a lot to carve out of a session that might only be an hour, but it's not wasted time — it's the difference between a session where your body responds the way you expect and one where it doesn't. Most adult skaters who adopt a consistent warm-up report that their first twenty minutes of skating feel noticeably less shaky than they used to.

If you're short on time, don't cut the warm-up — cut the session. Five minutes less skating with a warm body beats five extra minutes on a cold one.

## What to skip

Skip static stretching — long, held stretches of a cold muscle — before you skate. It doesn't warm anything up and can temporarily reduce power output. Save static stretching for after your session, when your muscles are already warm and you're working on long-term flexibility rather than preparing to move.

If a specific joint is already sore or recovering from something, don't push it through this routine to "test it out." Warm it up gently within a pain-free range, and if it doesn't feel right, that's your answer for the day.

Ten minutes, four joints, no equipment. It's the single most controllable thing you can do to reduce your injury risk before you ever step on the board.
