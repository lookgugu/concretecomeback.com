On May 2, 2026, Pelican Park in Mandeville, Louisiana opened a new skatepark built around a simple brief: welcome anyone on wheels, not just skateboards. The grand opening schedule led with a free WCMX and adaptive skate clinic before the ribbon was even cut, with demonstrations from Andrew Garcia of Ability Skateboarding & Action Sports and Tony Torres of TNT 360 Mobility. The park itself was designed as an "all wheels" space — skateboards, wheelchairs, and scooters sharing the same terrain rather than being sorted into separate zones.

I see a lot of patients whose bodies don't move the way they used to — replaced hips, surgically repaired ankles, decades-old injuries that never fully resolved. Watching adaptive skateboarding get real visibility this year has made me think harder about what "accessible design" actually means, and why it matters even if you've never used a wheelchair, a prosthetic limb, or an adaptive board in your life.

## What adaptive skateboarding actually looks like

"Adaptive skateboarding" isn't one thing. It covers WCMX (skatepark riding on a wheelchair built or modified for it), seated and modified skateboards, riders using prosthetics adapted for balance and grip, and a range of other setups built around whatever a given athlete's body needs. What unites it is the same terrain — bowls, ramps, transitions — ridden by people whose equipment or technique looks different from a standard stance, but whose relationship to the concrete is the same one every skater has: read the line, commit, manage the fall.

## Why it's suddenly visible

Skateboarding doesn't currently have a Paralympic medal program, but that's actively shifting in the run-up to the 2028 Los Angeles Games. Adaptive skaters have been featured at high-profile Olympic and Paralympic buildup events — Edward Jeffries, known as "Skateboard E," demonstrated adaptive skateboarding in Paris ahead of it becoming part of the broader LA28 push, and the LA28 Paralympic handover celebration showcased adaptive athletes including Oscar Loreto Jr., Kanya Sesser, and Aaron "Wheelz" Fotheringham. On the ground, programs like the City of Los Angeles's PlayLA Adaptive Youth Sports Program — run with the Challenged Athletes Foundation and coached by Paralympians including Mike Shea and Rudy Garcia-Tolson — are already teaching adaptive skateboarding to kids with disabilities. Pelican Park's opening is one visible example of that momentum reaching a local park rather than staying at the elite level.

## The design lesson that applies to you

Here's the part that's directly relevant to your own comeback, whatever your body currently does or doesn't do easily.

An "all wheels" park designed to work for a wheelchair or an adaptive board tends to include, almost by necessity: smoother, more consistent transitions (a rough patch that's a minor annoyance for an able-bodied skater can be a hard stop for someone on a WCMX rig); mellower entry angles into bowls and banks, so commitment isn't all-or-nothing; wider approach lines that don't force a single narrow path into a feature; and enough flat space to build speed gradually rather than needing an aggressive drop-in.

Read that list again as a 45- or 55-year-old with a knee that doesn't love sudden impact, or a shoulder you're protective of since a bad fall. Every single one of those features helps you too. Accessible design isn't a separate, parallel version of good park design — it's what good park design actually looks like once it's built for the full range of real bodies using it, rather than defaulting to a fearless teenager as the only user who matters. This site has covered how [newer, inclusive park builds](/blog/inclusive-skateparks-all-ages/) already trend this direction; adaptive skating is the sharpest version of the same argument.

## What this isn't

This isn't a suggestion that your comeback and an adaptive athlete's experience are the same thing, or that you should treat your own rusty joints as remotely comparable to a permanent disability. They're not, and collapsing that distinction does a disservice to adaptive athletes who navigate barriers most of us never will. The point is narrower and more useful: the same physical principles — forgiving surfaces, mellow commitment points, room to build speed — that make a park genuinely accessible for an adaptive rider are the same principles that make it a better park for a cautious, recovering, or aging able body. You benefit from the same design thinking, even though your reasons for needing it are different.

## How to see it or support it

**Ask at your local park or shop whether an adaptive session exists.** Adaptive skate programming is growing fast but is still unevenly distributed — some cities have regular sessions, others have none yet.

**Watch a demo if one comes through your area.** Adaptive skaters riding transition are, without exaggeration, some of the most technically impressive skating you'll see at any park, and watching it tends to reframe what you think is physically possible at any age or ability level.

**If you're involved in advocating for a park upgrade locally,** raise adaptive access explicitly rather than assuming standard "all-ages" language covers it — smooth transitions, mellow entries, and wide approach lines are specific asks, not a given even in newer builds.

**If you coach, volunteer, or organize adult sessions,** a genuinely welcoming session is one that doesn't assume a single body type or ability level walking through the gate. That's true whether or not anyone using an adaptive setup shows up on a given day.

The Pelican Parks of the world are a small, concrete signal of a bigger shift: skateboarding is quietly redefining what "for everybody" actually requires in its infrastructure, not just its marketing. Whatever body you're bringing back to a board this year, that shift is working in your favor too.
