October means two things for a lot of working adults: a run of business trips before the year closes out, and half-term holidays with the family. Both are exactly the kind of stretch where a skating habit you've worked hard to build quietly falls apart — not because you don't want to skate, but because you didn't plan for it.

It doesn't have to be that way. Here's how to keep a session alive when you're away from your usual park.

## Decide what you're actually optimizing for

Before you pack anything, be honest about the trip. A two-day work trip with back-to-back meetings is a different problem than a week-long family holiday with some free afternoons. For the former, you're mostly trying to protect the habit — even one short session keeps the routine from resetting to zero. For the latter, you have real time, and it's worth treating the trip as a chance to skate somewhere completely new.

Trying to force a full training session into a trip that was never going to have room for one is how people end up skating nothing at all and feeling bad about it. Scale the ambition to the actual trip.

## Finding a park before you land

Don't wait until you arrive to figure out where you'll skate. A quick search before you leave — Google Maps actually indexes a surprising number of skateparks if you search "skatepark" in the area you're headed, and dedicated spot-finder apps and sites built by skaters tend to have more detail on surface quality, crowd levels, and whether a park skews younger or welcomes a mixed-age crowd.

Local skate shops are a second, often better source. A quick email or a comment on a shop's social media asking "visiting next week, any mellow parks or quiet times you'd recommend for an adult skater getting back into it" tends to get a genuinely useful answer, and sometimes an invite to a local session you wouldn't have found otherwise.

If you can, check that a park's surface and feature mix match what you actually skate. Showing up to a vert-heavy bowl park when what you ride is mellow transition and flat ground wastes the limited time you have.

## Packing without overthinking it

You don't need your full home setup for a short trip. A board you don't mind checking as luggage or squeezing into an oversized bag is the main decision. Airlines generally treat a skateboard as ordinary sporting equipment — most will let it go as a checked bag or, trucks and all, in a padded travel bag, though policies and size limits vary enough between carriers that it's worth a quick check with your specific airline before you fly rather than assuming.

If you're flying and want to travel lighter, some skaters pack just a deck and wheels and source trucks, bearings, and grip locally or borrow a setup — more hassle than it's usually worth for a short trip, but worth knowing if luggage space is genuinely tight. For a road trip, none of this matters; just throw the board in the car the way you would at home.

Pads are the thing people leave behind to save space, and it's the wrong place to cut. A compact set of wrist guards packs small and takes up little room, and a short session in an unfamiliar park — where you don't know the surface, the crowd, or the quirks of the layout — is exactly when you want that protection most, not least.

## Keeping the session realistic

An unfamiliar park on a short trip is not the time to push your limits. You don't know the surface quality, you don't know how busy it gets at what hour, and you don't have the layout memorized the way you do at home. Treat the first ten minutes of any new park the way you'd treat week one of a comeback: push around, get a feel for the surface and the crowd, and let your sense of what's safe here build before you commit to anything.

This is also where skating with family works differently than skating alone. If you're travelling with kids who skate too, a new park is often more fun explored together at an easy pace than treated as your own training session — you'll probably enjoy it more that way anyway.

## Protecting the habit on trips where you can't skate at all

Some trips genuinely won't have room for a session — a packed conference schedule, a holiday with no park within reach. That's fine. The goal on those trips isn't to skate; it's to not let a week or two away become the reason you don't go back when you're home. A few minutes of balance work, ankle mobility, or just mentally planning your next session keeps the thread connected without needing a board at all.

The adults who keep a skating habit alive for years aren't the ones who never missed a trip. They're the ones who treated a missed week as a gap to close, not a reason to start over. Pack light, do a little research before you go, and let a trip be one more place you skate rather than one more reason you didn't.
