Ask an adult skater at any park what protective gear they own and you'll usually hear the same list: a helmet, wrist guards, maybe knee pads. Almost nobody mentions impact shorts. They're the piece of gear this site's own [gear guide for aging joints](/blog/gear-for-aging-joints/) and [safety gear guide](/blog/safety-gear-guide/) both touch on, but that almost everyone skips buying — right up until the fall that makes them wish they hadn't.

That gap matters more as you get older, because the body part impact shorts protect is the one you can least afford to land hard on.

## Why the hip and tailbone deserve their own gear

Wrist guards get bought first because wrist fractures are the classic beginner injury — an instinctive hand-catch on an outstretched arm, covered in detail in [the guide to falling without breaking your wrist](/blog/how-to-fall-without-breaking-your-wrist/). That instinct is real and worth protecting against. But it isn't the only fall pattern, and it isn't the one that matters most as bone density changes with age.

A sideways fall that lands on the hip, or a backward fall that catches the tailbone, is a different category of injury entirely. [This site's piece on bone density](/blog/osteoporosis-bone-density-skateboarding/) covers why that matters specifically past 40 and more so past 50: bone density declines gradually with age in both men and women, and a fall onto the hip is exactly the mechanism behind the fractures that worry orthopedists most in older adults. A sprained wrist heals in weeks. A hip fracture is a different, much longer road — which is exactly why it's worth padding against a fall you're trying to avoid in the first place, not treating as an acceptable risk the way a bruised palm might be.

The tailbone takes a similar unprotected hit on nearly any backward fall, and coccyx injuries have a well-earned reputation for lingering — weeks of pain sitting down long after the bruise itself has faded.

## What impact shorts actually are

Impact shorts are a compression short — similar in base construction to a cycling or running base layer — with shaped padding sewn in over the hips, tailbone, and sometimes the outer thighs. The padding is usually a dense foam or honeycomb-structured shock-absorbing material, built to spread the force of an impact across a wider area and slow it down, rather than letting it concentrate on one point of bone. They're worn under your regular skate clothes, not as a visible layer over them, which is a big part of why they're easy to forget exist — unlike a helmet or wrist guards, nobody can see whether you're wearing them.

They won't make a hard fall painless, and they're not a guarantee against a serious injury — even trials of purpose-built hip protectors haven't shown a clear reduction in hip-fracture risk in community-dwelling older adults, and there's no skating-specific research on impact shorts to point to. What padding reliably does is spread an impact out and slow it down rather than letting it concentrate on one point of bone, which plausibly reduces how much force actually reaches the joint underneath. That's worth having in your corner on a fall that matters, even though it isn't a promise of a minor outcome.

## When they matter most

Flatground falls tend to be lower-speed and more controllable — you have more warning and more chance to redirect the fall. The falls that put real force through a hip or tailbone tend to happen on transition: dropping in, riding a bowl, anything where your speed and your commitment are both higher and the margin for a clean recovery is smaller. If you're working on [committing to the drop-in](/blog/committing-to-the-drop-in-at-any-age/) or spending real time in bowls and ramps rather than staying on flat street terrain, that's exactly the context where impact shorts earn their place in your gear bag.

They're also worth considering during any period where you're relearning to fall at all — the first few months back on a board, before your instincts have caught up with your intentions, the way [the guide to falling safely after 40](/blog/how-to-fall-safely-after-40/) describes. A confident, well-practiced fall is still the best protection there is. Padding is what covers you in the gap before that confidence is built.

## Choosing a pair

A few things matter more than brand:

**Documented impact testing first.** Not every padded short absorbs impact equally well — independent testing of commercial hip protectors has found force attenuation ranging anywhere from barely any reduction to a substantial one, and thicker padding doesn't automatically mean better attenuation. As [the safety gear guide](/blog/safety-gear-guide/) puts it, a short that merely looks padded isn't the same as one that's been impact-tested over the greater trochanter. Look for a product that documents its impact testing before anything else on this list, especially if you're buying specifically because of a bone-density concern.

**Padding placement, not just thickness.** Once you've confirmed a product is actually tested, make sure the shaped pads sit over your hip bones and tailbone when you're in a skating stance — crouched, not standing upright — rather than just somewhere on the short. Padding that's an inch off target when you're actually bent into a ride does very little, however well it tested in a lab.

**A snug, compression fit.** Loose padding shifts out of position exactly when you need it, usually mid-fall. Impact shorts are supposed to fit like a tight base layer, not like regular shorts.

**Breathability for the season.** Full-coverage padding against skin in summer heat is a real comfort trade-off. Look for mesh ventilation panels if you're buying for warm-weather sessions, and don't assume dense means better — some of the most breathable options use a honeycomb cut structure rather than solid foam blocks.

**Try them under your actual riding clothes before trusting them.** A pair that bunches or rides up under your usual pants at the park is one you'll quietly stop wearing within a month. Test comfort during a low-key session before committing to wearing them every time out.

## The honest trade-off

Impact shorts cost more than knee pads, feel unnecessary until the day they aren't, and nobody at the park will ever know you're wearing them. That's probably the whole reason they're the gear adults skip. They're not a guarantee against the injury they're built to blunt — a hip fracture is the one with the longest, hardest recovery on this entire list, and no study has shown padded shorts actually prevent one. What they plausibly do is attenuate the force of a fall that matters, which is a real reason to have them in your bag even without a promise attached to it.
