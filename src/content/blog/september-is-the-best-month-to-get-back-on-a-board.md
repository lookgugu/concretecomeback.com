Every year around this time, I hear some version of the same sentence from people thinking about picking a board back up: "I'll wait until spring, when the weather's better." It sounds reasonable. It's also exactly backwards, and if you've been putting off your comeback for that reason, this is the post talking you out of it.

## The heat problem you've forgotten about

If you skated as a teenager, there's a decent chance most of it happened in summer, and you remember summer sessions fondly. What you may have forgotten is how much harder hot pavement makes everything for an adult body. Concrete radiates heat back at you well into the evening, grip and control both suffer once you're sweating through a session, and recovering from a fall hurts more when you're already overheated and dehydrated. September's lower temperatures aren't just more pleasant — they make the actual mechanics of learning or relearning easier, because you can push longer before fatigue and heat start affecting your balance.

## The park is yours again

Summer skateparks, especially in the afternoon and early evening, are often packed — kids on break from school, families out until dark, lines forming for the bowl. That's a legitimately harder environment to be a returning adult beginner in. You're sharing limited space with people moving faster and less predictably than you, and self-consciousness about being watched is worse when there's an audience.

Once school is back and the evenings draw in — September in the Northern Hemisphere — exactly the crowd that made summer sessions intimidating thins out. You'll often find yourself with long stretches of flat ground or a quiet corner of the park to yourself — the conditions that actually let a beginner focus on fundamentals instead of traffic management.

## You get a real runway before winter

This is the part that matters most if you're serious about actually building skill rather than just trying it once. From early September (early March if you're south of the equator) to whenever winter weather shuts down your local outdoor options, you typically have eight to twelve weeks of good skating conditions — enough time to get through the genuinely awkward early phase and arrive at the cold months with some real footing under you, instead of arriving at spring having never started.

Compare that to waiting until spring: you lose the entire autumn window, you're starting from zero at the exact moment everyone else is also starting their season (crowded parks again), and you're now doing your shakiest, most self-conscious early sessions in public at the same time as everyone else's. Starting now means your beginner phase happens in relative privacy, and by the time the parks fill back up next spring, you're not a beginner anymore.

## A note for the Southern Hemisphere

Everything above is written from the Northern Hemisphere, where September is early autumn. If you're in Australia or anywhere south of the equator, flip the calendar six months: in the temperate south — Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide, Perth, New Zealand — your version of this post is **March**, when the summer heat and the summer crowds fade and you get the same eight-to-twelve-week runway before winter. In the tropical north, where March can still be hot and wet and winter never really shuts skating down, ignore the calendar and aim for your local cooler, drier season instead. Starting in September — your early spring — is the exact scenario the previous section argues against: you'd be doing your shakiest sessions at the moment everyone else's season starts. The advice is seasonal, not calendar-specific: start in the mild weeks *after* your region's peak season, not before it.

## What "starting now" actually looks like

You don't need a new training plan to act on this — the site already has one. Follow the [8-week return plan for adult skaters](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/) exactly as written; the only thing September changes is when you start it. Two things to hold onto alongside it:

- **Gear from day one:** helmet, wrist guards and full-cup knee pads on every session — not after you've decided you're "serious enough" to need them. Non-negotiable regardless of month, and the knee pads matter most once the plan puts you on small banks and gentle transitions, where a knee slide is the safe bail.
- **Off-peak hours:** weekday mornings and early afternoons tend to be quietest once school is back. Spend your shakiest sessions in them.

None of that requires ideal conditions or a big block of free time. It requires starting before the excuse of "next season" turns into another full year gone.

## The excuse doesn't actually go away in spring

Here's the uncomfortable truth about "I'll start when the weather's better": the person who says it in September usually says something equivalent in March, too — it's cold still, or the parks are wet, or work got busy over winter and the moment passed. The weather was never really the obstacle. It's a reasonable-sounding cover for the same hesitation that shows up at any time of year.

If you've been circling the idea of getting back on a board, early autumn — September up here, March down there — gives you the best combination of conditions you'll get all year: comfortable temperatures, thinner crowds, and enough runway to actually build something before the season turns. Waiting for spring doesn't remove the hard part — it just delays it, and hands you a more crowded park to do it in.

Go find your board. The window is open right now.
