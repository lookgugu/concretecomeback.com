Open enrollment rolls around every year — often in the fall, though the exact window depends on your employer, your country, and whether you're on a marketplace or workplace plan — and most adult skaters treat their health plan the way they treat the fine print on a software update — something to click past. Then February arrives, a knee flares up, physical therapy gets recommended, and it's a scramble to figure out what's actually covered. Check your own plan's actual dates rather than assuming a calendar month; missing your specific window can mean waiting a full year for another chance to change coverage.

You don't need to become an insurance expert. You do need to ask a handful of specific questions before you lock in a plan for the year, because the honest truth is that returning to a fall-based sport in your 40s or 50s changes what you need from a policy. None of this is legal or financial advice, and most of the specifics below — HSAs, FSAs, copays, provider networks — are U.S. concepts tied to U.S. employer and marketplace plans. If you're in the UK, Canada, or Australia, your public and private coverage works differently, but the underlying questions still transfer: what a recurring physio/PT need actually costs you, what a fall-related ER visit or imaging scan costs under your system, and whether any employer benefit or tax-advantaged account in your country covers protective gear. Check what your own system's equivalent looks like rather than assuming any of this applies directly.

## Why this is worth ten minutes of your enrollment window

Adult skaters tend to carry a specific injury profile: wrist and ankle sprains, overuse issues in knees and hips, the occasional fracture, and a genuine ongoing need for physical therapy rather than one-off urgent care visits. A plan built around "I rarely see a doctor" doesn't fit that pattern well, and the cost difference between plans is often smaller than people assume once you actually run the numbers for a year that includes a sprained ankle and six PT sessions.

## Questions to ask about physical therapy coverage

This is the one most skaters skip, and it's the one that matters most if you're managing a recurring issue.

**How many PT visits are covered per year, and does that reset with a new diagnosis or a new injury?** Some plans cap visits per condition, others cap total visits regardless of cause. If you're managing something chronic — a knee that flares with higher skating volume, a shoulder from an old fall — know which kind of plan you have.

**Is a physician referral required before PT is covered?** Plans that require a referral add a step (and a copay) you'll want to budget time for before you can start treatment, not after an injury is already slowing your recovery.

**What does a PT visit actually cost you, in-network versus out-of-network?** A flat copay isn't the only way plans charge for this — plenty put PT toward your deductible or charge coinsurance on the negotiated rate instead, so "no copay" doesn't necessarily mean cheap before you've met your deductible. Check deductible applicability, copay or coinsurance, and the in-network allowed amount, not just the copay line. A physiotherapist who specializes in sports or skate-specific rehab may be out-of-network, too — worth knowing that cost difference before you need to decide under pressure.

## What's often eligible under an HSA or FSA

If your employer offers a Health Savings Account or Flexible Spending Account, a surprising amount of skate-adjacent gear can qualify as an eligible medical expense — but eligibility rules are specific and change, and the two accounts don't check that eligibility the same way. For an FSA, your plan administrator is the one to confirm with before you buy. For an HSA, there's no administrator to ask — you're the one determining whether an expense qualifies, so check current IRS guidance (or a tax professional) rather than treating anything short of that as approval. Either way, keep receipts.

Items that are sometimes eligible, often with a Letter of Medical Necessity from a doctor: wrist guards and knee braces prescribed for an existing or recurring injury, orthotic inserts, and compression gear recommended as part of a treatment plan. A general "just in case" purchase of protective gear is far less likely to qualify than gear tied to a documented injury or a provider's recommendation — that documentation step is usually what decides it.

The two accounts work differently once you've got that paperwork. An FSA typically runs through your employer's plan administrator — you submit a claim, often with that documentation attached, and get reimbursed. An HSA usually has no claims process at all: you pay directly from the account (or reimburse yourself later), and you're the one who determines the expense is qualified and keeps the records — it's self-certified, with the documentation there to support you if the IRS ever asks, not to get approved by anyone up front. If you've already been through [a knee brace decision](/blog/knee-braces-for-adult-skaters/) or an [ankle sprain](/blog/ankle-sprains-adult-skaters/) this year, hang onto that paperwork from your provider either way.

## Documenting a recurring injury so it actually counts

Insurers respond better to a paper trail than a verbal explanation, and if you're ever audited on an HSA withdrawal, so will you. If you're dealing with something that keeps coming back — a wrist that aches after longer sessions, a hip that needs a warm-up before it cooperates — a short, dated note from your doctor or physiotherapist describing the diagnosis and recommended treatment is worth getting even if you don't need it immediately. It turns "this happens sometimes" into something an insurer's claims reviewer can act on — or, for an HSA withdrawal, something you have on hand if you ever need to substantiate it — and it's much easier to request while you're already in an appointment than to chase down six months later.

## What to actually compare between plans

These four categories are specific to the injury pattern this sport creates — they're not the whole picture. A plan can lead on all four and still cost you more over the year once you factor in the premium, so weigh them against the basics you'd compare for any plan: the premium itself (and what your employer covers), the general deductible, and the overall out-of-pocket maximum, which is what actually caps your exposure in a bad-fall year. With those as the baseline, check these four numbers for how this specific sport will use the plan:

**What you'd actually owe for outpatient imaging.** An X-ray or MRI after a bad fall is routine in this sport, and the deductible alone won't tell you the number — plenty of plans apply a copay or coinsurance to imaging on top of or instead of the general deductible. Check whether imaging counts toward your deductible at all, what the copay or coinsurance is once it's met, whether network matters, and where that falls relative to your out-of-pocket maximum. Know the real number before you need one.

**Out-of-network urgent care coverage**, especially if you skate somewhere other than your home city — travel, visiting family, a weekend trip to a park you've been wanting to try.

**Annual or per-condition visit caps on physical therapy**, covered above, because this is the recurring cost most skaters underestimate.

**Durable medical equipment (DME) coverage**, which is the category that sometimes applies to custom or semi-custom braces for a documented joint issue. It's a narrow category, but worth asking about directly if you're managing something beyond an off-the-shelf compression sleeve.

## The honest bottom line

None of this is about chasing the cheapest premium or the most comprehensive plan in the abstract. It's about matching your plan to the specific, fairly predictable pattern of a sport where falls happen, recovery sometimes needs a professional, and the gear that helps you stay on the board is occasionally the same gear your insurer has a category for. Ten minutes with your plan's actual summary of benefits during enrollment beats a surprised phone call to your insurer in March.
