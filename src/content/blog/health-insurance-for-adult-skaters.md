Every November, open enrollment rolls around and most adult skaters treat their health plan the way they treat the fine print on a software update — something to click past. Then February arrives, a knee flares up, physical therapy gets recommended, and it's a scramble to figure out what's actually covered.

You don't need to become an insurance expert. You do need to ask a handful of specific questions before you lock in a plan for the year, because the honest truth is that returning to a fall-based sport in your 40s or 50s changes what you need from a policy. None of this is legal or financial advice — plans vary enormously by country, employer, and insurer — but the categories below are worth checking regardless of where you live.

## Why this is worth ten minutes of your enrollment window

Adult skaters tend to carry a specific injury profile: wrist and ankle sprains, overuse issues in knees and hips, the occasional fracture, and a genuine ongoing need for physical therapy rather than one-off urgent care visits. A plan built around "I rarely see a doctor" doesn't fit that pattern well, and the cost difference between plans is often smaller than people assume once you actually run the numbers for a year that includes a sprained ankle and six PT sessions.

## Questions to ask about physical therapy coverage

This is the one most skaters skip, and it's the one that matters most if you're managing a recurring issue.

**How many PT visits are covered per year, and does that reset with a new diagnosis or a new injury?** Some plans cap visits per condition, others cap total visits regardless of cause. If you're managing something chronic — a knee that flares with higher skating volume, a shoulder from an old fall — know which kind of plan you have.

**Is a physician referral required before PT is covered?** Plans that require a referral add a step (and a copay) you'll want to budget time for before you can start treatment, not after an injury is already slowing your recovery.

**What's the copay per visit, and is it different for in-network versus out-of-network providers?** A physiotherapist who specializes in sports or skate-specific rehab may be out-of-network. Worth knowing the cost difference before you need to decide under pressure.

## What's often eligible under an HSA or FSA

If your employer offers a Health Savings Account or Flexible Spending Account, a surprising amount of skate-adjacent gear can qualify as an eligible medical expense — but eligibility rules are specific and change, so confirm with your plan administrator before you buy, and keep receipts either way.

Items that are sometimes eligible, often with a Letter of Medical Necessity from a doctor: wrist guards and knee braces prescribed for an existing or recurring injury, orthotic inserts, and compression gear recommended as part of a treatment plan. A general "just in case" purchase of protective gear is far less likely to qualify than gear tied to a documented injury or a provider's recommendation — that documentation step is usually what decides it.

If you've already been through [a knee brace decision](/blog/knee-braces-for-adult-skaters/) or an [ankle sprain](/blog/ankle-sprains-adult-skaters/) this year, that paperwork from your provider is exactly what an FSA/HSA administrator will want to see attached to the claim.

## Documenting a recurring injury so it actually counts

Insurers and HSA administrators both respond better to a paper trail than a verbal explanation. If you're dealing with something that keeps coming back — a wrist that aches after longer sessions, a hip that needs a warm-up before it cooperates — a short, dated note from your doctor or physiotherapist describing the diagnosis and recommended treatment is worth getting even if you don't need it immediately. It turns "this happens sometimes" into something a claims reviewer can act on, and it's much easier to request while you're already in an appointment than to chase down six months later.

## What to actually compare between plans

When you're looking at open enrollment options side by side, skip the marketing summary and check these four numbers directly:

**The deductible for outpatient imaging.** An X-ray or MRI after a bad fall is routine in this sport. Know what you'd actually pay before you need one.

**Out-of-network urgent care coverage**, especially if you skate somewhere other than your home city — travel, visiting family, a weekend trip to a park you've been wanting to try.

**Annual or per-condition visit caps on physical therapy**, covered above, because this is the recurring cost most skaters underestimate.

**Durable medical equipment (DME) coverage**, which is the category that sometimes applies to custom or semi-custom braces for a documented joint issue. It's a narrow category, but worth asking about directly if you're managing something beyond an off-the-shelf compression sleeve.

## The honest bottom line

None of this is about chasing the cheapest premium or the most comprehensive plan in the abstract. It's about matching your plan to the specific, fairly predictable pattern of a sport where falls happen, recovery sometimes needs a professional, and the gear that helps you stay on the board is occasionally the same gear your insurer has a category for. Ten minutes with your plan's actual summary of benefits during enrollment beats a surprised phone call to your insurer in March.
