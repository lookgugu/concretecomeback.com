A pattern I see constantly in clinic, and constantly at the park, is the adult comeback that goes well for two weeks and then falls apart in the third. Not from a bad fall or an obvious mistake — from an ache that was ignorable on day twelve and became an actual injury by day twenty. The skater usually describes it as coming out of nowhere. It didn't. It came from a predictable, well-understood mismatch between two systems in your body that recover from a layoff at completely different speeds.

## Two clocks, running at different rates

When you come back to skating after months or years off, two separate adaptations have to happen, and they don't happen on the same timeline.

**Your cardiovascular system comes back fast.** Heart and lung efficiency respond to training quickly — meaningful improvements in a few weeks of consistent aerobic activity, with some of the earliest changes showing up within the first couple of weeks. This is why a lot of returning adults feel like they're "getting their fitness back" almost immediately. Pushing down a straightaway stops leaving you winded. You can skate for forty-five minutes instead of fifteen. That feeling is real, and it's also the trap.

**Your tendons and ligaments come back slowly.** Tendon is a different kind of tissue entirely — dense connective tissue with a much lower blood supply than muscle, which means it remodels on the order of months, not weeks, even under ideal, progressive loading. The research on tendon adaptation consistently puts meaningful stiffness changes at somewhere around two to three months of regular, graded loading, roughly ten times longer than your cardio needs to feel normal again.

That gap — weeks for your lungs, months for your Achilles, your patellar tendon, your wrist ligaments — is the connective tissue gap. It's not a flaw in your training. It's just how the tissue works, and it's the same for every returning adult, not a sign you're doing something wrong.

## Why week 3 specifically

Week one and two, most returning skaters are appropriately cautious. The soreness from skating again is fresh, the awkwardness is obvious, and nobody pushes hard because everything still feels new.

Week three is where it turns. Your cardio has caught up enough that sessions feel easy. Your balance and basic movement patterns have started coming back. You feel, correctly, more capable than you did in week one. What you don't feel — because there's no sensation that reliably signals it — is that your Achilles tendon, your ACL, your wrist ligaments are maybe 20% of the way through a remodeling process that takes two to three months. The feeling of readiness and the actual state of your connective tissue are telling you two different things, and the feeling is louder.

That's the week people add the bigger drop-in, the longer session, the trick they used to do without thinking about it. The cardiovascular system says yes. The tendon doesn't have a vote in that decision, because it doesn't communicate through effort or breathlessness. It communicates through pain, and by the time it does that, something is usually already strained.

## What this actually means for pacing

None of this is an argument against pushing your training. It's an argument for pushing the right variable at the right time.

**Increase duration and frequency before you increase intensity.** Skating longer, or skating more days a week, loads your cardiovascular system and your general movement patterns without necessarily demanding the sudden, forceful loading — a hard pop, a big drop, an aggressive carve — that puts the most strain on tendons and ligaments. Save the intensity increases for later in your return than feels strictly necessary.

**Expect the "I feel ready" feeling to arrive before your tissue actually is.** This is the single most useful thing to know going into a comeback. The feeling isn't wrong about your fitness. It's just not measuring the thing that actually determines your injury risk at week three.

**Warm up the specific tissue, not just your heart rate.** A general warm-up raises your heart rate and loosens your major muscles. It does less for the tendons and ligaments taking the specific, repetitive loads of pushing, popping, and landing. A few minutes of ankle circles, calf raises, and light loading of your wrists and knees before a session gives those slower-adapting tissues a head start the cardio warm-up doesn't.

**Treat week three to six as the highest-risk window, not week one.** If you're going to be conservative anywhere in a comeback, this is the stretch to do it in — not because you're weak at that point, but because it's exactly when the gap between how you feel and what your tissue can handle is widest. [The 8-week return plan for adult skaters](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/) builds its progression around exactly this mismatch, which is part of why it's structured in weekly stages rather than left open-ended.

**Don't mistake a plateau in soreness for a green light.** If your legs stop being sore between sessions, that's your muscles adapting, not your tendons finishing their much longer remodel. Use time elapsed and gradual progression as your guide for adding intensity, not how sore you do or don't feel the next day.

## The reassurance in this

The connective tissue gap isn't a reason to distrust your body during a comeback. It's a reason to trust a calendar over a feeling for a specific stretch of weeks. Your cardio genuinely is back faster than your tendons — that's not your imagination, and it's not you being unfit. It just means the thing to pace against for the first couple of months isn't how capable you feel on a given day. It's how long your tissue has actually had to adapt, which is a slower and far less exciting number than your improving stamina, but it's the one that actually determines whether week three ends in progress or in a setback.
