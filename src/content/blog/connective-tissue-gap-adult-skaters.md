A pattern I see constantly in clinic, and constantly at the park, is the adult comeback that goes well for two weeks and then falls apart in the third. Not from a bad fall or an obvious mistake — from an ache that was ignorable on day twelve and became an actual injury by day twenty. The skater usually describes it as coming out of nowhere. It didn't. It came from a predictable, well-understood mismatch between two systems in your body that recover from a layoff at completely different speeds.

## Two clocks, running at different rates

When you come back to skating after months or years off, two separate adaptations have to happen, and they don't happen on the same timeline.

**Your cardiovascular system comes back fast.** Heart and lung efficiency respond to training quickly — meaningful improvements in a few weeks of consistent aerobic activity, with some of the earliest changes showing up within the first couple of weeks. This is why a lot of returning adults feel like they're "getting their fitness back" almost immediately. Pushing down a straightaway stops leaving you winded. You can skate for forty-five minutes instead of fifteen. That feeling is real, and it's also the trap.

**Your tendons come back more slowly.** Tendon is a different kind of tissue entirely — dense connective tissue with a much lower blood supply than muscle. Research on tendon adaptation is mixed on exactly how long measurable stiffness changes take — some studies find changes within four to eight weeks of consistent, graded loading, others find it takes longer — but even the faster end of that range is still weeks behind the one-to-two-week timeline for cardiovascular fitness. Good evidence specifically on ligaments is thinner, but there's no reason to think they're quicker than tendon to adapt, so it's reasonable to extend the same caution to them.

That gap — a couple of weeks for your lungs, several more weeks on top of that for your Achilles, your patellar tendon, probably your wrist ligaments too — is the connective tissue gap. It's not a flaw in your training. It's just how the tissue works, and it's the same for every returning adult, not a sign you're doing something wrong.

## Why week three tends to be where it turns

Week one and two, most returning skaters are appropriately cautious. The soreness from skating again is fresh, the awkwardness is obvious, and nobody pushes hard because everything still feels new.

Week three is where it turns for a lot of returning skaters. Your cardio has caught up enough that sessions feel easy. Your balance and basic movement patterns have started coming back. You feel, correctly, more capable than you did in week one. What you don't feel — because there's no sensation that reliably signals it — is exactly how far along your Achilles tendon, your ACL, your wrist ligaments actually are in adapting to the new load. Nobody has run the kind of prospective injury study on adult comebacks that would pin an exact riskiest week, so take this as a plausible explanation drawn from how the two systems adapt at different rates, not a proven injury statistic — but the mismatch itself, cardio fast and tendon slower, is well established, and it points toward caution in the early-to-mid stretch of a comeback rather than easing off once the first couple of weeks feel fine.

That's the week people add the bigger drop-in, the longer session, the trick they used to do without thinking about it. The cardiovascular system says yes. The tendon doesn't have a vote in that decision, because it doesn't communicate through effort or breathlessness. It communicates through pain — and pain there is a signal to back off load and get it assessed if it persists, not proof that something has already torn. Waiting for pain before you adjust anything, though, means you've already let the gap between how you feel and what your tissue can handle run as wide as it's going to get.

## What this actually means for pacing

None of this is an argument against pushing your training. It's an argument for pushing the right variable at the right time.

**If you're going to add load, add intensity last — but duration and frequency are still load.** A longer session or an extra day a week doesn't demand the sudden, forceful loading — a hard pop, a big drop, an aggressive carve — that puts the most strain on tendons and ligaments, which is why intensity is the variable to hold back longest. That doesn't make duration and frequency free to increase however you like, though: both still need to go up gradually, with real recovery between sessions, or you can overuse your way into the same problem from a different direction.

**Expect the "I feel ready" feeling to arrive before your tissue actually is.** This is the single most useful thing to know going into a comeback. The feeling isn't wrong about your fitness. It just isn't measuring your tendons, so don't let it override gradual progression and attention to how a specific joint is actually responding.

**Warm up the specific tissue, not just your heart rate.** A general warm-up raises your heart rate and loosens your major muscles. It does less for the tendons and ligaments taking the specific, repetitive loads of pushing, popping, and landing. A few minutes of ankle circles, calf raises, and light loading of your wrists and knees before a session gives those slower-adapting tissues a head start the cardio warm-up doesn't.

**Stay as conservative in weeks three through six as you were in week one, not less.** There's no injury-surveillance data that pins the riskiest point down to an exact week, but the underlying adaptation timelines point the same direction: don't let your guard down just because the first couple of weeks went fine. [The 8-week return plan for adult skaters](/blog/8-week-return-plan-adult-skaters/) builds its progression around this same mismatch, which is part of why it's structured in weekly stages rather than left open-ended.

**Don't mistake a plateau in soreness for a green light.** If your legs stop being sore between sessions, that's your muscles adapting, not necessarily your tendons catching up at the same rate. Use gradual, graded increases as your default, and treat how a specific tendon or joint actually responds — any pain that lingers, swells, or shows up at the start of a session rather than easing out of it — as a more reliable signal than how fit or capable you feel in general.

## The reassurance in this

The connective tissue gap isn't a reason to distrust your body during a comeback. It's a reason not to let a feeling of general fitness talk you into skipping the gradual part. Your cardio genuinely is back faster than your tendons — that's not your imagination, and it's not you being unfit. There's no fixed week or percentage that tells you exactly when your tissue has caught up, so the honest answer is to increase load gradually regardless of how ready you feel, and to let how a joint or tendon actually responds — not a calendar, and not your cardio — be the thing that tells you whether to keep progressing or to back off.
