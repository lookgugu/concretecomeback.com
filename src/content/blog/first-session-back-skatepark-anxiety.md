I organize a group that exists specifically to get adults through this. And I still remember sitting in my car outside a skatepark for ten minutes before my first session back, board on the passenger seat, absolutely certain that walking in was a mistake.

Nothing had actually gone wrong yet. I hadn't fallen, hadn't been laughed at, hadn't done anything embarrassing. The anxiety wasn't a reaction to anything real. It was anticipatory, specific, and loud — the skatepark version of imposter syndrome — and it's one of the most common things new members of our group describe about their first day back, almost word for word.

This is different from the general, long-arc question of building confidence over months of skating. This is about one specific morning: the first time you show up somewhere public after years away, and your brain runs a very particular set of intrusive thoughts before you've even pushed off.

## What it actually sounds like

If you've felt this, the thoughts probably sound familiar: *Everyone's going to be watching me fall.* *I'm the oldest person here by twenty years.* *I used to be able to do this and now I can't even push properly.* *People are going to think I don't belong here.*

None of these thoughts are really about skateboarding. They're about being visibly bad at something in public, as an adult, in a culture that mostly expects adults to already be competent at the things they do. Kids get to be beginners in public without much self-consciousness about it. Somewhere between adolescence and middle age, most of us lose that permission.

Add in the specific gap of coming back after 10, 20, or 30 years, and there's a second layer: the mismatch between what your body remembers being able to do and what it can actually do right now. That gap is often more jarring than starting from zero would be, because you have a clear memory to measure yourself against.

## Why this one hits harder than general nervousness

A few things make the first session back specifically hard, in a way later sessions aren't:

**You don't have data yet.** By session five, you have real evidence: you didn't get hurt, nobody said anything, a couple of people were actually friendly. By session one, you have nothing but the anxious prediction, and anxious predictions are almost always worse than what happens.

**The stakes feel higher than they are.** Your brain treats "first impression at a new park" like it matters more than it does. In reality, almost nobody at a skatepark is tracking who's new. Regulars are focused on their own lines, their own sessions, their own friends.

**You're comparing two versions of yourself.** Not just "am I good enough compared to that teenager doing kickflips," but "am I as good as the 19-year-old version of me who used to skate here." That second comparison is the one that actually stings, and it's one nobody else at the park is making about you — they have no idea who you used to be.

## A ritual for the specific morning of session one

This isn't about eliminating the anxiety. It's about having a plan that works whether the anxiety shows up or not.

**Go at a quiet time.** Weekday mornings or early afternoons, before school lets out, tend to be lighter and skew older. Fewer people around means less to feel watched by, and it's genuinely easier to find your footing without an audience.

**Sit in the car for five minutes on purpose, then get out.** If the sit-in-the-car moment is going to happen anyway, put a cap on it. Set a timer. When it goes off, you get out, whatever you're feeling.

**Watch for ten minutes before you skate.** This isn't stalling — it's information gathering. You'll see the terrain, the flow, who's using which section, and you'll almost always notice that nobody's watching the parking lot for new arrivals. That observation alone does a lot of the work.

**Pick one small, boring goal.** Not a trick. Something like "push around the flat ground for ten minutes and see how it feels" or "do five slow laps of the smallest bowl." A low bar means you're likely to clear it, and clearing it is what actually starts to quiet the anxious prediction.

**Say hello to one person.** It doesn't have to be a conversation. A nod and a "how's it going" to whoever's nearest does more to make a park feel like a place you belong than any amount of skating well would. Most skaters, at any age, respond to this warmly — it's a small, low-stakes way to test the "nobody wants me here" thought against reality.

**Give yourself permission to leave early.** If it's genuinely too much, twenty minutes counts as a session. Leaving isn't failing. It's still more than the version of you who stayed in the car.

## What actually happens, most of the time

Almost universally, the people I've watched go through this — including myself — report the same thing afterward: the session itself was fine. Nobody said anything. A couple of people were friendly. The catastrophe their brain had rehearsed simply didn't happen, because it was never really about the skatepark. It was about the anticipation.

That doesn't make the anticipation any less real going in. But it's worth knowing, walking into session one, that the version of events your anxiety is predicting is almost never the version you'll actually get.

## It gets quieter fast

This specific, acute version of the anxiety is mostly a first-session and maybe second-session phenomenon. By session three or four, you have your own evidence to work from instead of a prediction, and that evidence tends to be reassuring. The long-term work of [building real confidence on a board](/blog/building-confidence-at-40/) comes after this — but you have to get through the parking lot first. Set the timer, watch for ten minutes, say hello to one person, and go.
