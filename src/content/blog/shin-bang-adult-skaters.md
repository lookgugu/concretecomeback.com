Every skate safety guide covers wrists. Most cover ankles, knees, hips, even collarbones. Almost none of them mention the injury that will probably happen to you first, and happen often: the board flipping up and catching you square in the shin.

Skaters call it shin bang, and if you've been back on a board for more than a few weeks, you already know the feeling without needing it described. Here's why it happens, why it's worse for adults, and what actually helps.

## What's actually happening

Shin bang happens when the tail or edge of the board strikes the front of your shin, usually during a bail, a missed trick, or an awkward dismount where the board doesn't go where your feet expect. The front of the shin — the tibia — sits right under the skin with almost no muscle padding to absorb an impact, which is exactly why it hurts disproportionately to the size of the hit. A board edge catching you there delivers concentrated force directly onto bone and the thin layer of tissue over it.

The result is usually a combination of a sharp, immediate pain response, followed by swelling, and often a deep, spreading bruise that can look far more dramatic a day or two later than it did in the moment. Repeated minor hits in the same spot, which is extremely common for anyone learning flip tricks or still inconsistent with their landings, can turn one sore spot into a chronically tender area that takes weeks to fully settle.

## Why adults feel it more

Younger skaters get shin bang too, but a few things make it a bigger deal for adult returners specifically. Skin and subcutaneous tissue thin out somewhat with age, leaving even less natural cushioning over the shin bone than a teenager has. Healing from soft-tissue bruising also slows down, so a hit that a 16-year-old shrugs off in three days can leave an adult sore and tender for one to two weeks. And because adults are more likely to be relearning tricks they haven't attempted in years — or attempting new ones that were never natural to them — the repetitive miss-and-recatch pattern that causes shin bang in the first place happens more, not less, during a comeback.

## When it's just a bruise, and when it's something more

The overwhelming majority of shin bang is a straightforward, if painful, soft-tissue bruise: swelling, tenderness, and a bruise that changes color over the following week or two, with no sharp point of bone tenderness and no change in sensation below the injury.

Pay attention, though, if any of the following shows up: pain that keeps getting worse rather than settling over the first 24 to 48 hours, a lower leg that feels unusually tight, swollen, or hard compared to the other side, numbness or tingling in the foot, or pain that feels dramatically out of proportion to the hit itself, especially with increasing tightness. Those are the warning signs of compartment syndrome — a rare but serious buildup of pressure in the muscle compartments of the lower leg that needs emergency evaluation, not home management. It's uncommon, but it's the reason "it's probably just a bruise" isn't a safe default if the leg is behaving in any of those ways. Sharp, localized pain directly over the bone itself, rather than spread across the soft tissue, is also worth getting checked — a hard enough hit can occasionally cause a stress reaction or, rarely, a fracture, and that's a different recovery timeline entirely.

## Treating ordinary shin bang

For the standard bruise-and-swelling version, the same POLICE framework used for sprains applies here in a lighter form: protect the area from more direct hits while it's tender, keep moving within what's comfortable rather than going fully sedentary, ice for short periods in the first day or two, and elevate when you can. Compression sleeves can help with the swelling and also add a small layer of protection against re-hitting the same spot before it's healed.

Most shin bang resolves on its own timeline — several days for a mild hit, one to two weeks for a harder one — without needing more than basic care. What it does need is patience: hitting the same half-healed spot again resets the clock and tends to make each subsequent hit feel worse than the last.

## The gear that actually helps

Dedicated skate shin guards exist and have genuinely improved in recent years — slimmer, less bulky designs that sit under pants without looking like hockey gear, built specifically for the kind of glancing board-edge impact skating produces rather than the blunt, high-force hits shin guards for other sports are designed around. If you're in the early, trick-learning phase where shin bang happens most, or if you've already got a sore spot you're trying to protect while it heals, they're worth the investment.

Short of dedicated guards, thicker socks pulled up over the shin and slightly looser-fitting pants that don't bind against a tender spot both help in the meantime. What doesn't help much is toughing it out on the theory that your shins will "adapt" — they don't develop meaningful extra padding no matter how many times you hit them. They just accumulate soreness.

## The part that actually fixes it

Gear reduces the damage, but the real fix is technique: bailing cleanly by stepping or jumping off the board deliberately, rather than letting it flip up underneath you as you fall. That's a skill in itself, one most returning adults never practiced because it wasn't something anyone taught explicitly. Spend a few sessions on flat ground deliberately practicing controlled bail-outs — stepping away from the board rather than trying to ride out every wobble — and the frequency of shin bang drops noticeably, long before any piece of gear does the same job.

It's not a dramatic injury, and it won't end your comeback. But it's the one nobody warns you about, and it's worth knowing what's normal, what's not, and how to make it happen less.
