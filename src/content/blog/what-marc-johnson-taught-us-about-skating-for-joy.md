Marc Johnson died on May 26, 2026, at 49. If that name doesn't mean much to you, that's fine — this isn't a post for people who already know the catalog. It's a post about the specific thing he was known for among people who skated through the 2000s, because that thing is more useful to a 45-year-old coming back to a board than almost anything a current pro could teach you.

Johnson started skating in 1990 at 13, growing up in Burke County, North Carolina. He turned pro, co-founded Enjoi with Rodney Mullen in 2000, and built a career that peaked with his closing part in *Fully Flared* in 2007 — a part still widely cited as one of the best skate video parts ever filmed, and the one that earned him Thrasher's Skater of the Year award that year. His friend Louie Barletta said Johnson had seemed sober, healthy, and full of life when the two spent time together in San Jose less than a month before he died. A cause of death hasn't been made public, and I'm not going to speculate on it here.

What I want to talk about instead is why people who watched him skate for twenty years keep using the same word to describe it: style.

## Difficulty was never the point

Street skating at the turn of the millennium was loud. A lot of the era's defining footage was built around raw difficulty and aggression — bigger stair sets, gnarlier slams, a kind of macho escalation that made "hardest trick" the obvious measure of who mattered.

Johnson skated differently. People who write about him reach for words like balletic and idiosyncratic — a rider who looked like he was levitating over his board rather than fighting it, who brought elements of freestyle into ledge spots that other skaters were just trying to grind as hard as possible. He wasn't skating the easiest tricks available to him. He was skating in a way that prioritized how something looked and felt over how much it would impress a judge counting stair counts.

That distinction — difficulty versus quality of movement — is the entire thing worth borrowing.

## Why this is the right mindset at 45, not just a nice one

If you're coming back to skating as an adult, the difficulty-first framework is actively working against you. You are not going to out-trick a 19-year-old who's been skating every day since he was eight. If you measure your sessions by whether you're closing the gap on what the best skater at the park can do, you will lose that comparison every single time, and it will make the whole thing feel worse instead of better.

Johnson's approach offers a different scoreboard. A clean, controlled line through a section of the park — one that flows, where your weight is in the right place, where you're not fighting the board — is a meaningful accomplishment regardless of how technically difficult the individual moves are. You can chase that kind of quality at any age, with any amount of time back on a board. It doesn't require catching up to anyone.

This isn't a consolation prize for people who can't do the harder version. Plenty of skaters who could do genuinely difficult tricks have said Johnson's less difficult, better-executed lines were more interesting to watch than someone else's harder trick done awkwardly. Quality of movement is its own standard, not a fallback for people who've run out of difficulty to chase.

## What it looks like in practice

A few ways this translates to an actual session, rather than staying a nice idea:

**Pick fewer things and do them better.** Instead of adding a new trick to the list every session, spend a week making a trick you can already land look cleaner — more controlled pop, a smoother landing, less wasted motion. That's a Johnson-shaped goal, not a difficulty-shaped one.

**Carve for the sake of carving.** A long, controlled carve across a bowl or a flat section isn't a trick and won't show up on anyone's trick list, but doing it well is genuinely satisfying, and it's exactly the kind of thing that doesn't care how old you are.

**Stop treating "easy" tricks as beneath you.** If an ollie or a kickturn still feels slightly sloppy, there's more room there than in trying to force a harder trick your body isn't ready for. Making the simple thing look effortless is the harder skill, not the lesser one.

**Notice when you're skating for a scoreboard that isn't yours.** If you catch yourself feeling bad because a teenager two feet away just did something you'll never do, that's the difficulty-first mindset talking. It's not the one that's going to keep you coming back in ten years.

## The thing worth keeping

Nobody remembers Marc Johnson because he did the hardest trick anyone had seen. They remember him because watching him skate felt different from watching most other people skate — unhurried, considered, clearly his own. That's not a description of raw talent. It's a description of a choice about what to value.

You get to make that same choice every time you push off. Difficulty isn't the only currency a session can be worth something in. If you're forty-five and three months back on a board, chasing cleaner and more controlled over bigger and harder is not a lesser version of skating. By at least one account of the sport's best-remembered rider, it might be the better one.
