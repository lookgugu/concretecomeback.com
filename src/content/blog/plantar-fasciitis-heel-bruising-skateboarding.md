Foot and heel pain stops more older skaters mid-comeback than almost any acute ankle sprain, and it usually comes from one of two distinct mechanisms: **plantar fasciopathy** (gradual overload of the thick connective tissue band under the arch) or a **calcaneal fat-pad contusion** (a bruised heel pad from repetitive hard run-outs or flat landings). Distinguishing between those two conditions, and knowing when sudden heel pain after a bail requires an X-ray to rule out a calcaneal fracture, determines whether you need progressive tendon-style loading or immediate impact rest.

## Why skateboarding loads the pushing foot and board foot differently

Unlike running, where both feet cycle through identical heel-to-toe strides, skateboarding splits mechanical load unevenly between your front (board) foot and your back (pushing and popping) foot:

### The pushing foot: repetitive forefoot propulsion and eccentric braking

Every push asks your back foot to strike the pavement, absorb ground reaction force through a plantarflexed ankle, and propel your bodyweight forward while the plantar fascia winds tight around the first metatarsophalangeal (big toe) joint via the windlass mechanism. If you also foot-brake on rough asphalt, that same heel pad and plantar fascia absorb repeated friction and vertical vibration.

### The board foot: sustained isometric grip and axial landing impact

Your front foot stays planted on coarse grip tape inside a flat-soled shoe, making constant micro-adjustments across the subtalar and midfoot joints to steer the trucks. When a trick or transition run goes sideways and you bail onto flat concrete, both heels take a sudden axial spike that thin vulcanized outsoles barely attenuate. We covered the footwear side of that equation in [our guide to skate shoe cushioning and insoles for older skaters](/blog/skate-shoes-for-adults-over-40/) and our comparison of [cupsole vs. vulcanized skate shoes](/blog/cupsole-vs-vulcanized-skate-shoes-older-skaters/); here the focus is on what happens inside the foot tissues themselves.

## Telling plantar fasciitis, a heel bruise, and a fracture apart

Before trying any self-care or rehab exercise, check where the pain sits, when it peaks, and how it started.

### 1. Plantar fasciitis (plantar fasciopathy)

Plantar fasciitis typically develops gradually over days or weeks rather than from a single hard landing. Its hallmark is sharp or tight pain at the **medial calcaneal tubercle** (the inside front edge of the heel bone where the fascia attaches) during your **first few steps out of bed in the morning** or after sitting at a desk. It often warms up and feels milder after ten minutes of walking or early in a skate session, only to ache again that evening or the next morning.

### 2. Calcaneal fat-pad contusion (heel bruise)

A bruised heel fat pad usually traces back to a specific session involving repeated bailouts, Primo landings, or pushing on hard concrete in worn-out shoes. Unlike plantar fasciitis, fat-pad pain sits directly under the **center of the heel pad** or slightly toward the outer rim, feels like stepping on a stone, and **gets progressively worse the longer you stand, walk, or skate on it**. It does not "warm up" mid-session; every additional vertical impact adds soreness.

### 3. Red flags that require medical imaging or urgent evaluation

Do not treat heel or foot pain as a routine bruise or plantar fascia flare if any of the following apply:

- If you cannot take four steps immediately after landing hard on your heel, or if you have direct bony tenderness when squeezing the sides of the calcaneus (heel bone), seek same-day medical evaluation and imaging to rule out an acute calcaneal fracture or talar injury. If you have osteoporosis, osteopenia, or low bone mineral density, get evaluated after any painful heel impact even if you can still limp on it.
- A sudden tearing or popping sensation in the arch during a push or landing accompanied by bruising or a visible drop in the arch can indicate an acute plantar fascia rupture, and a pop at the back of the ankle with weakness pushing off the toes requires prompt evaluation for an Achilles tendon tear.
- Deep bony heel pain that builds over weeks without a single fall, aches at rest or at night, and hurts when both sides of the heel bone are squeezed together (a positive calcaneal squeeze test) warrants a clinical assessment to rule out a calcaneal stress fracture.
- Burning pain, tingling, or numbness radiating into the sole or toes can point to nerve entrapment (such as Baxter's nerve or tarsal tunnel syndrome) or lumbar radiculopathy rather than plantar fasciitis. If you have diabetes, peripheral neuropathy, poor circulation, or bilateral heel pain accompanied by morning stiffness in other joints, see a physician before starting self-directed loading.

## Evidence-based rehab for plantar fasciitis: the Rathleff high-load protocol

For decades, standard advice for plantar fasciitis consisted solely of rolling the arch on a frozen water bottle and stretching the calf. Stretching helps short-term morning stiffness, but degenerated collagen in the plantar fascia responds much like Achilles or patellar tendinopathy: it needs controlled, progressive tensile load to remodel.

In a 2015 randomized controlled trial published in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*, Michael Rathleff and colleagues compared 48 patients with ultrasound-confirmed plantar fasciitis split into two groups of 24: one group used silicone heel inserts plus daily plantar-specific stretching, while the second group used heel inserts plus **high-load progressive strength training** performed every other day. At the three-month mark, the high-load strength group reported significantly larger improvements in Foot Function Index pain scores than the stretching-only group (with both groups continuing to improve at twelve months).

Note the boundaries of that trial: it studied 48 general-population participants rather than skateboarders specifically, and improvements took weeks of consistent every-other-day loading rather than days. Still, its mechanical principle translates directly to older skaters whose plantar fascia needs load tolerance for pushing.

### How to perform the modified heel raise safely

Only start this exercise if acute fracture and rupture red flags have been ruled out, and keep discomfort during the movement mild (no higher than 3 out of 10 on a pain scale, with no spike in first-step morning pain the next day):

1. Roll up a small hand towel and place it on the edge of a stair step or flat floor so that when you stand on the ball of your foot, **your toes are gently extended upward (dorsiflexed)** by the towel while your heel hangs free (or rests on the floor if starting flat). Extending the toes engages the windlass mechanism so the plantar fascia shares the load with the calf.
2. Holding a wall or railing for balance, take **three seconds to rise up** onto the ball of your foot, hold **two seconds at the top**, and take **three slow seconds to lower** your heel down.
3. Perform this **every second day** (not daily, as the fascia needs 48 hours between loading sessions), starting with 3 sets of 12 slow repetitions using bodyweight before ever considering added weight. Stop and consult a physiotherapist if pain sharpens or radiates.

## How to manage a bruised heel fat pad

Because a calcaneal fat-pad contusion is a compression injury rather than a tensile overload, doing heavy heel raises on a bruised heel edge will only irritate it further. A bruised fat pad needs de-loading from vertical impact:

1. Switch to flat, smooth concrete where you never have to run out a high-speed bail, or take seven to fourteen days off the board until walking barefoot on a hard floor no longer reproduces the stone-bruise pain.
2. A soft flat foam insert flattens out under bodyweight. A structured insole or silicone heel cup with raised sidewalls gathers the heel's natural adipose tissue underneath the calcaneus so the fat pad cannot splay outward on impact.
3. Limited ankle bend forces your heel to slap or lift early during a crouch, concentrating pressure on smaller patches of the foot. Pairing foot recovery with [ankle stability and sprain prevention work for older skaters](/blog/ankle-sprains-adult-skaters/) and [a proper pre-session warm-up](/blog/warm-up-routine-for-adult-skaters/) keeps load distributed across the whole foot when you step back on the deck.
