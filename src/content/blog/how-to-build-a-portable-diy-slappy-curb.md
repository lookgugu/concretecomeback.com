Not everyone has the yard space, budget, or neighborhood zoning to build a full backyard transition, but almost anyone with a flat driveway, a single-car garage bay, or a quiet cul-de-sac has room for a portable six-foot slappy curb. At six inches tall, a low curb or parking block is the most joint-friendly obstacle in skateboarding: you do not even have to ollie onto it, the drop back to flatground is shorter than a single porch step, and you can slide it against the garage wall in two minutes when you are done. Building one yourself over a Saturday morning gives you a year-round home practice spot for twenty-minute after-work sessions.

If you have the space and budget for transitions, start with our guide to [building a backyard mini ramp](/blog/building-a-backyard-mini-ramp/). If you want a compact, low-impact obstacle you can store beside your lawnmower and skate whenever you have fifteen spare minutes, here is how to put together a portable slappy curb that actually grinds cleanly.

## Why a low curb is the ideal home obstacle after 40

Most street obstacles punish rusty timing because they require a high pop before you can even test the lock-in. A slappy curb removes the ollie entirely: you carve at the angled edge at a shallow 20- to 30-degree approach, bash your front wheel and truck hanger up onto the corner by shifting your weight from heels to toes (frontside) or toes to heels (backside), and let your momentum carry the grind.

Because the obstacle sits only five to six inches off the ground, the consequences of sticking or slipping out are minimal compared to a two-foot ledge or a steep quarter pipe. Practicing that weight shift at home directly builds the truck-lock feel you need when you step up to [learning axle stalls and 50-50 grinds](/blog/learn-axle-stalls-50-50-grinds-over-40/) on park transitions, and it slots naturally into the middle rungs of our [beginner trick ladder for older skaters](/blog/beginner-trick-ladder-for-adult-skaters/).

## Two ways to build a portable home slappy curb

Depending on whether your driveway is smooth broom-finished concrete or slightly rough asphalt, you can build a portable curb in one of two ways: a pre-cast concrete parking block mounted on a wooden sled, or a lightweight framed wood curb capped with steel angle iron or PVC coping.

### Option 1: The concrete parking-block sled (best for true concrete grind feel)
A standard six-foot pre-cast concrete parking bumper from a local masonry yard or building-supply distributor runs about 40 dollars a block at contractor pricing (expect somewhat more for a single unit) and has the exact sloped face that makes slappy grinds work without ollieing. The catch is weight: suppliers list a solid six-foot block anywhere from about 125 to 250 pounds depending on the mix, which makes it tough to move solo and prone to gouging a finished garage floor.

To make it portable and slightly taller for clean truck lock-ins, mount the block onto a base cut from a single two-by-ten or two-by-twelve pressure-treated plank:
1. Cut a 72-inch length of pressure-treated two-by-ten lumber so it matches the footprint of the parking block.
2. Drop two half-inch carriage bolts or concrete anchor pins down through the factory rebar holes molded into the parking block and secure them through recessed washers on the underside of the wood base.
3. Screw a pair of heavy-duty recessed lifting handles and two fixed rubber wheels onto one end of the wood base so they sit half an inch off the ground when the curb is flat, engaging only when you tilt the opposite end up like a wheelbarrow.
4. Glue two strips of rubber stall mat or nonslip drawer liner to the bottom of the plank so the curb never slides back when you bash your trucks into it at speed.

### Option 2: The framed wood-and-angle-iron curb (lightweight and garage-friendly)
If you need to carry the curb in and out of a trunk or up basement stairs by yourself, build a hollow six-foot box that mimics parking-block geometry at a third of the weight:
1. Frame a six-foot-long, ten-inch-wide, five-inch-tall box using kiln-dried two-by-four studs spaced twelve inches on center, ripping the front rail at a 15-degree bevel so your wheels roll up into the coping rather than hitting a vertical wall.
2. Skin the top and beveled front face with three-quarter-inch exterior birch or marine plywood, countersinking every deck screw so nothing catches a wheel.
3. Cap the top grinding edge with a six-foot length of two-by-two-inch, eighth-inch-thick mild steel angle iron (drilled and countersunk every sixteen inches) or heavy-wall schedule-80 PVC pipe split lengthwise if you want a quieter, zero-wax slide inside a residential garage.
4. Seal the wood with exterior porch enamel so morning dew or damp tires in the garage do not warp the frame.

## Prepping a concrete edge so your trucks actually grind

Raw concrete out of a masonry yard is porous and chalky; if you rub a block of skate wax directly onto an untreated parking block, the concrete simply chews the wax up and stops your hanger dead. Before your first session, take thirty minutes to prep the grinding edge properly:
1. Rub a spare piece of concrete brick or a coarse 60-grit sanding block along the top six inches of the sloped face to knock down any sharp mold seams or protruding aggregate stones.
2. Roll two coats of clear acrylic concrete sealer or spray lacquer along the grinding edge and let it cure overnight. The sealer fills the microscopic pores so your trucks slide across a smooth shell rather than grinding into raw sand.
3. Once cured, apply a light coat of curb wax to both the top bevel and the vertical face two inches below it where your wheels rub during a slide or grind.

Slappy curbs reward a wheel that rolls fast over driveway expansion joints while still sliding cleanly when your wheel rubs the vertical face of the block.
