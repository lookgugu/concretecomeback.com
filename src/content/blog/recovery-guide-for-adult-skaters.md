The first time I skated for a full hour after my twenty-year break, I felt fine that evening. The next morning, I understood why people warn you about this specifically.

Nothing was injured. I hadn't fallen badly. I was just sore in places I didn't know could be sore — the outside of my ankles, my forearms, a spot in my lower back I couldn't explain. The mechanics of skating come back faster than you expect. The next-day experience is its own adjustment.

## Why the day after is worse at 40+

Skating uses muscles and stabilisers that most adult daily life doesn't touch — the small muscles around your ankles that make constant micro-corrections, your forearms from bracing and balance, your core from staying centered over a moving board. When you use those muscles hard after months or years of not using them at all, you get delayed-onset muscle soreness, the kind that shows up 12 to 48 hours later rather than immediately.

This is a normal training response. It happens to teenagers too; the difference is that an adult body takes longer to clear the inflammation and repair the tissue, so the soreness lingers and the temptation to worry about it is higher.

## What's normal

Expect some combination of the following after a session, especially early on:

- **General muscle soreness** in the calves, forearms, glutes, and lower back that peaks around 24–48 hours after skating and fades over the following few days
- **Mild stiffness** in the ankles and knees, especially first thing in the morning, that loosens up once you're moving
- **Tiredness** that's more than you'd expect from an hour of what looked like gentle rolling around — balance work is more taxing than it looks

All of this is your body adapting. It will happen less, and fade faster, as you skate more consistently.

## What's not normal

See a doctor or physio rather than waiting it out if you notice:

- **Sharp, localized pain** rather than general soreness — soreness is usually diffuse across a muscle; a specific point of pain is more likely to be an actual injury
- **Swelling in a joint**, especially a knee, ankle, or wrist, that doesn't start going down within a day or two
- **Pain that gets worse** rather than better over the days following a session, instead of following the normal soreness curve of peaking and then improving
- **Numbness or tingling**, particularly in the wrist or hand after a fall, which can indicate nerve involvement rather than ordinary bruising
- **Pain that changes how you walk or move** outside of skating

The general rule: soreness is dull, diffuse, and improves day over day. Injury is sharp, localized, or gets worse. When in doubt, get it checked rather than guessing.

## What actually helps recovery

**Move the next day, gently.** Complete rest the day after a session often makes stiffness worse. A walk, some light stretching, or an easy bike ride keeps blood moving through sore tissue and speeds up how quickly it clears.

**Hydrate properly.** You lose more fluid during a session than it feels like, especially outdoors in warm weather, and dehydration makes muscle recovery slower and cramping more likely.

**Prioritize sleep.** Most of the physical repair work your body does after exercise happens during sleep. If you're skating regularly and not sleeping well, that's usually the first thing to fix before anything else.

**Eat enough protein.** Muscle repair requires protein as a raw material. You don't need to overthink this — a normal, adequate intake across the day is enough for the level of activity most returning adult skaters are doing.

**Use heat for stiffness, ice for acute swelling.** If you're generally stiff and achy, warmth — a hot shower, a heating pad — helps loosen tissue and eases discomfort. If you've taken a specific hit and there's localized swelling, ice that spot instead.

**Don't stack hard sessions back to back early on.** When you're rebuilding, skating hard two days in a row before your body has adapted is the most common way people talk themselves into an unnecessary week off. Space out your first several weeks of sessions — every other day is a reasonable starting cadence — and let the frequency increase as your recovery time shortens.

## Pacing your comeback

The soreness curve tells you where you are relative to your body's baseline. Early on, everything is sore and recovery takes days. Over a few months of consistent skating the soreness shrinks, clears faster, and eventually shows up only after unusually long or hard sessions.

That shift is a better measure of getting back into skating shape than how your tricks look. Track it loosely — how sore, how long — and you'll have a reasonable picture of your own progress.
