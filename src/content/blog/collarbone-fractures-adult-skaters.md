Wrists get the warnings. Ankles get their own guide. The collarbone quietly ends up on crutches of its own kind, and almost nobody saw it coming until the fall that did it.

It's a predictable injury once you understand the mechanics, and a frustrating one to go through unprepared. Here's what you need to know before it happens, and what to actually expect if it already has.

## Why this bone, and why adults especially

A collarbone fracture usually happens one of two ways: landing directly on the point of the shoulder, or an outstretched arm transmitting the force of a fall up through the arm and into the clavicle itself. Both are common in skating falls — a backward spill where you reach out to catch yourself, or a shoulder-first landing when you don't get your hands under you in time.

Adult skaters are more exposed to this mechanism than younger ones for a simple reason: reaction time slows with age, and the instinct to brace with an outstretched arm often fires a beat too late or too early relative to how the fall is actually unfolding. Bone density also declines gradually from the 40s onward, so the same impact that would bend a 20-year-old's clavicle can crack a 45-year-old's. Neither of these is a reason to stop skating. It's a reason to take falling technique seriously rather than treating it as something you'll just figure out instinctively.

## What it feels like, and how to tell it's this and not a shoulder injury

A clavicle fracture usually announces itself clearly: sharp, localized pain right along the collarbone, often with visible swelling or a bump at the fracture site, and a strong reluctance to lift the arm away from the body at all. Many people instinctively cradle the injured arm against their chest with the other hand — that posture alone is a useful clue.

It's easy to confuse with a shoulder dislocation or an AC joint sprain, both of which also follow shoulder-first falls. The distinguishing feature is usually location: a clavicle fracture hurts along the collarbone itself, often with a palpable step or deformity you can see or feel through the skin, while a shoulder or AC joint injury concentrates pain at the joint. Either way, this isn't something to self-diagnose with confidence. Get it imaged.

## The first steps

Any suspected clavicle fracture needs an X-ray before anything else gets decided. Until you've had one, immobilize the arm against your body — a sling, or even a jacket wrapped to hold the arm still, works for the trip to urgent care or the ER. Don't try to test the range of motion or "walk it off." Ice helps with pain and swelling in the meantime, applied with a barrier between ice and skin.

Most clavicle fractures in adults are managed without surgery: a sling for several weeks, pain management, and a gradual return to motion as the bone heals. Fractures that are significantly displaced, shortened, or that involve the skin breaking sometimes need surgical fixation with a plate and screws — that decision belongs to an orthopedist reading your actual imaging, not a general rule of thumb.

## What the sling period actually involves

This is the part that surprises people: a non-displaced clavicle fracture typically stays in a sling for somewhere in the range of four to six weeks, with some adults needing longer, especially if the fracture is more complex or healing is slower than average. During that window your job isn't to rest the whole arm into stiffness — it's to follow whatever graded mobility your provider clears you for, usually starting with gentle pendulum exercises for the elbow and hand while the shoulder itself stays protected.

Full healing, meaning bone strength adequate for the kind of unpredictable loading skating demands, generally takes longer than the sling period itself — often two to three months before a provider is comfortable clearing contact-level activity, and sometimes more. That gap between "out of the sling" and "actually ready to skate again" is where a lot of adult skaters get into trouble, because the shoulder can look and feel mobile well before the bone has the structural integrity to handle a hard fall onto it.

## Rebuilding before you go back

Once you're cleared for strengthening, the shoulder and the muscles around the collarbone — the trapezius, deltoid, and the smaller stabilizers of the shoulder blade — need deliberate rebuilding. Weeks in a sling cost you strength and range of motion that doesn't come back just by waiting. A physiotherapist can guide progressive loading; if you don't have access to one, start with gentle range-of-motion work and very light resistance band exercises well before you consider anything that involves falling again.

Before you actually push off, you want full pain-free range of motion in the shoulder, strength that feels close to symmetrical with your uninjured side, and confidence that you could brace a fall without immediately protecting the area out of fear. That last one matters more than it sounds — a shoulder that's structurally healed but that you're still flinching to protect isn't really ready, because hesitation changes how you fall.

## Reducing the odds of a repeat

You can't eliminate this risk, but you can shift it. Falling technique — tucking in, rolling rather than reaching straight out, keeping elbows bent instead of locked — redirects force away from a straight shot into the collarbone. Padded shirts and some impact-rated gear built for shoulder and upper-body protection exist and are worth considering if you've already broken a collarbone once; a second fracture in the same bone, or a break in the other collarbone from compensating awkwardly, is a real pattern among skaters who come back without changing anything about how they fall.

A broken collarbone isn't a reason to give up your comeback. It is a reason to respect the actual timeline rather than the one you'd prefer, and to use the recovery to fix the habits that led to the fall in the first place.
