When a board shoots out on transition or stops dead on a stone, the collarbone can take a direct hit that a thin strut between the breastbone and the shoulder blade handles poorly. Skaters prepare for wrist and ankle injuries because those joints feel exposed every time you step on a deck. The clavicle rarely gets attention until a fall drives the outside of the shoulder into concrete.

Knowing what fracture studies actually measure, which symptoms require urgent medical care, and why everyday arm comfort returns before the bone is ready for another fall helps you manage recovery without rushing back too soon.

## What studies measure about how the collarbone breaks

It is easy to assume a broken collarbone comes mainly from catching yourself with an outstretched hand. Force can travel up the arm that way, but clinical data point to a more direct cause in most cases. In a [1988 study in the *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3372571/) of 150 patients with clavicle fractures, 122 could give a clear account of how they fell: 87 percent landed directly on the point of the shoulder, 7 percent took a direct blow, and 6 percent fell onto an outstretched hand. When the outside of the shoulder hits the ground first, the impact compresses the collarbone inward toward the breastbone.

In a [prospective study of 1,000 consecutive adult clavicle fractures in Edinburgh](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9619941/), 69.2 percent occurred in the middle three-fifths of the bone, 28.0 percent in the outer fifth near the shoulder joint, and 2.8 percent in the inner fifth. Midshaft fractures were more common in younger adults after sports and road impacts, while outer-fifth fractures made up a larger share in older adults after simple falls. Neither study looked specifically at skateboarders over 40, so they do not give an individual skater's fracture risk, and age-related decreases in bone density vary from person to person. Still, the basic mechanics are clear: landing on the point of the shoulder sends force straight along the collarbone.

## What requires urgent care versus prompt imaging

Any suspected collarbone fracture needs medical assessment and an X-ray. Pain alone cannot tell you whether the bone is aligned, shortened, or broken near the outer end, and an acromioclavicular (AC) joint sprain or shoulder dislocation can follow the same kind of fall. Sharp pain along the collarbone, swelling or a visible bump, bruising, and needing to support the elbow with your other hand are common reasons to get imaged promptly.

Seek emergency medical care rather than waiting for a routine clinic visit if any of these warning signs appear after a fall:

- **Skin tenting or an open wound:** The bone is pushing the skin into a tight, pale point or has broken through the skin.
- **Shortness of breath or chest pain:** Breathing difficulty or sharp chest pain after a hard impact near the chest wall needs immediate evaluation.
- **Numbness, tingling, weakness, or a pale, cold hand:** Changes in sensation, strength, or circulation down the arm require urgent assessment.

Until you have been seen, [orthopaedic patient guidance from AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/diseases--conditions/clavicle-fracture-broken-collarbone/) notes that a sling or support holds the arm for comfort while you arrange evaluation; do not try to lift the arm overhead to test it, and use an ice pack wrapped in cloth for brief intervals if needed for pain.

## Sling management, surgery, and early movement

Whether an adult clavicle fracture is treated in a sling alone or discussed with an orthopaedic surgeon depends on which part of the bone broke and how far the pieces shifted. Undisplaced or minimally displaced fractures are usually managed without surgery in a sling while early healing takes place. Completely displaced midshaft fractures and unstable outer-end fractures have higher rates of nonunion (failing to heal across the gap) when managed with a sling alone.

In a [2007 multicentre randomized trial by the Canadian Orthopaedic Trauma Society](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17200303/) of 132 adults aged 16 to 60 with completely displaced midshaft fractures, the operative group had fewer nonunions (2 of 62 operative patients versus 7 of 49 non-operative patients analysed at one year) and 0 cases of symptomatic malunion versus 9 in the non-operative group in that trial, while carrying surgical and hardware complications that led to plate removal in some patients. Because that trial excluded adults over 60 and studied only completely displaced midshaft breaks, its results are not a rule for every older skater. An orthopaedist reading your X-rays can weigh those trade-offs for your specific fracture.

While you are in a sling, follow your clinician's guidance on movement. Most protocols allow gentle finger, wrist, and elbow motion so those joints do not stiffen, while holding off on shoulder exercises until the fracture is stable enough. Avoid starting generic exercises from the internet before your provider clears them.

## Why coming out of the sling is not clearance to skate

The easiest mistake after a collarbone fracture is treating everyday comfort as readiness to skate. A sling is often worn mainly for comfort, and weaning out of a sling and lifting activity restrictions are separate clinician decisions—stopping the sling at home does not mean the bone is ready for loading or impact, so keep following your treatment plan even after the sling comes off.

There is no single return-to-skating timeline. Healing depends on fracture location, initial displacement, surgical versus sling treatment, age, bone density, and factors such as smoking, which slows bone union. Weeks of protecting the arm also leave the shoulder muscles weaker and stiffer on the injured side.

Before returning to a board, check your readiness with your doctor or physiotherapist:

- **Individual medical clearance for impact and fall risk,** rather than clearance only for daily tasks or non-contact exercise.
- **Restored, pain-free shoulder range of motion and strength** in reaching, pushing, and supporting load, rebuilt progressively with clinical guidance.

Be realistic about protective gear as well. A helmet, [wrist guards](/blog/wrist-guards-for-adult-skaters/), and knee and elbow pads are designed to reduce impact at those specific sites, and soft padded shirts may reduce skin scrapes or surface bruising, but do not rely on pads or a padded shirt to make a healing collarbone ready for a fall. Waiting for full medical clearance, rebuilding shoulder strength, and keeping speed and terrain within your control are the practical steps that matter when you return.
