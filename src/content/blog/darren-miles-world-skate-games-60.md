Right now, in Asunción, Paraguay, a 60-year-old amateur from Plymouth is competing at the World Skate Games. His name is Darren Miles. He took up skateboarding around 1977. He has never been paid to skate, never had a sponsor, never had his name on a deck. He described his goals for this trip as: "to finish and not get disqualified."

He is representing Great Britain.

That combination — nearly 50 years on a board, no professional career to show for it, competing at the world level at 60 — is more useful to most people reading this site than any pro's story. Andy Macdonald trained in skateboarding from the age of 12, turned pro at 21, and won 23 X Games medals before showing up at an Olympic Games at 51. [Judi Oyama](/blog/judi-oyama-oldest-competitive-skateboarder/) has a Guinness record and has been competing professionally since 1976. Both are worth knowing about. Neither is a version of you.

Darren Miles might be.

## The first fifty years

Miles started in the late 1970s, the same golden-age moment that launched the careers of most of the icons in this sport — Alva, Adams, Caballero. Unlike those names, he didn't go pro. He kept skating through the lean years when skating wasn't cool, through whatever life put in the way, because skating is what he did. Sources place his origins at different spots in England — Essex, Norfolk — but the constant is the same: a kid who picked up a board and never fully put it down.

That part isn't unusual. What's unusual is what he did when he noticed things changing.

## The discipline pivot at 57

Around 2020, Miles realised he was slowly losing his tricks. His body at 57 was no longer doing what his body at 37 had done. A lot of skaters reach this point and stop. Miles didn't stop — he changed disciplines.

He moved to slalom skateboarding. Slalom is a timed racing event on a longboard, weaving a course of cones as tightly and quickly as possible. It's a discipline where technique, timing, and line-reading matter more than the specific athletic movements that decline fastest with age. The tricks aren't going anywhere. But the ability to read a course, carry speed efficiently, and shift your weight precisely from edge to edge — those compound with practice rather than erode with years.

It's the same insight that runs through [Judi Oyama's career](/blog/judi-oyama-oldest-competitive-skateboarder/) and through this site's own argument for why [bowls and transitions tend to suit adult returners better than stairs and rails](/blog/why-bowls-beat-street-for-adult-returners/). You don't have to stop skating when your body changes. You have to be willing to find the part of skating that your changing body can still do well — and then get good at that instead.

Miles was 57 or 58 when he made that call. Within a few years he was competing at domestic slalom events, appearing in UK Slalom Skateboard Association results from 2022 and 2023. Then, this year, he qualified for Great Britain's squad for the World Skate Games.

## The WADA training, and what it represents

To finalise his qualification, Miles was required to complete comprehensive anti-doping awareness training mandated by the World Anti-Doping Agency. The same requirement that applies to every elite athlete on a national squad.

This is worth pausing on. Miles is competing in a legitimate international competition, under the same regulatory framework as athletes who have built careers preparing for exactly this. He didn't slip through as a novelty entrant or a feel-good story. He met the requirements and qualified.

He described his targets simply: not being disqualified, finishing. He's competing in three disciplines across the event window. He's not going there to make a point about age. He's going there to skate, because that's what he does.

## What he's not doing

Miles isn't part of a recovery arc — he didn't battle back from injury or illness to get here. He didn't get a second wind after decades away. He just never stopped. The story is accumulation, not redemption.

That makes it harder to package but more honest about what actually works. There's no transformation moment to point to, no watershed year where he decided to go for it. There's just five decades of showing up, and eventually "showing up" meant being on the start line at a world championship.

## What transfers to your comeback

You are almost certainly not going to the World Skate Games. That's not the point.

**The pivot matters more than the discipline.** Miles didn't keep trying to land tricks his body had stopped cooperating with. He found something his body could learn and grow in instead. If you're 45 and noticing that your board feels different than it did at 25, that gap is a question, not a verdict: what kind of skating can you get better at right now? Carving, cruising, slalom, longboard dancing, pump tracks — the discipline is a starting point, not a permanent assignment. [Changing course isn't quitting](/blog/carving-first-skill-adult-returners-skip/); it's how people last.

**The slow years count.** Miles' decades of casual skating, the years where nothing notable happened competitively, are the reason he was ready to compete internationally at 60. The sessions you do this autumn that feel like nothing, the Tuesday afternoon at the park where nothing clicked, the half-hour of carving before work — those are the slow years. They count. Consistency compounds further than most people believe when they're in the middle of it.

**Not going pro isn't a failure mode.** Miles has skated for nearly 50 years and never earned a paycheque from it. The sport just kept being worth doing. If you're coming back at 45 or 55 with no expectation of sponsorship, no audience, no narrative reason beyond "I want to skate," you are in the same relationship with the sport that Miles was in for most of those fifty years. That's what turns out to produce a world games competitor at 60. It's also, more importantly, what produces someone who's still skating at 60.

Miles' goal for Paraguay — "to finish and not get disqualified" — is the goal of someone who knows exactly what he's there for. He's there to skate. Everything else is detail.
