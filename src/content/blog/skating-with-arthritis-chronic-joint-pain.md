A meaningful number of the adults I see in clinic aren't dealing with vague "aging joints" — they have an actual diagnosis. Osteoarthritis in a knee or hip. Old tendinitis that flares under load. A previous fracture that still talks to them when the weather changes. Generic advice about warming up and listening to your body is true but not specific enough to be useful when you're standing in your kitchen at 7am trying to decide whether today is a skate day.

What I give patients instead is a simple three-zone framework — green, amber, red — for reading your own signals before a session and adjusting the plan accordingly. It's not a diagnosis tool and it doesn't replace your doctor or physio. It's a way to make a faster, more consistent decision than "I guess we'll see how it feels," which is where most people default to when they don't have a structure.

## Green: skate as planned

Green is ordinary stiffness that eases within the first few minutes of moving — the kind almost every adult over 40 feels getting out of bed, diagnosed condition or not. No swelling. No pain that's sharper or different in character from your usual baseline. Your joint feels like itself, just a little rusty until you warm it up.

On a green day, skate your normal session. Do your usual warm-up, and don't hold back out of caution you don't actually need. Constantly under-training a joint that's ready for load doesn't protect it — it just means you lose conditioning you'll need on the days that are genuinely harder.

## Amber: modify, don't cancel

Amber is pain that's present but changes — it eases somewhat with movement rather than staying flat or worsening, there's a small amount of puffiness consistent with a flare you've had before, or you're a day or two out from a previous flare and not fully back to baseline. This is the zone where most adults either push through blindly or cancel the whole session, and modifying is usually the better call — but only when the signal matches a pattern you and your doctor or physio already recognize. New swelling you haven't seen before, or swelling alongside inflammatory arthritis, isn't automatically amber — treat it as red until a clinician has weighed in, since it can mean something more than an ordinary flare.

On an amber day:

- **Shorten the session.** Half your normal time, not your normal intensity for a normal length.
- **Trade impact for flow.** Favor pumping, carving, and flatground over anything involving drops, stairs, or repeated landings. The joint still gets to move; it just isn't taking repeated jolt loading.
- **Extend the warm-up.** Give the joint noticeably longer to ease into movement before you ask anything demanding of it — this is not the day to jump straight into your usual routine.
- **Watch how it responds mid-session, not just before.** If an amber joint gets worse once you're rolling rather than easing further, that's your cue to wrap up early, not push through on stubbornness.

## Red: rest and get it looked at

Red is sharp pain, meaningful swelling, a joint that feels unstable or gives way, or pain that doesn't ease at all once you're moving — it just gets worse or stays where it started. Red is also any pain that's clearly different in character from your known condition, rather than a familiar flare.

On a red day, don't skate. This isn't the framework asking you to be cautious for caution's sake — a joint sending genuinely different or worsening signals is telling you something specific changed, and skating on it risks turning a manageable flare into a longer setback, or masking a new issue that needs actual medical attention. Treat a joint that's unstable or gives way, swelling you haven't seen before, or pain that's new or different in character as signals to contact your doctor or physio promptly — don't wait days to see if they settle on their own. A familiar flare that's simply slow to ease is different: if that specific, known pattern is still lingering after a few days of rest, loop in your doctor or physio then rather than continuing to sit it out indefinitely.

## Setup adjustments worth making regardless of zone

A few equipment choices reduce joint stress across all three zones, not just the harder days. Softer wheels absorb more of the small, constant vibration that comes from rolling on real-world pavement, which adds up over a long session. If your condition affects a hip or knee rather than an ankle, trucks set up a touch looser can make carving and turning require less forceful hip input — but tighter trucks are more stable, so if your ankle is the joint involved, or you're generally unsteady, stick with a standard setup rather than trading stability for less effort. Footwear matters too, but the fix here is cushioned, flat-soled skate shoes (or supportive insoles inside them), not general athletic shoes — a running or walking shoe's shaped, flexible sole works against the board feel you need to stay balanced, which matters more, not less, when a joint is already compromised.

None of these are a substitute for the zone check itself. They just lower the baseline demand on the joint, which gives you a little more room across all three zones before you hit the point where you need to modify or stop.

## The point of having a framework at all

The value here isn't the specific colors — it's replacing a vague, mood-based decision with a quick, consistent check you can run in under a minute before you ever get on the board. Adults managing a real diagnosed condition don't need to be talked out of skating. They need a reliable way to keep doing it for years, which means knowing which days call for your normal session, which call for a modified one, and which call for sitting this one out and trying again once the joint has actually settled.
