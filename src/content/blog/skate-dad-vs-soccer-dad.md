For most of the last few decades, being a sports parent meant a folding chair. You show up, you sit on the sideline, you watch, you drive home. It's a fine arrangement, and plenty of parents are genuinely happy in the chair. But a different pattern has been showing up at skateparks for a while now, and it's worth naming: parents who don't watch from the sideline because there isn't one. They're on a board too.

Call it the skate dad phenomenon, though it's just as often a skate mom, aunt, or older sibling. The shape of it is the same either way — an adult who could easily just supervise chooses instead to participate, badly at first, alongside their kid.

## The chair is optional at a skatepark

Most youth sports have a hard structural line between player and spectator. There's a field, there's a sideline, and the rules of the game make the boundary literal — you can't wander onto a soccer pitch mid-match because you felt like kicking the ball around.

A skatepark has no such line. It's an open bowl of concrete that doesn't know or care whether you're 9 or 49. Nothing about the physical space tells a parent to stay off it. The only thing that's ever kept parents in the chair is the assumption that skating is a kid's activity and adults are there to supervise, not participate. That assumption is the thing quietly breaking down.

## Why more parents are actually getting on

A few things are converging here. Adults returning to activities they gave up in their twenties — running, climbing, cycling — has been a broader trend for a while, and skateboarding is catching some of that same energy. Parents who skated as teenagers and stopped when life got busier are rediscovering that the board is still in the garage and their kid is asking to go to the park anyway. The overlap is obvious once you notice it: your kid needs a ride to the skatepark, and you have a skateboard-shaped memory sitting unused.

There's also a simpler force at work. Watching your child struggle with something difficult, fall, and get back up is a specific kind of parenting experience — encouraging from the sideline, unable to feel any of it yourself. Being on your own board changes that completely. You're not just watching your kid wrestle with fear and frustration and slow progress. You're doing it too, in real time, next to them.

## What changes when you're both on wheels

The clearest shift is conversational. "How was skating today" is a different question to answer when you were both there and both trying something hard. You don't need your kid to narrate their session for you — you watched the fall, or you took one yourself at the same time. That shared reference point turns a debrief into a conversation you're both actually part of.

It also resets the power dynamic in a way most parents don't expect. Your kid is very likely better than you, faster than you, and less afraid than you. For a parent used to being the more competent one in almost every shared activity, that reversal is uncomfortable at first and then, for most people, genuinely valuable. Your kid gets to be the one who's good at something, teaching you instead of the other way around. That's a rare position for a child to occupy with a parent, and it tends to build a specific kind of confidence that doesn't show up when the roles stay fixed.

## It's not about becoming a good skater

Nobody needs to land a kickflip to make this work. The parents doing this well are not trying to keep pace with their teenager's progression. They're pushing, carving, falling on purpose in the flat area, and building just enough comfort on the board to be a genuine participant rather than a supervisor with a coffee. That's a low bar, and it's the right one. The goal isn't shared skill level. It's shared experience.

This also means it doesn't require starting young or being naturally athletic. Plenty of the parents doing this are in their 40s and 50s, picking up a board for the first time or the first time in decades, wobbling around a flat section while their kid drops into the bowl on the other side of the park. Nobody at a skatepark expects a parent to be advanced. What they notice, and generally respect, is a parent who's actually trying instead of standing at the fence.

## The trade-off worth knowing about

There's a real risk here, and it's worth naming honestly: turning your own skating into a proxy for your kid's progress. The parents who get this wrong start coaching from the flat ground, commenting on every attempt, treating the session as an extension of their kid's development plan rather than genuinely shared time. That version of "skate dad" is the one that burns kids out on a sport that's supposed to be fun.

The version that works is simpler and requires more restraint: get on your own board, work on your own thing, and let your kid do the same. Be available, not directive. The connection comes from being in it together, not from managing their experience of it.

## Bring your own board next time

If your kid already skates and you've been the driver, the chair, the supervision, there's a genuinely open invitation sitting in that dynamic. You don't need lessons first, though a session or two with an instructor can take the edge off the awkward beginner phase. You don't need to be good. You need a board, fifteen minutes of just pushing around near your kid, and a willingness to be the newest person at the park.

The folding chair will still be there if it doesn't take. But a lot of parents who've tried the alternative aren't going back to it.
