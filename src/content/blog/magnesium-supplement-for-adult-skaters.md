Magnesium keeps coming up in questions from readers and patients alike, and it's a genuine gap: it's one of the more plausible recovery supplements on the market, and also one of the more overhyped, which makes it worth a straight answer rather than either dismissal or enthusiasm.

## What magnesium actually does

Magnesium is involved in several processes that matter directly to a skater: muscle contraction and relaxation, nerve signaling, and the cellular energy production that powers every push and pop. It's not a niche nutrient — it's a basic mineral your body needs for ordinary function, and a meaningful share of adults don't get quite enough of it from food alone, particularly if your diet is light on nuts, seeds, leafy greens, and whole grains.

That baseline matters more than the supplement conversation usually lets on. If you're actually deficient, correcting that can genuinely improve how you feel. If you're already getting enough through food, adding more magnesium on top isn't likely to do much beyond what your body already has.

## The three things skaters actually want it for

**Muscle cramps.** This is the most common reason people reach for magnesium, and it's also where the evidence is weakest. A 2020 Cochrane review of trials on ordinary nighttime leg cramps found magnesium, in any form, unlikely to meaningfully reduce them in older adults. If cramps are your main reason for considering a magnesium supplement, it's reasonable to try — the downside risk is low — but go in without expecting a reliable fix, and look at hydration and overall mineral balance (sodium and potassium too, not just magnesium) alongside it.

**Sleep.** This is the plausible half of the pitch. Magnesium plays a role in the nervous system processes involved in settling down for sleep, and glycine — the amino acid magnesium is bound to in the glycinate form — has its own separate evidence for improving sleep quality and reducing how long it takes to fall asleep. Readers who've tried it for sleep more than muscle soreness tend to report the more consistent results, though "tends to help some people sleep a bit better" is a realistic bar to set, not a guaranteed fix for a bad night.

**Muscle recovery generally.** This is the vaguest of the three claims and the hardest to pin to solid evidence specific to magnesium alone. What's true is that magnesium is genuinely required for normal muscle function, so correcting an actual deficiency can plausibly help recovery feel less dragged-out. What's not well supported is the idea that magnesium on top of an already-adequate intake accelerates recovery beyond what sleep, hydration, and [the basics covered in the main recovery guide](/blog/recovery-guide-for-adult-skaters/) already do. [Sleep in particular does more heavy lifting here](/blog/sleep-most-underrated-skate-recovery-tool/) than any supplement is likely to.

## The forms, and which one to actually buy

**Magnesium glycinate** is bound to glycine, tends to be gentler on digestion than other forms, and is the one most often recommended for steady daily use or an evening routine aimed at sleep. It's a reasonable default if you're trying magnesium for the first time and don't want to risk stomach upset.

**Magnesium citrate** is cheaper and widely available, and absorbs reasonably well, but the unabsorbed portion draws water into the bowel — which is exactly why it also doubles as an over-the-counter laxative at higher doses. Fine at a modest dose; easy to overshoot into digestive discomfort if you're not paying attention to the amount.

**Magnesium oxide**, the form in a lot of the cheapest multivitamins, absorbs poorly compared to the other two. It's not dangerous, it's just a less efficient way to actually raise your magnesium levels.

**Topical magnesium** — oils, sprays, bath flakes — is popular but the evidence that magnesium meaningfully absorbs through skin in amounts that matter is thin. If a magnesium bath or spray relaxes you, that's a real effect worth keeping, but don't count on it as your main source.

## Realistic dosing, and when to be cautious

Adults generally need somewhere around 310 to 420 milligrams a day total, from food and supplements combined, depending on age and sex — and most multivitamins and standalone magnesium supplements are formulated with that range in mind. More isn't better past a certain point; higher doses mostly just increase the odds of digestive discomfort rather than adding extra benefit.

Talk to your doctor before adding a magnesium supplement if you have kidney disease — impaired kidneys can't clear excess magnesium the way healthy ones do — or if you're on medications that interact with it, including some antibiotics and diuretics. For most otherwise healthy adults, magnesium is one of the lower-risk supplements to experiment with, which is part of why it's worth trying if sleep or cramps are a real issue for you.

## The honest verdict

Magnesium isn't a recovery shortcut, and it won't undo a training load your body isn't ready for. It's a basic mineral that some adults are mildly short on, with plausible-to-solid evidence for sleep and weak evidence for cramps specifically. If your diet is already magnesium-rich, you're probably not missing much by skipping it. If it isn't, or if sleep has been genuinely rough, a glycinate supplement at a sensible dose is a reasonable, low-risk thing to try — just don't expect it to replace the unglamorous basics that do most of the actual recovery work.
