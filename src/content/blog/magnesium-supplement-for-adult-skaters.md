Magnesium keeps coming up in questions from readers and patients alike, and it's a genuine gap: it's one of the more plausible recovery supplements on the market, and also one of the more overhyped, which makes it worth a straight answer rather than either dismissal or enthusiasm.

## What magnesium actually does

Magnesium is involved in several processes that matter directly to a skater: muscle contraction and relaxation, nerve signaling, and the cellular energy production that powers every push and pop. It's not a niche nutrient — it's a basic mineral your body needs for ordinary function, and a meaningful share of adults don't get quite enough of it from food alone, particularly if your diet is light on nuts, seeds, leafy greens, and whole grains.

That baseline matters more than the supplement conversation usually lets on. If you're actually deficient, correcting that can genuinely improve how you feel. If you're already getting enough through food, adding more magnesium on top isn't likely to do much beyond what your body already has.

## The three things skaters actually want it for

**Muscle cramps.** This is the most common reason people reach for magnesium, and it's worth separating two different things people call "cramps." For ordinary nighttime leg cramps — the kind unrelated to exercise — a Cochrane review found clinically meaningful benefit unlikely in older adults, whatever form of magnesium you use. For cramps that actually happen during or right after skating, there's no good randomized evidence either way; it's simply not been studied properly. And the downside isn't nothing: that same review found gastrointestinal side effects (loose stools, cramping of a different kind) in a meaningful share of people taking magnesium, not none. The honest version is: don't reach for magnesium as a general cramp treatment. What actually causes exercise-associated cramps is itself unsettled — a 2026 systematic review found no consistent link to hydration, electrolyte levels, or training factors, and some cramping athletes have tested with higher rather than lower electrolyte levels afterward, undercutting the tidy "you're just dehydrated" story. If cramps are a recurring problem, that's worth raising with a doctor rather than guessing your way through hydration, electrolytes, and supplements on your own.

**Sleep.** This is the more plausible-sounding half of the pitch, but "plausible" is as far as the evidence currently goes. Magnesium plays a role in the nervous system processes involved in settling down for sleep, and glycine — the amino acid magnesium is bound to in the glycinate form — has its own separate research for improving sleep quality. But a 2026 systematic review of randomized trials testing oral magnesium for insomnia found inconsistent results and low-to-very-low certainty evidence overall, and stopped short of recommending it as a routine treatment. "Might help some people a bit, evidence is genuinely mixed" is the honest summary — not a reliable fix for a bad night.

**Muscle recovery generally.** This is the vaguest of the three claims and the hardest to pin to solid evidence specific to magnesium alone. What's true is that magnesium is genuinely required for normal muscle function, so correcting an actual deficiency can plausibly help recovery feel less dragged-out. What's not well supported is the idea that magnesium on top of an already-adequate intake accelerates recovery beyond what sleep, hydration, and [the basics covered in the main recovery guide](/blog/recovery-guide-for-adult-skaters/) already do. [Sleep in particular does more heavy lifting here](/blog/sleep-most-underrated-skate-recovery-tool/) than any supplement is likely to.

## The forms, and which one to actually buy

**Magnesium glycinate** is bound to glycine, and tends to be gentler on digestion than other forms. It's a reasonable default if you're trying magnesium for the first time and don't want to risk stomach upset — not because it's a stronger choice for sleep specifically, but because it's the form least likely to cause GI side effects at a steady daily dose.

**Magnesium citrate** is cheaper and widely available, and absorbs reasonably well, but the unabsorbed portion draws water into the bowel — which is exactly why it also doubles as an over-the-counter laxative at higher doses. Fine at a modest dose; easy to overshoot into digestive discomfort if you're not paying attention to the amount.

**Magnesium oxide**, the form in a lot of the cheapest multivitamins, absorbs poorly compared to the other two. It's not dangerous, it's just a less efficient way to actually raise your magnesium levels.

**Topical magnesium** — oils, sprays, bath flakes — is popular but the evidence that magnesium meaningfully absorbs through skin in amounts that matter is thin. If a magnesium bath or spray relaxes you, that's a real effect worth keeping, but don't count on it as your main source.

## Realistic dosing, and when to be cautious

Adults generally need somewhere around 310 to 420 milligrams a day total, from food and supplements combined, depending on age and sex. That total-RDA figure is not the same thing as a safe supplement dose, though, and it's worth not conflating the two: the NIH sets a separate upper limit of 350 milligrams a day of elemental magnesium specifically from supplements and magnesium-containing medications, because food magnesium doesn't carry the same risk of digestive upset and excess that supplemental magnesium does. Check the elemental magnesium amount on the label — not just the total pill weight — and stay under that 350 milligram supplemental ceiling regardless of how much you're also getting from food.

Talk to your doctor before adding a magnesium supplement if you have kidney disease — impaired kidneys can't clear excess magnesium the way healthy ones do — or if you're on medications that interact with it, including some antibiotics and diuretics. For most otherwise healthy adults, magnesium is one of the lower-risk supplements to experiment with — but that's a reason to discuss a suspected deficiency with your doctor, not a reason to self-prescribe it for sleep or cramps on the strength of "low risk" alone.

## The honest verdict

Magnesium isn't a recovery shortcut, and it won't undo a training load your body isn't ready for. It's a basic mineral that some adults are mildly short on, with genuinely weak evidence for cramps and uncertain, mixed evidence for sleep — a 2026 systematic review of a dozen randomized trials found inconsistent results with low-to-very-low certainty, and stopped short of recommending oral magnesium as a routine insomnia treatment. "Some people report it helps a bit" is a fair, honest description. "Solid evidence it works" is not — and the current evidence doesn't support reaching for it as a routine insomnia fix. If your diet is already magnesium-rich, you're probably not missing much by skipping it. If it isn't — or you suspect an actual deficiency — a glycinate supplement at a sensible, within-limit dose is a low-risk thing to discuss with your doctor; sleep alone, without a suspected deficiency, isn't a strong enough reason on its own. Either way, don't expect it to replace the unglamorous basics that do most of the actual recovery work.
